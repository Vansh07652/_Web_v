# Electrolytes

> **Book:** [Chemistry 2e](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 11: Solutions and Colloids](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 2  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

An **electrolyte** is a substance whose aqueous solution conducts electricity. Pure water conducts almost nothing; when certain solutes dissolve, they release **ions** — charged particles that can drift through the solution and carry current. The more ions a solution contains, the better it conducts.

Electrolytes come in three classes, defined by how completely they produce ions:

- **Strong electrolytes** ionize essentially completely (≈100%): soluble ionic compounds (e.g., \(\ce{NaCl}\)), strong acids (e.g., \(\ce{HCl}\), \(\ce{HNO3}\)), and strong bases (e.g., \(\ce{NaOH}\), \(\ce{KOH}\)).
- **Weak electrolytes** ionize only partially, establishing an equilibrium: weak acids (e.g., \(\ce{CH3COOH}\)), weak bases (e.g., \(\ce{NH3}\)), and a few salts (e.g., \(\ce{HgCl2}\), \(\ce{Pb(CH3COO)2}\)).
- **Nonelectrolytes** produce no ions at all: molecular solutes like sugar, ethanol, and urea. Their solutions do not conduct.

The distinction is about *degree of ionization*, not concentration — a dilute strong electrolyte still ionizes completely, while a concentrated weak electrolyte still ionizes only a little. This topic's equations also set up the **van't Hoff factor** (\(i\)), which quantifies how many particles each formula unit contributes — the link to colligative properties (topic 4).

## Why This Matters

- **The body runs on electrolytes:** Nerve impulses and muscle contraction depend on \(\ce{Na+}\), \(\ce{K+}\), \(\ce{Ca^{2+}}\), and \(\ce{Mg^{2+}}\) gradients across cell membranes. Oral rehydration solutions and IV fluids are, at heart, carefully balanced electrolyte solutions; severe imbalance (from diarrhea, vomiting, or dehydration) can be dangerous.
- **Electrical safety and technology:** Conductivity of solutions matters in batteries, electroplating, and water-quality testing (e.g., measuring total dissolved solids in drinking water).
- **Medicine and pharmacy:** Whether a drug is a salt (electrolyte) or a neutral molecule (nonelectrolyte) determines its solubility, absorption, and behavior in the body.
- **Exam foundation:** Classifying substances as strong/weak/nonelectrolytes and writing their dissociation equations is a classic general-chemistry skill that feeds directly into acids/bases, solubility, and colligative properties.

## Core Concepts

### Strong electrolytes: complete ionization

Three families:

- **Soluble ionic compounds:** \(\ce{NaCl(s) -> Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}\). The ions pre-exist in the crystal; water simply separates them.
- **Strong acids:** \(\ce{HCl(aq) + H2O(l) -> H3O+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}\) — essentially no \(\ce{HCl}\) molecules remain. The common strong acids: \(\ce{HCl}\), \(\ce{HBr}\), \(\ce{HI}\), \(\ce{HNO3}\), \(\ce{H2SO4}\) (first proton), \(\ce{HClO4}\).
- **Strong bases:** \(\ce{NaOH(s) -> Na+(aq) + OH-(aq)}\). Group 1 metal hydroxides and the heavier group 2 hydroxides (e.g., \(\ce{Ba(OH)2}\)).

Strong electrolyte solutions conduct well because nearly every formula unit contributes ions. Use \(\ce{->}\) (single arrow) in their equations.

### Weak electrolytes: partial ionization with equilibrium

Weak acids and bases ionize only a few percent, so the solution contains mostly intact molecules plus a small ion population — conductivity is poor but measurable.

\[
\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) <=> H3O+(aq) + CH3COO-(aq)}
\]

\[
\ce{NH3(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq)}
\]

Use \(\ce{<=>}\) (equilibrium arrows) and describe with \(K_a\) or \(K_b\). Note that "weak" refers to the *extent* of ionization, not the concentration: 0.1 M acetic acid is a weak electrolyte even though the solution may feel strong.

### Nonelectrolytes: no ions

Molecular compounds that do not ionize in water — sugar (\(\ce{C12H22O11}\)), ethanol (\(\ce{C2H5OH}\)), urea — dissolve as neutral molecules. No charged species, no conductivity. Water itself, though it autoionizes slightly, conducts so little that pure water is effectively a nonelectrolyte.

### Conductivity as a diagnostic

A simple circuit test — two electrodes, a bulb (or ammeter), and the solution — sorts the classes:

- Bright bulb / large current → strong electrolyte (many ions).
- Dim bulb / small current → weak electrolyte (few ions).
- No light / no current → nonelectrolyte.

The same measurement, done quantitatively, gives the **van't Hoff factor**: for a strong electrolyte like \(\ce{NaCl}\), \(i = 2\) (two ions per formula unit); for \(\ce{CaCl2}\), \(i = 3\). Measured values fall slightly below ideal because ions pair up at higher concentrations. This factor multiplies concentrations in all colligative-property calculations (topic 4).

### General lab safety principles

Conductivity and electrolyte experiments often involve acids, bases, and salts. General principles: wear eye protection, handle concentrated reagents carefully, never taste or directly touch samples, and dispose of solutions as directed by your instructor. None of the classification chemistry requires unsafe procedures.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

> Think of ions as tiny charged runners in water. Salt water has lots of runners (ions), so electricity can race through it — that's a strong electrolyte. Vinegar has only a few runners, so electricity crawls through slowly — a weak electrolyte. Sugar water has no runners at all — the electricity just stops, like a road with no one to pass the ball. Your body needs these runners to send messages between your brain and your muscles.

## High-Yield Points

- Electrolyte = solute whose solution conducts electricity by releasing ions; classification = degree of ionization, not concentration.
- Strong electrolytes (≈100% ionized): soluble ionic compounds, strong acids (\(\ce{HCl}\), \(\ce{HNO3}\), \(\ce{H2SO4}\) first proton, \(\ce{HClO4}\), \(\ce{HBr}\), \(\ce{HI}\)), strong bases (group 1 hydroxides, \(\ce{Ba(OH)2}\)) — single arrow.
- Weak electrolytes (partial): weak acids (\(\ce{CH3COOH}\), \(\ce{HF}\), \(\ce{HCN}\)), weak bases (\(\ce{NH3}\)) — equilibrium arrows, \(K_a\)/\(K_b\).
- Nonelectrolytes: molecular solutes with no ions (sugar, ethanol, urea) — no conductivity.
- Conductivity test: bright = strong, dim = weak, none = nonelectrolyte.
- Van't Hoff factor \(i\): ions per formula unit (\(\ce{NaCl}\) → 2, \(\ce{CaCl2}\) → 3); needed for colligative properties.
- Body function depends on electrolyte balance: \(\ce{Na+}\), \(\ce{K+}\), \(\ce{Ca^{2+}}\), \(\ce{Mg^{2+}}\) gradients drive nerves and muscles; rehydration solutions restore them.
- General safety: eye protection and careful handling for all acid/base/salt work.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| electrolyte | Substance whose solution conducts electricity via ions | The basis of nerve signaling, batteries, and solution chemistry |
| strong electrolyte | Ionizes ~100% in water | High conductivity; single-arrow dissociation equations |
| weak electrolyte | Ionizes only partially (equilibrium) | Low conductivity; described by \(K_a\)/\(K_b\) |
| nonelectrolyte | Dissolves as neutral molecules; no ions | No conductivity; e.g., sugar, ethanol |
| ionization | Formation of ions from a dissolved substance | The process that creates charge carriers in solution |
| van't Hoff factor (\(i\)) | Number of particles a formula unit produces | Scales concentrations in colligative-property calculations |
| conductivity | Ability of a solution to carry electric current | The measurable property that classifies electrolytes |

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process

### Worked example 1: classifying and writing equations

**Problem.** Classify each substance as a strong electrolyte, weak electrolyte, or nonelectrolyte, and write the equation describing its behavior in water: (a) \(\ce{KNO3}\), (b) \(\ce{HF}\), (c) \(\ce{C12H22O11}\) (sugar), (d) \(\ce{Ba(OH)2}\).

**Solution.**

1. (a) \(\ce{KNO3}\) — soluble ionic compound → **strong electrolyte**:
   \[
   \ce{KNO3(s) -> K+(aq) + NO3-(aq)}
   \]
2. (b) \(\ce{HF}\) — a weak acid → **weak electrolyte**:
   \[
   \ce{HF(aq) + H2O(l) <=> H3O+(aq) + F-(aq)}
   \]
3. (c) Sugar — covalent, non-ionizing → **nonelectrolyte**: dissolves as intact \(\ce{C12H22O11(aq)}\) molecules.
4. (d) \(\ce{Ba(OH)2}\) — group 2 hydroxide → **strong electrolyte**:
   \[
   \ce{Ba(OH)2(s) -> Ba^{2+}(aq) + 2OH-(aq)}
   \]
   Note the stoichiometry: each formula unit yields **three** ions → \(i = 3\).

### Worked example 2: predicting relative conductivity

**Problem.** Rank these 0.1 M solutions by expected conductivity: \(\ce{NaCl}\), \(\ce{CH3COOH}\), \(\ce{C6H12O6}\) (glucose), \(\ce{CaCl2}\).

**Solution.**

1. Count ions per formula unit at full ionization: \(\ce{NaCl}\) → 2; \(\ce{CaCl2}\) → 3; \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) → far fewer than 2 (only a few percent ionize); glucose → 0.
2. Rank by ion concentration: **\(\ce{CaCl2}\) > \(\ce{NaCl}\) >> \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) > glucose**. \(\ce{CaCl2}\) gives three ions per formula unit; \(\ce{NaCl}\) two; acetic acid only a tiny fraction of ions; glucose none.
3. Lesson: conductivity tracks *number of ions in solution*, which depends on both the formula (i) and the degree of ionization.

### Worked example 3: using the van't Hoff factor

**Problem.** A solution is 0.050 M in \(\ce{CaCl2}\). Assuming complete ionization, what is the total concentration of dissolved particles? What would the measured \(i\) tell you if it came out as 2.7?

**Solution.**

1. Write the dissociation: \(\ce{CaCl2 -> Ca^{2+} + 2Cl-}\) → \(i = 3\).
2. Total particle concentration = \(i \times M\):
   \[
   [\text{particles}] = 3 \times 0.050\ \text{M} = 0.15\ \text{M}
   \]
   Dimensional check: (unitless)(mol/L) = mol/L ✓
3. A measured \(i\) of 2.7 (below 3) means some ion pairing or incomplete separation of ions at this concentration — real solutions deviate slightly from the ideal; this matters when computing colligative properties precisely (topic 4).

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| "Strong" electrolyte | "Concentrated" solution | Strong = complete ionization; a 0.001 M HCl is still a strong electrolyte, and a 6 M acetic acid is still weak |
| Weak electrolyte | Nonelectrolyte | Weak electrolytes produce *some* ions (dim bulb); nonelectrolytes produce none (no bulb) |
| Soluble | Electrolyte | Solubility is about dissolving; conductivity is about ionizing. Sugar is very soluble but a nonelectrolyte |
| Molecular compound | Nonelectrolyte | Not always: strong acids like HCl are molecular yet strong electrolytes; classification depends on ionization, not bond type alone |
| Equilibrium arrow | Single arrow | Weak electrolytes need \(\ce{<=>}\) (equilibrium); strong electrolytes use \(\ce{->}\) (complete) |
| One ion per formula unit | Ions per formula unit | Count ALL ions: \(\ce{CaCl2}\) gives 3 particles, \(\ce{Na2SO4}\) gives 3, \(\ce{NaCl}\) gives 2 — the factor \(i\) matters for colligative properties |
