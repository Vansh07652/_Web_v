# Hybrid Atomic Orbitals

> **Book:** [Chemistry 2e](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 8: Advanced Theories of Covalent Bonding](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 2  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Topic 1 left a loose end: valence bond theory explains bonds as orbital overlaps, but carbon in its ground state (\(2s^2 2p^2\)) should form only two bonds at 90°, yet methane has four identical bonds at 109.5°. **Hybridization** resolves this by *mathematically mixing* the atomic orbitals on an atom to create new, equivalent **hybrid orbitals** before bonding occurs.

The key idea: the number of hybrid orbitals equals the number of atomic orbitals mixed. An \(s\) orbital plus three \(p\) orbitals gives four \(sp^3\) hybrids; an \(s\) plus two \(p\)'s gives three \(sp^2\) hybrids; an \(s\) plus one \(p\) gives two \(sp\) hybrids. The hybrid orbitals point in specific directions, and those directions are exactly the electron-pair geometries VSEPR predicts: tetrahedral (109.5°), trigonal planar (120°), and linear (180°). Adding \(d\) orbitals produces \(sp^3d\) (trigonal bipyramidal) and \(sp^3d^2\) (octahedral) hybrids for expanded octets.

Hybridization is not a claim that atoms "really" have these orbitals before bonding. It is a **mathematical model** — a way of re-describing the atom's orbitals so that VB theory's overlap picture matches observed geometry and bond equivalence.

## Why This Matters

- **One theory, all geometries:** Hybridization lets a single framework (orbital overlap) explain linear \(\ce{CO2}\), trigonal planar \(\ce{BF3}\), tetrahedral \(\ce{CH4}\), bent \(\ce{H2O}\), and octahedral \(\ce{SF6}\) — the shapes that determine how molecules interact.
- **Biology and medicine:** The shape of a molecule controls what it can bind to. The planar \(sp^2\) geometry of the peptide bond and the tetrahedral \(sp^3\) carbons in amino acids shape proteins; drug designers think in hybridization terms because \(sp^3\) carbons add 3-D "bulk" while \(sp^2\) regions stay flat.
- **Reactivity:** Hybridization affects bond angles, bond strengths, and acidity (an \(sp\)-hybridized C–H bond is more acidic than an \(sp^3\) one because the \(sp\) hybrid holds electrons closer to carbon, stabilizing the anion).
- **Exams:** Hybridization questions are among the most common in general chemistry — identify the hybridization, count \(\sigma\)/\(\pi\) bonds, and predict geometry.

## Core Concepts

### How mixing works

Hybridization combines one \(s\) orbital with \(n\) orbitals of similar energy (usually \(p\), sometimes \(d\)) to make \(n+1\) **equivalent** hybrid orbitals. The hybrids keep the *total* number of orbitals and electrons — nothing is created or destroyed; the atom's orbitals are just re-packaged so they point in the directions where bonds actually form.

### \(sp^3\): four orbitals, tetrahedral

Mixing one \(s\) + three \(p\) gives four \(sp^3\) hybrids pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron, 109.5° apart. Examples:

- \(\ce{CH4}\) — four \(\sigma\) bonds to H, 109.5°.
- \(\ce{NH3}\) — three \(sp^3\) hybrids bond to H; the fourth holds the **lone pair**. The lone pair pushes the N–H bonds down to about 107°.
- \(\ce{H2O}\) — two \(sp^3\) hybrids bond to H; two hold lone pairs. Lone-pair repulsion squeezes the bond angle to about 104.5°.

A common error is to think that only the *bonding* orbitals count. Lone pairs occupy hybrids too: the hybridization of the central atom is decided by the **total number of electron domains** (bonding pairs + lone pairs), exactly as in VSEPR.

### \(sp^2\): three orbitals, trigonal planar

One \(s\) + two \(p\) → three \(sp^2\) hybrids in a plane at 120°. The remaining unhybridized \(p\) orbital sticks out above and below the plane and is available for a \(\pi\) bond. Example: \(\ce{BF3}\) (three \(\sigma\) bonds, no lone pairs, 120°) and the carbon atoms in ethene, \(\ce{C2H4}\) (three \(\sigma\) domains each: two C–H and one C–C).

### \(sp\): two orbitals, linear

One \(s\) + one \(p\) → two \(sp\) hybrids 180° apart. Two unhybridized \(p\) orbitals remain, both perpendicular to the axis, ready to form two \(\pi\) bonds. Examples: \(\ce{BeCl2}\) and the carbon in \(\ce{CO2}\) or \(\ce{HCCH}\) (ethyne).

### Adding \(d\) orbitals: expanded octets

Atoms in period 3 and beyond can use \(d\) orbitals:

- \(sp^3d\) (one \(s\) + three \(p\) + one \(d\)) → five hybrids, **trigonal bipyramidal** (e.g., \(\ce{PCl5}\)).
- \(sp^3d^2\) (one \(s\) + three \(p\) + two \(d\)) → six hybrids, **octahedral** (e.g., \(\ce{SF6}\)).

The rules are the same: total electron domains = number of hybrids = geometry.

### A summary shortcut table

| Hybridization | Orbitals mixed | Number of hybrids | Electron-domain geometry | Bond angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| \(sp\) | one \(s\) + one \(p\) | 2 | linear | 180° |
| \(sp^2\) | one \(s\) + two \(p\) | 3 | trigonal planar | 120° |
| \(sp^3\) | one \(s\) + three \(p\) | 4 | tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| \(sp^3d\) | one \(s\) + three \(p\) + one \(d\) | 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 90°/120° |
| \(sp^3d^2\) | one \(s\) + three \(p\) + two \(d\) | 6 | octahedral | 90° |

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

> Imagine you have a bag of round and squished balloons. Before making a model, you blow them all up into identical long balloons so they point in different directions — that's mixing orbitals into hybrids. Mix four of them and they naturally point to the four corners of a pyramid (tetrahedral). Mix three and they lie flat like a peace sign (trigonal planar). Mix two and they point straight out (linear). The direction the balloons point is the shape of the molecule.

## High-Yield Points

- Hybridization = mathematical mixing of atomic orbitals on one atom into equivalent hybrid orbitals; count is conserved.
- **Total electron domains (bonds + lone pairs) determine hybridization**, matching VSEPR.
- \(sp\) → 2 domains → linear (180°); \(sp^2\) → 3 domains → trigonal planar (120°); \(sp^3\) → 4 domains → tetrahedral (109.5°); \(sp^3d\) → 5 domains → trigonal bipyramidal; \(sp^3d^2\) → 6 domains → octahedral.
- Lone pairs occupy hybrids: \(\ce{NH3}\) (107°) and \(\ce{H2O}\) (104.5°) are \(sp^3\) with lone-pair compression.
- Unhybridized \(p\) orbitals on \(sp^2\)/\(sp\) atoms are reserved for \(\pi\) bonds (topic 3).
- \(\ce{CH4}\), \(\ce{NH3}\), \(\ce{H2O}\) are all \(sp^3\); \(\ce{BF3}\), \(\ce{C2H4}\) carbons are \(sp^2\); \(\ce{CO2}\), \(\ce{C2H2}\) carbons are \(sp\).
- Hybridization is a model, not a claim about a pre-existing physical state.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| hybrid orbital | New orbital made by mixing atomic orbitals on the same atom | Lets VB theory explain equivalent bonds and correct geometries |
| \(sp^3\) hybridization | One \(s\) + three \(p\) → four equivalent hybrids | Tetrahedral geometry; the standard for carbon in alkanes, \(\ce{NH3}\), \(\ce{H2O}\) |
| \(sp^2\) hybridization | One \(s\) + two \(p\) → three hybrids + one \(p\) left over | Trigonal planar geometry; the leftover \(p\) orbital makes \(\pi\) bonds |
| \(sp\) hybridization | One \(s\) + one \(p\) → two hybrids + two \(p\)'s left over | Linear geometry; two leftover \(p\) orbitals give triple bonds |
| electron domain | Any bonding pair or lone pair around an atom | Counting domains gives the hybridization and the geometry |
| lone pair | Valence electron pair not involved in bonding | Occupies a hybrid orbital and compresses bond angles (e.g., \(\ce{H2O}\)) |

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process

### Worked example 1: assign hybridization and geometry to \(\ce{CH4}\), \(\ce{NH3}\), and \(\ce{H2O}\)

**Problem.** Determine the hybridization of the central atom in each molecule.

**Solution.**

1. Count electron domains around the central atom (bonding pairs + lone pairs):
   - \(\ce{CH4}\): 4 bonds, 0 lone pairs → 4 domains → \(sp^3\), tetrahedral, 109.5°.
   - \(\ce{NH3}\): 3 bonds + 1 lone pair → 4 domains → \(sp^3\); observed angle ~107° (lone pair compresses it).
   - \(\ce{H2O}\): 2 bonds + 2 lone pairs → 4 domains → \(sp^3\); observed angle ~104.5°.
2. Rule: the hybridization depends on domains, not on bonds alone — all three are \(sp^3\).

### Worked example 2: predict hybridization of carbon in \(\ce{CO2}\) and \(\ce{C2H4}\)

**Problem.** Find the hybridization and bond angles at each carbon.

**Solution.**

1. \(\ce{CO2}\): carbon has 2 double bonds → 2 electron domains → \(sp\), linear, 180°. The two unhybridized \(p\) orbitals form the two \(\pi\) bonds of the double bonds (one per C=O).
2. \(\ce{C2H4}\): each carbon has 3 domains (two C–H single bonds + one C=C double bond) → \(sp^2\), trigonal planar, ~120° around each carbon. Each carbon's leftover \(p\) orbital overlaps sideways with the other carbon's → the \(\pi\) half of the double bond. All six atoms lie in one plane.

### Worked example 3: account for the geometry of \(\ce{SF6}\) with an expanded octet

**Problem.** Sulfur in \(\ce{SF6}\) has six bonds. Assign hybridization and geometry.

**Solution.**

1. Count domains: 6 S–F bonds, 0 lone pairs → 6 domains.
2. Six hybrids require one \(s\) + three \(p\) + two \(d\) → \(sp^3d^2\).
3. Geometry: octahedral, all F–S–F angles 90°. The \(d\) orbitals make expanded octets possible — a reminder that hybridization can extend beyond \(s\) and \(p\).

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Number of bonds | Number of electron domains | Lone pairs count as domains; \(\ce{H2O}\) has only 2 bonds but 4 domains → \(sp^3\), not \(sp\) |
| Hybridization "happening" physically | Hybridization as a mathematical model | No experiment separates "before" and "after" mixing; hybrids are a re-description that matches geometry and equivalence |
| \(sp^2\) carbon with a double bond | \(sp^3\) carbon with a single bond | \(sp^2\) has 3 domains and a leftover \(p\) orbital for a \(\pi\) bond; \(sp^3\) has 4 domains and no leftover \(p\) |
| Bond angle exactly 109.5° | Actual measured angle | Lone pairs compress angles (\(\ce{NH3}\) ~107°, \(\ce{H2O}\) ~104.5°); 109.5° is the lone-pair-free ideal |
| Hybridization of the atom | Geometry of the molecule | Hybridization describes the central atom's orbitals; molecular shape also depends on which domains are lone pairs |

## Quick Review

1. Why was hybridization introduced in VB theory?
2. How many hybrid orbitals result from mixing one \(s\) with three \(p\)? What geometry?
3. What is the hybridization of oxygen in \(\ce{H2O}\), and why is the angle less than 109.5°?
4. Which atoms in \(\ce{C2H4}\) are \(sp^2\), and what orbital makes the \(\pi\) bond?
5. What hybridization does \(\ce{PCl5}\) (five bonds, no lone pairs) use?
