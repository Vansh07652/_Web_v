# Multiple Bonds

> **Book:** [Chemistry 2e](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 8: Advanced Theories of Covalent Bonding](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 3  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

A single bond is one \(\sigma\) bond. A **double bond** is one \(\sigma\) **plus one \(\pi\)** bond; a **triple bond** is one \(\sigma\) **plus two \(\pi\)** bonds. The extra \(\pi\) components come from sideways overlap of parallel \(p\) orbitals on atoms whose hybridization leaves \(p\) orbitals unhybridized — which is why only \(sp^2\) and \(sp\) centers (not \(sp^3\)) form multiple bonds.

This one-sigma-plus-pi structure explains three famous facts:

1. **Multiple bonds are shorter and stronger than single bonds** — but not twice or three times as strong, because the \(\pi\) components are weaker than the \(\sigma\) component.
2. **Rotation around a double bond is restricted** — twisting would break the sideways \(p\) overlap, which is what creates cis/trans isomerism.
3. **\(\pi\) electrons can be shared across more than two atoms** — delocalization in molecules like benzene stabilizes them dramatically.

## Why This Matters

- **Restricted rotation and biology:** The double bond in the peptide (amide) link fixes the shape of protein backbones. The cis/trans geometry of fatty-acid double bonds changes whether fats pack tightly (trans fats are straighter and pack like saturated fats) — a real health-relevant structural fact.
- **Strength and reactivity:** The \(\pi\) bond of a double bond is exposed above and below the molecular plane, which is why alkenes react readily (they are the reactive site in polymerization, hydrogenation, and the chemistry of natural rubber and plant oils).
- **Stability from delocalization:** Benzene's delocalized \(\pi\) system explains why it resists addition reactions that ordinary alkenes undergo easily — a cornerstone of all aromatic chemistry.
- **Industrial and atmospheric chemistry:** The strong \(\ce{N2}\) triple bond (941 kJ/mol) makes nitrogen gas so inert that making ammonia requires high pressure, temperature, and a catalyst (the Haber process). Understanding "how hard it is to break a triple bond" is a direct consequence of this topic.

## Core Concepts

### The anatomy of a multiple bond

- **Double bond** = 1 \(\sigma\) + 1 \(\pi\). The \(\sigma\) bond forms from head-on overlap (for an \(sp^2\) carbon, one hybrid orbital toward the partner); the \(\pi\) bond forms from sideways overlap of the leftover \(2p\) orbitals. \(\pi\) electron density sits above and below the internuclear axis, so the electron cloud looks like two overlapping lobes, not a cylinder.
- **Triple bond** = 1 \(\sigma\) + 2 \(\pi\). An \(sp\) atom has *two* leftover \(p\) orbitals, perpendicular to each other and to the bond axis; each forms one \(\pi\) bond. The two \(\pi\) bonds wrap around the \(\sigma\) bond, giving a cylindrical cloud overall.

### Bond order, length, and energy

Bond order = number of shared electron pairs (1, 2, or 3). As bond order rises:

- **Bond length decreases:** \(\ce{C-C}\) ≈ 154 pm, \(\ce{C=C}\) ≈ 134 pm, \(\ce{C#C}\) ≈ 120 pm. More electron density between the nuclei pulls them closer.
- **Bond energy increases:** \(\ce{C-C}\) ≈ 347 kJ/mol, \(\ce{C=C}\) ≈ 611 kJ/mol, \(\ce{C#C}\) ≈ 837 kJ/mol. Notice the steps: adding the second bond adds only ~264 kJ/mol and the third ~226 kJ/mol — each \(\pi\) bond is weaker than the \(\sigma\) bond beneath it.

### Restricted rotation and cis/trans isomerism

A \(\sigma\) bond alone (as in ethane) allows free rotation about the bond axis. A double bond does not: rotating one end would destroy the sideways \(p\)–\(p\) overlap that makes the \(\pi\) bond. Because rotation is locked, two different arrangements exist for 1,2-disubstituted alkenes:

- **cis** — the two identical groups on the same side of the double bond.
- **trans** — the two identical groups on opposite sides.

These are different compounds with different properties (boiling points, packing, biological activity).

### Delocalized \(\pi\) systems

When \(\pi\) orbitals on three or more atoms can overlap continuously (every atom in the chain is \(sp^2\) or \(sp\)), the \(\pi\) electrons are **delocalized** — shared over the whole region rather than locked between one pair of atoms. Benzene (\(\ce{C6H6}\)) is the classic case: six \(p\) orbitals overlap in a ring, and the six \(\pi\) electrons spread over all six carbons. The real structure is a resonance hybrid of the two Kekulé structures, and the delocalization energy makes benzene much more stable than a hypothetical "cyclohexatriene" with three isolated double bonds.

### \(\ce{N2}\) and \(\ce{O2}\): two famous cases

- \(\ce{N2}\): triple bond (1 \(\sigma\) + 2 \(\pi\)), bond energy 941 kJ/mol — one of the strongest bonds in chemistry. This is why nitrogen gas is so unreactive.
- \(\ce{O2}\): double bond (1 \(\sigma\) + 1 \(\pi\)), bond energy 495 kJ/mol, bond length 121 pm. Note the oxygen molecule's two unpaired \(\pi^*\) electrons — a fact VB theory struggles with and molecular orbital theory (next topic) explains cleanly.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

> A single bond is one strong handshake between two atoms, straight on. A double bond is that handshake plus two people holding hands sideways above and below — the sideways grips (pi bonds) are easier to break than the handshake (sigma bond). A triple bond adds one more sideways grip. Because the sideways grips lock the atoms in place, you can't twist a double bond like you can twist a single bond — that's why some molecules have cis and trans versions that are different things.

## High-Yield Points

- Double bond = 1 \(\sigma\) + 1 \(\pi\); triple bond = 1 \(\sigma\) + 2 \(\pi\). The \(\pi\) bonds come from sideways \(p\)–\(p\) overlap.
- Multiple bonds require unhybridized \(p\) orbitals → only \(sp^2\) (double) and \(sp\) (triple) centers form them; \(sp^3\) atoms never do.
- Higher bond order → shorter and stronger, but each added \(\pi\) bond adds less energy than the \(\sigma\) bond: \(\ce{C-C}\) 347 → \(\ce{C=C}\) 611 → \(\ce{C#C}\) 837 kJ/mol; lengths 154 → 134 → 120 pm.
- \(\pi\) bonds lock geometry: no rotation around double bonds → cis/trans isomers are distinct compounds.
- Delocalized \(\pi\) systems (benzene) are more stable than isolated double bonds; resonance describes the sharing.
- \(\ce{N2}\)'s 941 kJ/mol triple bond explains nitrogen's inertness and why ammonia synthesis needs a catalyst.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| double bond | One \(\sigma\) + one \(\pi\) bond between the same two atoms | Shorter/stronger than a single bond; locks rotation → cis/trans isomers |
| triple bond | One \(\sigma\) + two \(\pi\) bonds between the same two atoms | Very short and strong (e.g., \(\ce{N2}\)); explains inertness and high reactivity sites |
| \(\pi\) bond | Bond from sideways overlap of parallel \(p\) orbitals | The "extra" component of multiple bonds; weaker, exposed, and rotation-locking |
| bond order | Number of shared electron pairs between two atoms | Predicts relative bond length and strength (higher order = shorter, stronger) |
| cis/trans isomerism | Same connectivity, different side-of-double-bond arrangement | Different compounds with different properties because \(\pi\) bonds prevent rotation |
| delocalization | \(\pi\) electrons spread over three or more atoms | Stabilizes systems like benzene; central to aromatic chemistry |
| resonance | Multiple Lewis structures whose average describes the real molecule | The standard way to represent delocalized electrons |

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process

### Worked example 1: count \(\sigma\) and \(\pi\) bonds in \(\ce{C2H4}\) (ethene)

**Problem.** How many \(\sigma\) and \(\pi\) bonds are in \(\ce{C2H4}\)? What is the hybridization of each carbon?

**Solution.**

1. Each carbon is \(sp^2\) (3 electron domains: 2 C–H + 1 C–C), so each carbon has one leftover \(2p\) orbital.
2. Count \(\sigma\) bonds: each C–H bond is \(\sigma\) (4 total) + the C–C head-on overlap is \(\sigma\) → **5 \(\sigma\) bonds**.
3. Count \(\pi\) bonds: the two leftover \(2p\) orbitals overlap sideways → **1 \(\pi\) bond** (the second half of the C=C double bond).
4. Total: 5 \(\sigma\) + 1 \(\pi\) = 6 shared pairs — matching the Lewis structure's 6 bonds (4 C–H, 1 C=C).

### Worked example 2: explain why \(\ce{N2}\) is so unreactive

**Problem.** \(\ce{N2}\) has a triple bond with bond energy 941 kJ/mol, while \(\ce{O2}\) has a double bond with 495 kJ/mol. Use bond structure to explain the difference in reactivity.

**Solution.**

1. \(\ce{N2}\): N is \(sp\)-hybridized (2 domains), leaving two \(2p\) orbitals per atom → 1 \(\sigma\) + 2 \(\pi\) bonds.
2. The three shared pairs put a great deal of electron density between the nuclei → very short bond (110 pm) and very strong bond (941 kJ/mol).
3. Breaking \(\ce{N2}\) costs far more energy than typical reactions release, so nitrogen gas is kinetically inert; the Haber process supplies heat, high pressure, and an iron catalyst to weaken the bond stepwise.
4. \(\ce{O2}\)'s double bond (495 kJ/mol) is much easier to break, which is why oxygen participates in combustion and metabolism readily.

### Worked example 3: predict the number of isomers for 1,2-dichloroethene

**Problem.** \(\ce{C2H2Cl2}\) (1,2-dichloroethene) exists as two distinct compounds. Explain why using \(\pi\)-bond structure.

**Solution.**

1. Each carbon is \(sp^2\) with a C=C double bond (1 \(\sigma\) + 1 \(\pi\)).
2. The \(\pi\) bond prevents rotation, so the two Cl atoms are locked either on the same side (**cis**-1,2-dichloroethene, boiling point 60 °C) or on opposite sides (**trans**-1,2-dichloroethene, boiling point 48 °C).
3. These are not conformations of one molecule — they are different compounds with different physical properties, exactly because rotating around a double bond would require breaking the \(\pi\) overlap.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Double bond = two equal bonds | Double bond = one \(\sigma\) + one weaker \(\pi\) | The two components are not equivalent; \(\pi\) bonds are weaker than \(\sigma\) bonds |
| Bond order as a simple multiplier | Actual bond energy | Adding a \(\pi\) bond adds energy but less than the \(\sigma\) bond: C=C is not twice C–C |
| Rotation around single vs double bonds | All bonds rotating freely | \(\sigma\) bonds rotate freely; \(\pi\) bonds lock the geometry (cis/trans) |
| Resonance (delocalized) structure | A molecule flipping between structures | The molecule is one hybrid; resonance structures are drawings, not real states |
| \(\pi\) electron density location | \(\sigma\) electron density location | \(\sigma\) density is along the bond axis; \(\pi\) density is above and below the plane |
| \(sp^2\) or \(sp\) centers forming multiple bonds | \(sp^3\) centers forming multiple bonds | \(sp^3\) has no unhybridized \(p\) orbital, so it cannot form \(\pi\) bonds |
