# Chemical Formulas

> **Book:** [Chemistry 2e](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 2: Atoms, Molecules, and Ions](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 4  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

A chemical formula is the shorthand language of chemistry: it tells you which elements are present, how many atoms of each, and — for molecular compounds — how those atoms are connected. But not all formulas carry the same information. **Molecular formulas** state the actual number of atoms in one molecule (\(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\) for hydrogen peroxide). **Empirical formulas** give the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms (\(\text{HO}\) for hydrogen peroxide). **Structural formulas** show how the atoms are bonded together. Ionic compounds, which exist as extended networks rather than discrete molecules, are described by **formula units** — the smallest electrically neutral ratio of ions, such as \(\text{CaCl}_2\) or \(\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\). Learning to read and write these formulas — and to derive empirical formulas from mass data — is the gateway to stoichiometry, the quantitative heart of chemistry.

## Why This Matters

Formulas are the difference between a recipe and a chemical identity. Water is \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\); hydrogen peroxide is \(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\) — one extra oxygen atom changes a safe drink into a disinfectant, which is why misreading formulas is dangerous. The same shorthand underlies medication labels (NaCl for saline, \(\text{NaHCO}_3\) for baking soda), fertilizer analysis (N-P-K), and industrial safety data sheets. Deriving empirical formulas from percent composition is also the classic "unknown compound" problem: forensic labs, quality-control chemists, and researchers all use the same method to identify what a substance is made of. For the nursing and health-science reader, recognizing that \(\text{CaCO}_3\) (calcium carbonate) and \(\text{CaCl}_2\) (calcium chloride) are different calcium salts — with very different uses — is exactly the kind of formula literacy that prevents medication errors.

## Core Concepts

### The formula family: molecular, empirical, structural

A **molecular formula** uses subscripts to state the exact atom count per molecule: glucose is \(\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6\) — 6 carbons, 12 hydrogens, 6 oxygens. An **empirical formula** is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms: dividing glucose's subscripts by 6 gives \(\text{CH}_2\text{O}\). The empirical formula of glucose is therefore \(\text{CH}_2\text{O}\) — but so is the empirical formula of acetic acid (\(\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{O}_2\)), formaldehyde, and several other substances. Many different molecular formulas can share one empirical formula; the empirical formula alone cannot identify a compound. A **structural formula** adds connectivity — for water it is written H–O–H, showing both hydrogens bonded to the oxygen, not to each other. Condensed structural formulas (\(\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH}\) for ethanol) pack the same connectivity information into one line.

### Molecular vs. empirical: the multiplier \(n\)

For any molecular compound, the molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula:

\[
\text{molecular formula} = n \times \text{empirical formula}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
\]

The multiplier \(n\) is found by comparing the compound's molar mass with its empirical formula mass:

\[
n = \frac{\text{molar mass}}{\text{empirical formula mass}}
\]

When \(n = 1\), the empirical and molecular formulas are the same, as with water (\(\text{H}_2\text{O}\) is already simplest) and methane (\(\text{CH}_4\)).

### Deriving an empirical formula from mass data

Given the mass (or percent) of each element in a sample, the empirical formula comes from four steps:

1. Convert each element's mass to moles using its molar mass.
2. Divide every mole value by the smallest mole value.
3. If the results are not whole numbers, multiply all by the smallest integer that makes them whole.
4. Write the element symbols with those integers as subscripts.

The mole concept and molar masses are treated in detail in Chapter 3; here the method is introduced so the logic of formulas is clear from the start.

### Formula units for ionic compounds

Ionic compounds do not form discrete molecules; they form repeating three-dimensional lattices of cations and anions. The **formula unit** is the smallest electrically neutral ratio of ions. It is dictated by charge balance: the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge. Sodium (Na⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻) give NaCl; calcium (Ca²⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻) give \(\text{CaCl}_2\) because two chlorides are needed to balance one calcium. Aluminum (Al³⁺) and oxide (O²⁻) give \(\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\) — two Al³⁺ (total +6) balance three O²⁻ (total −6). A practical shortcut, the "criss-cross" method, takes the magnitude of each ion's charge as the subscript of the other ion, then reduces the ratio to lowest terms.

### Formulas encode more than atom counts

A formula can also carry structural hints: parentheses group polyatomic ions (\(\text{Ca(NO}_3\text{)}_2\) contains two nitrate groups), and a dot in a hydrate formula shows incorporated water (\(\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}\) is copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate — five waters per formula unit). Coefficients (numbers in front of formulas, like the 2 in \(2\text{H}_2\text{O}\)) count *molecules*, not atoms within one molecule — a distinction that becomes critical when balancing equations.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

A chemical formula is like a LEGO instruction card. It tells you how many of each brick to use: \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\) means two hydrogen bricks and one oxygen brick. Sometimes the card shows the *simplest* recipe — "1 of each" — while the real toy needs more, like how both a tiny and a giant snowman can be built from "1 head + 1 body" repeated different numbers of times. Ionic compounds are different: they're like a wall of bricks where plus-bricks and minus-bricks must balance perfectly, so the formula just tells you how many of each kind you need to make a stable wall. Reading the card correctly — and not mistaking \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\) for \(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\) — is the whole game.

## High-Yield Points

- **Molecular formula**: exact atom count per molecule (\(\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6\)); **empirical formula**: simplest whole-number ratio (\(\text{CH}_2\text{O}\)); **structural formula**: shows connectivity (H–O–H).
- Many molecules share one empirical formula; the empirical formula alone cannot identify a compound.
- \(\text{molecular formula} = n \times \text{empirical formula}\), with \(n = \frac{\text{molar mass}}{\text{empirical formula mass}}\).
- Empirical formula from data: mass → moles → divide by smallest → multiply to whole numbers.
- Ionic compounds are described by **formula units** (smallest neutral ion ratio), governed by charge balance: \(\text{CaCl}_2\), \(\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\), \(\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4\).
- Criss-cross shortcut: charge magnitude of one ion becomes the subscript of the other, then reduce.
- Parentheses enclose polyatomic ions; a dot in a hydrate (e.g., \(\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}\)) means incorporated water.
- Subscripts count atoms *inside* a formula; coefficients count whole formulas (molecules) in front.
- Exam trap: \(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\) (hydrogen peroxide, empirical HO) vs \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\) (water, empirical \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\)) — different compounds, different properties.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Chemical formula | Symbolic description of a substance's composition using element symbols and subscripts | The universal shorthand for communicating chemical identity |
| Molecular formula | Exact numbers of each atom in one molecule | Distinguishes compounds that share an empirical formula |
| Empirical formula | Simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound | Derived from composition data; the starting point for identifying unknowns |
| Structural formula | Shows which atoms are bonded to which | Explains properties and reactions that depend on connectivity |
| Formula unit | Smallest electrically neutral ratio of ions in an ionic compound | The "molecule-equivalent" for salts, used in formula mass and stoichiometry |
| Polyatomic ion | A group of atoms with an overall charge, e.g., \(\text{NO}_3^-\), \(\text{SO}_4^{2-}\) | Behaves as a single unit in formulas and reactions |
| Hydrate | An ionic compound with incorporated water, e.g., \(\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}\) | Water content changes formula mass and properties (e.g., color of copper sulfate) |
| Coefficient | A number in front of a formula counting whole molecules, e.g., the 2 in \(2\text{H}_2\text{O}\) | Essential for balancing equations and mole ratios |
| Subscript | A number within a formula counting atoms of the preceding element | Directly encodes the composition of one formula unit |

## Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Empirical from molecular.** Glucose has the molecular formula \(\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6\). Find its empirical formula, and determine the multiplier \(n\).

Divide every subscript by the greatest common divisor (6):

\[
\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow \text{C}_{6/6}\text{H}_{12/6}\text{O}_{6/6} = \text{CH}_2\text{O}
\]

So the empirical formula is \(\text{CH}_2\text{O}\) and the multiplier is:

\[
n = \frac{6}{1} = 6
\]

Check: acetic acid (\(\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{O}_2\)) has the *same* empirical formula \(\text{CH}_2\text{O}\) with \(n = 2\) — proof that empirical formulas alone cannot identify a compound.

**Example 2 — Empirical formula from percent composition.** A compound is 40.00% carbon, 6.71% hydrogen, and 53.29% oxygen by mass. Determine its empirical formula.

Assume a 100.0 g sample, so percentages become grams: 40.00 g C, 6.71 g H, 53.29 g O. Convert each to moles (using molar masses 12.01, 1.008, and 16.00 g/mol):

\[
n_{\text{C}} = \frac{40.00\ \text{g}}{12.01\ \text{g/mol}} = 3.331\ \text{mol}
\]

\[
n_{\text{H}} = \frac{6.71\ \text{g}}{1.008\ \text{g/mol}} = 6.657\ \text{mol}
\]

\[
n_{\text{O}} = \frac{53.29\ \text{g}}{16.00\ \text{g/mol}} = 3.331\ \text{mol}
\]

Divide each by the smallest value (3.331):

\[
\text{C: } \frac{3.331}{3.331} = 1, \quad \text{H: } \frac{6.657}{3.331} = 1.998 \approx 2, \quad \text{O: } \frac{3.331}{3.331} = 1
\]

The ratio is 1 : 2 : 1, so the empirical formula is \(\text{CH}_2\text{O}\). If the compound's molar mass were later measured as 180 g/mol, the molecular formula would be:

\[
n = \frac{180\ \text{g/mol}}{30.03\ \text{g/mol}} = 6 \quad \rightarrow \quad \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6
\]

**Example 3 — Formula unit from charges.** Write the formula unit for the ionic compound formed from aluminum (Al³⁺) and oxide (O²⁻) ions.

The total charge must be zero. Two Al³⁺ give +6; three O²⁻ give −6:

\[
2(+3) + 3(-2) = 0
\]

So the formula unit is \(\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\). The criss-cross shortcut gives the same result: take the charge magnitude of Al (3) as the subscript of O, and the magnitude of O (2) as the subscript of Al, writing \(\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\) — already in lowest terms.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Empirical formula | Molecular formula | Empirical is the simplest ratio (\(\text{CH}_2\text{O}\)); molecular is the actual atom count (\(\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6\)). Different compounds can share an empirical formula |
| \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\) | \(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\) | Water vs. hydrogen peroxide — one extra O per molecule changes a stable beverage into an oxidizer. Formula literacy is a safety issue |
| Subscript | Coefficient | Subscript (in \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\)) counts atoms within one molecule; coefficient (in \(2\text{H}_2\text{O}\)) counts whole molecules. \(2\text{H}_2\text{O}\) = 4 H atoms total |
| \(\text{Ca(NO}_3\text{)}_2\) | \(\text{CaNO}_3\) | The parentheses mean TWO nitrate groups — 2 N and 6 O atoms. Dropping parentheses loses the polyatomic ion's identity |
| Formula unit | Molecule | Ionic compounds have no discrete molecules; the formula unit is just the smallest neutral ion ratio. Molecular formulas apply only to covalent compounds |
| Changing a formula | Balancing an equation | You never change subscripts to balance an equation (that changes the substance); you change coefficients only |
| Hydrate dot | Multiplication | The dot in \(\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}\) means "with 5 waters," not multiplication — the formula unit includes 5 water molecules |

## Quick Review

1. What is the empirical formula of butane, \(\text{C}_4\text{H}_{10}\)? Of benzene, \(\text{C}_6\text{H}_6\)?
2. A compound is 27.06% sodium, 16.47% nitrogen, and 56.47% oxygen by mass. What is its empirical formula?
3. Why can two different compounds have the same empirical formula, and what information resolves which is which?
4. Write the formula unit for the compound of calcium (Ca²⁺) and phosphate (\(\text{PO}_4^{3-}\)).
5. How many hydrogen atoms are represented by \(3\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3\)? How many nitrate groups?

1. Butane: divide C₄H₁₀ by 2 → \(\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\). Benzene: divide C₆H₆ by 6 → \(\text{CH}\). (Note: benzene's empirical formula is CH — its molecular formula is six times that.)
2. In a 100.0 g sample: Na = 27.06/22.99 = 1.177 mol; N = 16.47/14.01 = 1.176 mol; O = 56.47/16.00 = 3.529 mol. Divide by 1.176: Na 1, N 1, O 3 → \(\text{NaNO}_3\) (sodium nitrate).
3. Because the empirical formula only records the simplest ratio — e.g., \(\text{CH}_2\text{O}\) fits glucose, acetic acid, and formaldehyde. The molecular formula (or the molar mass) resolves the identity: \(n = \text{molar mass}/\text{empirical formula mass}\).
4. Charge balance: 3 Ca²⁺ (+6) with 2 \(\text{PO}_4^{3-}\) (−6) → \(\text{Ca}_3(\text{PO}_4\text{)}_2\) — the familiar mineral form of calcium phosphate.
5. Each \(\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3\) has 4 H atoms, so \(3 \times 4 = 12\) hydrogen atoms total. Each formula unit has one nitrate group (\(\text{NO}_3^-\)), so 3 nitrate groups.
