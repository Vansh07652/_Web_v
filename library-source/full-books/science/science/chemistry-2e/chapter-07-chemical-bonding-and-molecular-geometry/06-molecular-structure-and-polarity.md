# Molecular Structure and Polarity

> **Book:** [Chemistry 2e](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 7: Chemical Bonding and Molecular Geometry](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 6  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

A Lewis structure tells you which atoms are connected and how electrons are shared, but almost nothing about the molecule's **shape**. Two questions remain: *What is the three-dimensional arrangement of the atoms?* and *Is the molecule polar or nonpolar?* This topic answers both.

**Molecular structure** (geometry) is predicted with **VSEPR theory** (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion): electron groups around a central atom repel and settle as far apart as possible. **Polarity** is decided by bond polarity (set by electronegativity differences) and geometry together (whether bond dipoles cancel or add). Symmetry can cancel the dipoles (CO₂, CCl₄), or they can reinforce (H₂O, CHCl₃).

## Why This Matters

- **Physical properties and solubility:** boiling points track intermolecular forces (strongest between polar molecules), and "like dissolves like" predicts which drugs dissolve in water and which pollutants reach groundwater.
- **Biology and drug action:** cell membranes are built from lipids with polar heads and nonpolar tails; a drug's polarity governs how well it crosses them.
- **Exam staple:** "Predict the shape and polarity" is among the most common general-chemistry questions, recurring in organic chemistry and biochemistry.

## Core Concepts

### VSEPR: count electron domains, then spread them out

An **electron domain** (electron group) is any region of high electron density around a central atom: a single bond, a double bond, a triple bond, or a lone pair each count as **one** domain. Domains repel and spread to maximize their separation:

| Domains | Electron-pair geometry | Bond angles |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Linear | 180° |
| 3 | Trigonal planar | 120° |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| 5 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90° and 120° |
| 6 | Octahedral | 90° |

Recipe: count domains (bonds of any order + lone pairs), look up the arrangement, then drop lone pairs to name the **molecular geometry** (the shape defined by atom positions only).

### Lone pairs are invisible but not silent

Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs and compress nearby bond angles: CH₄ has four equivalent bonds at 109.5°, NH₃ (one lone pair) has H–N–H angles near 107°, and H₂O (two lone pairs) has an H–O–H angle near 104.5°. All three share tetrahedral *electron-pair* geometry, but their *molecular* geometries are tetrahedral, trigonal pyramidal, and bent.

### Bond polarity comes from electronegativity differences

When two atoms with different electronegativities share electrons, the density sits closer to the more electronegative atom, creating a **bond dipole** — partial positive (\(\delta+\)) on one end, partial negative (\(\delta-\)) on the other. The quantitative measure is the **dipole moment** \(\mu\), charge times separation distance:

\[
\mu = Q \times d
\]

where \(Q\) is the separated charge and \(d\) the distance between charges. Dipole moments are reported in **debyes (D)**; one debye equals \(3.336\times10^{-30}\ \text{C}\cdot\text{m}\). Identical atoms (Cl₂) give \(\mu = 0\); a very polar bond (H–F) gives a large \(\mu\).

### Molecular polarity is a vector sum

Each polar bond is a vector pointing from \(\delta+\) toward \(\delta-\). The molecular dipole moment is the **vector sum** of all bond dipoles plus any lone-pair contribution. If the vectors cancel by symmetry, the molecule is nonpolar even when its bonds are polar:

- CO₂ is linear: the two C=O dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel (\(\mu = 0\)).
- H₂O is bent: the two O–H dipoles add at an angle, so they do not cancel (\(\mu = 1.85\ \text{D}\)).
- CCl₄ is tetrahedral: four C–Cl dipoles cancel exactly (\(\mu = 0\)).
- CHCl₃ is also tetrahedral, but three C–Cl dipoles and one C–H dipole do not cancel — it is polar (\(\mu \approx 1.04\ \text{D}\)).

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

> Magnets on a balloon push apart and spread evenly — that is VSEPR, and it decides a molecule's shape. Now imagine a tug-of-war: a strong atom (like oxygen) pulls the shared electrons toward itself. If all sides pull evenly, the molecule is nonpolar, like a balanced seesaw. If one side wins, the molecule gains a negative end and a positive end — polar, like a tiny battery.

## High-Yield Points

- VSEPR: each bond (single, double, or triple) and each lone pair counts as **one** electron domain; 2 domains → linear (180°), 3 → trigonal planar (120°), 4 → tetrahedral (109.5°), 5 → trigonal bipyramidal, 6 → octahedral.
- Name the **molecular** geometry from atom positions only; lone pairs compress angles (CH₄ 109.5° → NH₃ ~107° → H₂O ~104.5°).
- Bond polarity requires an electronegativity difference; dipole moment \(\mu = Q \times d\), with 1 D \(= 3.336\times10^{-30}\ \text{C}\cdot\text{m}\).
- Molecular polarity = vector sum of bond dipoles; symmetric shapes (CO₂, CCl₄, BF₃) stay nonpolar despite polar bonds.
- "Like dissolves like": polar solvents dissolve polar solutes; nonpolar solvents dissolve nonpolar solutes.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Electron domain | Any bond (of any order) or lone pair around a central atom | Counting domains is step one of VSEPR |
| VSEPR theory | Model in which electron groups repel and spread as far apart as possible | Predicts molecular shape from Lewis structures |
| Electron-pair geometry | Arrangement of all domains (bonds + lone pairs) | The "parent" shape you look up from the domain count |
| Molecular geometry | Arrangement of atoms only, ignoring lone pairs | The actual shape (bent, pyramidal, etc.) that governs properties |
| Electronegativity | An atom's pull on shared electrons in a bond | Difference in electronegativity makes a bond polar |
| Bond dipole | Uneven charge distribution along a bond, \(\delta+\) to \(\delta-\) | The building block of molecular polarity |
| Dipole moment (\(\mu\)) | Charge separation measured as \(Q \times d\), in debyes | Quantitative measure of polarity |
| Polar molecule | Molecule with a nonzero net dipole moment | Sets solubility, boiling point, and biological behavior |

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Predict the shape of CO₂, H₂O, NH₃, and CH₄

For each molecule: draw the Lewis structure, count electron domains on the central atom, then name the electron-pair and molecular geometries.

- **CO₂:** central C has two double bonds = 2 domains → linear, 180°. Molecular geometry: linear.
- **H₂O:** central O has two single bonds + two lone pairs = 4 domains → tetrahedral electron-pair geometry; molecular geometry: **bent**, ~104.5°.
- **NH₃:** central N has three single bonds + one lone pair = 4 domains → tetrahedral electron-pair geometry; molecular geometry: **trigonal pyramidal**, ~107°.
- **CH₄:** central C has four single bonds = 4 domains → tetrahedral; molecular geometry: tetrahedral, 109.5°.

### Example 2: Is HCl polar, and how much charge is separated?

HCl has an electronegativity difference of \(3.16 - 2.20 = 0.96\), so the bond is polar; its measured dipole moment is \(\mu = 1.08\ \text{D}\) and bond length is \(127\ \text{pm}\).

**Step 1 — convert \(\mu\) to SI units:**

\[
\mu = 1.08\ \text{D} \times \frac{3.336\times10^{-30}\ \text{C}\cdot\text{m}}{1\ \text{D}} = 3.60\times10^{-30}\ \text{C}\cdot\text{m}
\]

**Step 2 — solve** \(\mu = Q \times d\) for the separated charge \(Q\), with \(d = 127\ \text{pm} = 1.27\times10^{-10}\ \text{m}\):

\[
Q = \frac{\mu}{d} = \frac{3.60\times10^{-30}\ \text{C}\cdot\text{m}}{1.27\times10^{-10}\ \text{m}} = 2.83\times10^{-20}\ \text{C}
\]

**Step 3 — compare with the electron charge** \(e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}\):

\[
\frac{Q}{e} = \frac{2.83\times10^{-20}\ \text{C}}{1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}} \approx 0.18
\]

The H–Cl bond therefore behaves as though about **18% of a full electron charge** were transferred from H to Cl — strongly polar covalent, not ionic. Units cancel at each step (D → C·m → C).

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Electron-pair geometry | Molecular geometry | Electron-pair geometry counts lone pairs; molecular geometry describes atoms only (H₂O: tetrahedral vs. bent) |
| Double bond = two domains | Double bond = one domain | VSEPR counts any bond order as one domain — a double bond occupies more space but counts once |
| Polar bonds guarantee a polar molecule | Polar bonds + geometry decide polarity | CO₂ and CCl₄ have polar bonds yet \(\mu = 0\) because dipoles cancel |
| Shape and polarity being separate topics | Shape feeding polarity | You must know the shape before you can sum the bond dipoles |
| Lone pairs counting as atom positions | Lone pairs affecting shape | Lone pairs compress angles but are not counted when naming molecular geometry |
| Larger electronegativity difference always = larger dipole moment | Difference sets direction, geometry sets net | Bond polarity needs \(\Delta\chi\); the *molecular* moment also depends on bond directions |

## Quick Review

1. How many electron domains does a central atom have with one double bond and two single bonds? What is the electron-pair geometry?
2. Why is the H–O–H angle in water smaller than the H–C–H angle in methane?
3. CO₂ and SO₂ both contain polar bonds. Why is CO₂ nonpolar but SO₂ polar?
4. Convert a dipole moment of 0.44 D (the value for CO) to C·m.
5. A molecule has four identical polar bonds arranged tetrahedrally. Is it polar? Explain.
6. Rank H–F, H–Cl, H–Br, and H–I from most to least polar using electronegativity.

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. Three domains (one double bond + two single bonds = 3) → trigonal planar.
2. Two lone pairs on O repel more strongly than bonding pairs, compressing the angle to ~104.5°; methane has four identical bonding pairs at 109.5°.
3. CO₂ is linear, so its C=O dipoles point opposite and cancel. SO₂ is bent (lone pair on S), so the S–O dipoles add to a nonzero resultant — SO₂ is polar.
4. \(0.44\ \text{D} \times (3.336\times10^{-30}\ \text{C}\cdot\text{m}/\text{D}) = 1.5\times10^{-30}\ \text{C}\cdot\text{m}\).
5. No. The four bond dipoles point toward the corners of a tetrahedron and sum to zero (exactly CCl₄).
6. H–F (1.78) > H–Cl (0.96) > H–Br (0.76) > H–I (0.46).
