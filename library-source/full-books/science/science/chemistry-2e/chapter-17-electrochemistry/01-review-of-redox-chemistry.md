# Review of Redox Chemistry

> **Book:** [Chemistry 2e](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 17: Electrochemistry](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 1  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Many of the most important reactions in chemistry and biology involve the transfer of electrons. **Redox** (reduction–oxidation) reactions include combustion, rusting, photosynthesis, respiration, battery discharge, and the metabolism of drugs. In every redox reaction, one species **loses** electrons — it is **oxidized** — while another species **gains** them — it is **reduced**. The two processes are inseparable: oxidation cannot occur without a simultaneous reduction, which is why we call them half-reactions that must be combined.

Because electrons are invisible, chemists use **oxidation numbers** as a bookkeeping tool to track electron transfer, and they split balanced equations into half-reactions to see exactly where electrons go. This topic reviews how to assign oxidation numbers, identify oxidizing and reducing agents, and balance redox equations in acidic and basic solution. It is the foundation for everything else in this chapter: galvanic cells, cell potentials, batteries, corrosion, and electrolysis all assume you can dissect a reaction into its half-reactions.

## Why This Matters

- **Every battery and fuel cell** works by separating the oxidation and reduction half-reactions so electrons must travel through a wire.
- **Corrosion of metals** (rusting) is a redox process; understanding it leads to prevention strategies covered later in this chapter.
- **Biology depends on it:** cellular respiration oxidizes glucose and reduces oxygen; photosynthesis does the reverse. Electron-transfer chains move energy through living systems.
- **Industrial chemistry:** extraction of metals from ores, electroplating, and water treatment (e.g., chlorine disinfection) are all redox processes.
- **Exams:** oxidation-number assignment and half-reaction balancing are guaranteed skills on any electrochemistry assessment — get them solid now.

## Core Concepts

### Oxidation and reduction

- **Oxidation** = loss of electrons (an increase in oxidation number).
- **Reduction** = gain of electrons (a decrease in oxidation number).
- Mnemonic: **OIL RIG** — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.

Electrons are conserved: the total number lost by the oxidized species equals the total gained by the reduced species. That is the constraint that makes balancing redox equations work.

### Oxidation numbers: the bookkeeping rules

Oxidation numbers are assigned by a small set of priority rules:

1. Free elements (uncombined) have oxidation number 0.
2. Monatomic ions have oxidation number equal to their charge.
3. In compounds, fluorine is always \(-1\).
4. Hydrogen is \(+1\) except in metal hydrides (e.g., NaH), where it is \(-1\).
5. Oxygen is \(-2\) except in peroxides (\(-1\), e.g., \(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\)) and in \(\text{OF}_2\) (\(+2\)).
6. The sum of oxidation numbers equals the charge of the species: 0 for neutral molecules, the ion charge for polyatomic ions.

### Oxidizing and reducing agents

- The **oxidizing agent** is the species that gets **reduced** (it accepts electrons and causes the other species to be oxidized).
- The **reducing agent** is the species that gets **oxidized** (it donates electrons).
- The agent is the *reactant species*, not the product. In \(\text{Zn} + \text{Cu}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + \text{Cu}\), \(\text{Cu}^{2+}\) is the oxidizing agent and Zn is the reducing agent.

### Half-reactions

A half-reaction shows only the oxidation or only the reduction part, including electrons as explicit participants. For example:

\[
\text{Zn} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + 2e^-
\]

\[
\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}
\]

Adding the two half-reactions (electrons cancel) gives the overall balanced equation. Half-reactions are also the units used to describe electrodes in cells.

### Balancing redox equations in acidic solution

The half-reaction method, in acid:

1. Split the skeleton equation into oxidation and reduction half-reactions.
2. Balance atoms other than O and H in each half-reaction.
3. Balance O by adding \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\).
4. Balance H by adding \(\text{H}^+\).
5. Balance charge by adding electrons.
6. Multiply each half-reaction so electrons cancel; add and simplify.

### Balancing redox equations in basic solution

Balance as if acidic, then neutralize: add \(\text{OH}^-\) to both sides to convert each \(\text{H}^+\) into \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\), combine and cancel water molecules.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

> Some reactions trade electrons like trading cards. When an atom gives away electrons, we say it got oxidized; when an atom takes electrons, it got reduced. Oxidizing agents are "electron stealers," and reducing agents are "electron givers." Chemists keep score with oxidation numbers, just like keeping score in a game, so they can see exactly who gave and who took.

## High-Yield Points

- Oxidation = loss of electrons (number increases); reduction = gain (number decreases). OIL RIG.
- Oxidation numbers: free element 0; O usually \(-2\) (peroxides \(-1\)); H usually \(+1\) (hydrides \(-1\)); sum equals species charge.
- The oxidizing agent is *reduced*; the reducing agent is *oxidized*. Identify agents from reactants, not products.
- Half-reactions must be balanced for atoms and charge; electrons cancel when combined.
- In acidic solution: balance O with \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\), H with \(\text{H}^+\), then charge with \(e^-\).
- In basic solution: balance as acidic, then add \(\text{OH}^-\) to each side to convert \(\text{H}^+\) to water.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| oxidation | Loss of electrons, raising an atom's oxidation number. | Half of every redox process; tracked by oxidation numbers. |
| reduction | Gain of electrons, lowering an atom's oxidation number. | The complementary half; the species that gains electrons is reduced. |
| oxidation number | Bookkeeping charge assigned by rules to track electron transfer. | Lets you see which atoms change oxidation state without drawing electrons. |
| oxidizing agent | The reactant that is reduced (accepts electrons). | Causes oxidation of the other species; its own oxidation number decreases. |
| reducing agent | The reactant that is oxidized (donates electrons). | Causes reduction of the other species; its own oxidation number increases. |
| half-reaction | One side of a redox process written with electrons shown. | The building block for balancing and for describing cell electrodes. |

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process

1. Assign oxidation numbers to every atom; identify which species changes state.
2. Write the two half-reactions (oxidation and reduction) with electrons on the appropriate sides.
3. Balance atoms: non-O/H first, then O with \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\), then H with \(\text{H}^+\) (acidic) or convert later (basic).
4. Balance charge with electrons; equalize electron counts by multiplying half-reactions.
5. Add the half-reactions, cancel electrons and any species appearing on both sides, and verify atoms and charge balance in the final equation.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Assigning oxidation numbers

Find the oxidation number of each element in \(\text{MnO}_4^-\), \(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\), and \(\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}\).

For \(\text{MnO}_4^-\): O is \(-2\) each (4 O atoms = \(-8\)); the ion charge is \(-1\), so Mn must be \(+7\):

\[
x + 4(-2) = -1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x = +7
\]

For \(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\): H is \(+1\) each (total \(+2\)); the molecule is neutral, so the two O atoms sum to \(-2\), giving O = \(-1\) each — the peroxide exception to the usual \(-2\).

For \(\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}\): O is \(-2\) each (7 O atoms = \(-14\)); the ion charge is \(-2\), so the two Cr atoms sum to \(+12\), giving Cr = \(+6\) each:

\[
2y + 7(-2) = -2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad 2y = +12 \quad \Rightarrow \quad y = +6
\]

### Example 2: Balancing in acidic solution

Balance \(\text{MnO}_4^- + \text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+} + \text{Fe}^{3+}\) in acid.

**Reduction half-reaction** (Mn: \(+7 \rightarrow +2\), gains 5 \(e^-\)):

\[
\text{MnO}_4^- + 8\text{H}^+ + 5e^- \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}
\]

**Oxidation half-reaction** (Fe: \(+2 \rightarrow +3\), loses 1 \(e^-\)):

\[
\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+} + e^-
\]

Electrons must cancel: multiply the oxidation half-reaction by 5, then add:

\[
\text{MnO}_4^- + 8\text{H}^+ + 5\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O} + 5\text{Fe}^{3+}
\]

Check: atoms balance (1 Mn, 4 O, 8 H, 5 Fe each side) and charge balances (\(-1 + 8 + 10 = +17\) left; \(+2 + 0 + 15 = +17\) right). The permanganate ion is the oxidizing agent (reduced); \(\text{Fe}^{2+}\) is the reducing agent (oxidized).

### Example 3: Balancing in basic solution

Balance \(\text{Al} + \text{NO}_3^- \rightarrow \text{Al(OH)}_4^- + \text{NH}_3\) in base.

Oxidation: \(\text{Al} + 4\text{OH}^- \rightarrow \text{Al(OH)}_4^- + 3e^-\)

Reduction: \(\text{NO}_3^- + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} + 8e^- \rightarrow \text{NH}_3 + 9\text{OH}^-\)

Balance electrons (LCM of 3 and 8 is 24): multiply oxidation by 8, reduction by 3, add and cancel:

\[
8\text{Al} + 3\text{NO}_3^- + 5\text{OH}^- + 18\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 8\text{Al(OH)}_4^- + 3\text{NH}_3
\]

Verify atoms and charge: left \(3(-1) + 5(-1) = -8\); right \(8(-1) = -8\). Balanced. Nitrate is the oxidizing agent; aluminum is the reducing agent.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Oxidizing agent | Species that is oxidized | The oxidizing agent is *reduced* (it takes electrons); the reducing agent is oxidized. |
| Oxidation number | Ionic charge | Oxidation numbers are bookkeeping assignments and can be fractional or larger than typical charges. |
| O in all compounds | O = \(-2\) everywhere | Peroxides (\(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\)) have O = \(-1\); \(\text{OF}_2\) has O = \(+2\). |
| H in all compounds | H = \(+1\) everywhere | In metal hydrides (NaH, CaH\(_2\)), H = \(-1\). |
| Balancing by inspection | Half-reaction balancing | Redox equations with polyatomic ions usually need the half-reaction method to balance charge properly. |

## Quick Review

1. In \(\text{Cu}^{2+} + \text{Zn} \rightarrow \text{Cu} + \text{Zn}^{2+}\), which species is oxidized, which is reduced, and which is the oxidizing agent?
2. What is the oxidation number of S in \(\text{SO}_4^{2-}\)?
3. Why must oxidation and reduction always occur together?
4. What is the first step in balancing a redox equation in basic solution?
5. True or false: In \(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\), oxygen has an oxidation number of \(-2\). Explain.

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. Zn is oxidized (0 → +2, loses electrons) and is the reducing agent; \(\text{Cu}^{2+}\) is reduced (+2 → 0, gains electrons) and is the oxidizing agent.
2. S = +6: \(x + 4(-2) = -2 \Rightarrow x = +6\).
3. Electrons are conserved — the electrons lost by the oxidized species must be gained by the reduced species, so the two processes are always coupled.
4. Balance the equation as if it were acidic (using \(\text{H}^+\) and \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\)), then add \(\text{OH}^-\) to both sides to convert \(\text{H}^+\) into water and simplify.
5. False — \(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\) is a peroxide, so each O is \(-1\) (H is \(+1\) each, and the molecule is neutral).
