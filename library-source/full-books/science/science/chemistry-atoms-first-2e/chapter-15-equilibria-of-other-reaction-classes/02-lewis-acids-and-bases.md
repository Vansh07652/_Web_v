# Lewis Acids and Bases

> **Book:** [Chemistry: Atoms First 2e](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 15: Equilibria of Other Reaction Classes](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 2  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

The Arrhenius and Brønsted–Lowry definitions tie acid–base behavior to protons, but many clearly acid–base reactions involve no H⁺ at all. The **Lewis definition** focuses on electrons: a **Lewis acid** is any species that can accept an electron pair, and a **Lewis base** is any species that can donate one. The shared pair forms a **coordinate covalent bond**, and the product is an **adduct** — or, when a metal ion is involved, a **complex ion**. This one idea unifies proton transfer, the acidic behavior of electron-deficient molecules like \(\text{BF}_3\) and \(\text{AlCl}_3\), the acidity of hydrated metal ions, and all of coordination chemistry. Because the Lewis definition is the most general of the three, every Brønsted–Lowry acid–base reaction is also a Lewis reaction — but many Lewis reactions involve no proton transfer at all.

## Why This Matters

Lewis acid–base chemistry explains reactions the other definitions cannot touch. Hemoglobin binds O₂ because the Fe²⁺ center is a Lewis acid accepting O₂'s lone pair; vitamin B₁₂ centers on a cobalt–ligand complex; enzymes use metal-ion cofactors as Lewis acids to polarize substrates. Chelation therapy for heavy-metal poisoning uses EDTA — a six-toothed Lewis base — to bind Pb²⁺ or Hg²⁺ and escort it out of the body. In industry, \(\text{AlCl}_3\) and \(\text{BF}_3\) are Lewis-acid catalysts for Friedel–Crafts reactions. In the lab, metal ions are Lewis acids, which is why solutions of salts like \(\text{FeCl}_3\) are acidic even though no H⁺ was added. The **formation constant** \(K_f\) gives this chemistry a quantitative handle — the same equilibrium mathematics as the \(K_{sp}\) of the previous topic.

## Core Concepts

### The Lewis definitions

- **Lewis acid:** electron-pair acceptor — \(\text{H}^+\), \(\text{BF}_3\), \(\text{AlCl}_3\), \(\text{Fe}^{3+}\), \(\text{Ag}^+\), \(\text{SO}_3\).
- **Lewis base:** electron-pair donor — \(\text{NH}_3\), \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\), \(\text{OH}^-\), \(\text{CN}^-\), halide ions, any lone-pair species.

In \(\text{BF}_3 + \text{NH}_3 \rightarrow \text{F}_3\text{B}{-}\text{NH}_3\), the nitrogen lone pair fills the empty orbital on boron, forming a new B–N bond. Both electrons come from the base, which is why the bond is called **coordinate covalent** — though once formed, it is identical to an ordinary covalent bond.

### How Lewis theory relates to the other definitions

The definitions nest: every Arrhenius base is a Brønsted–Lowry base, and every Brønsted–Lowry base is a Lewis base. Every Brønsted acid is a Lewis acid, because H⁺ must accept an electron pair from the base. But the Lewis definition includes species that never transfer a proton — \(\text{BF}_3\) is a Lewis acid that is not a Brønsted acid, and \(\text{AlCl}_3\) contains no hydrogen at all. When \(\text{H}_3\text{O}^+\) forms, you are watching a Lewis reaction: \(\text{H}^+\) (acid) accepts the lone pair of \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\) (base).

### Recognizing Lewis acids and bases from electron structure

- A species with an **empty orbital** and fewer than an octet is a Lewis acid: \(\text{BF}_3\) (boron has only 6 valence electrons), \(\text{AlCl}_3\), \(\text{H}^+\) (no electrons), and transition-metal cations (vacant orbitals).
- A species with an **available lone pair** is a Lewis base: \(\text{NH}_3\), \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\), \(\text{OH}^-\), \(\text{CN}^-\), \(\text{Cl}^-\).

Ligands are Lewis bases by definition; the metal cation is the Lewis acid. In \(\text{[Ag(NH}_3\text{)}_2\text{]}^+\), two ammonia molecules donate lone pairs to Ag⁺, and the coordination number is 2.

### Metal ions in water: why salt solutions can be acidic

A hydrated metal cation like \(\text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_6^{3+}\) is a Lewis acid: the highly charged Fe³⁺ pulls electron density away from the O–H bonds of the bound waters, making those protons easier to release:

\[\text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_6^{3+}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_5\text{(OH)}^{2+}(aq) + \text{H}_3\text{O}^+(aq)
\]

The stronger the Lewis acidity of the cation (higher charge, smaller radius), the more acidic the solution — which is why \(\text{FeCl}_3(aq)\) and \(\text{AlCl}_3(aq)\) are acidic.

### Formation constants \(K_f\)

Complex formation is an equilibrium:

\[\text{Ag}^+ + 2\,\text{NH}_3 \rightleftharpoons [\text{Ag(NH}_3\text{)}_2\text{]}^+ \qquad K_f = \frac{[[\text{Ag(NH}_3\text{)}_2\text{]}^+]}{[\text{Ag}^+][\text{NH}_3]^2} = 1.7 \times 10^7
\]

Large \(K_f\) values (often \(10^{10}\)–\(10^{30}\) for chelating ligands) mean the metal is almost entirely tied up in the complex — which is exactly why complexation can dissolve precipitates, the subject of the next topic.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

> A Lewis acid is a hungry catcher and a Lewis base is a ball-thrower: the base tosses a pair of electrons and the acid catches them to make a new bond. Some catchers have no protons to give — like a bouncer with an empty hand — but they still catch electrons, which is why this rule covers many more reactions than acids that simply give protons.

## High-Yield Points

- Lewis acid = electron-pair **acceptor**; Lewis base = electron-pair **donor**.
- Every Brønsted–Lowry acid–base reaction is a Lewis reaction; the reverse is false (\(\text{BF}_3\), \(\text{AlCl}_3\) are Lewis acids with no H⁺).
- The donated pair forms a coordinate covalent bond; the product is an adduct or complex ion.
- Electron-poor species (incomplete octet, cations, H⁺) are Lewis acids; lone-pair species are Lewis bases.
- Hydrated metal ions are Lewis acids: small, highly charged cations (\(\text{Fe}^{3+}\), \(\text{Al}^{3+}\)) make their solutions acidic by hydrolysis.
- \(K_f\) quantifies complex stability; chelating ligands (like EDTA) give very large \(K_f\) values used in therapy and analysis.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Lewis acid | Any species that accepts an electron pair | Extends acid–base chemistry beyond proton transfer |
| Lewis base | Any species that donates an electron pair | Includes all Brønsted bases and many more (NH₃, H₂O, halides) |
| coordinate covalent bond | A bond whose shared pair is donated entirely by one atom | Forms when a Lewis base attacks a Lewis acid; identical to a normal covalent bond once formed |
| adduct | The product of a Lewis acid–base reaction, e.g., \(\text{F}_3\text{B}{-}\text{NH}_3\) | The tangible result of electron-pair donation |
| ligand | A Lewis base that binds a metal ion in a complex | Determines the color, reactivity, and stability of metal complexes |
| complex ion | A metal cation surrounded by ligands, e.g., \([Ag(NH_3)_2]^+\) | Central to coordination chemistry, biology, and medicine |
| formation constant (\(K_f\)) | Equilibrium constant for complex-ion formation | Measures how completely a metal binds its ligands |

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process

### Classifying a reaction partner as Lewis acid or base

1. Draw the Lewis structure of each species.
2. Look for an empty orbital or incomplete octet (acid) versus an available lone pair (base).
3. Confirm by writing the electron-pair movement: a lone pair from the base forms the new bond to the acid.

### Using \(K_f\) to find free metal-ion concentration

1. Write the complex-formation equilibrium and its \(K_f\) expression.
2. When \(K_f\) is large, assume near-complete complexation; compute the complex concentration and the excess ligand concentration.
3. Solve the \(K_f\) expression for the tiny free metal-ion concentration.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Classifying and drawing the \(\text{BF}_3 + \text{NH}_3\) reaction

Boron has 3 valence electrons; in \(\text{BF}_3\) it forms three single bonds and holds no lone pair, so boron is surrounded by only 6 electrons — a **sextet**, one pair short of an octet. \(\text{BF}_3\) is electron-poor: a Lewis acid with an empty p orbital. Ammonia's nitrogen has a lone pair, making \(\text{NH}_3\) a Lewis base. The reaction:

\[\text{BF}_3 + \text{NH}_3 \rightarrow \text{F}_3\text{B}{-}\text{NH}_3
\]

The ammonia lone pair fills the empty orbital on boron, completing boron's octet with a coordinate covalent bond. This electron-poor behavior makes \(\text{BF}_3\) (and \(\text{AlCl}_3\)) useful Lewis-acid catalysts. Note that \(\text{BF}_3\) never donates or accepts a proton, so it is *not* a Brønsted acid — only the Lewis definition covers it.

### Example 2: How much free Ag⁺ remains in an ammonia solution?

The formation constant for \(\text{Ag}^+ + 2\,\text{NH}_3 \rightleftharpoons [\text{Ag(NH}_3\text{)}_2\text{]}^+\) is \(K_f = 1.7 \times 10^7\). Suppose 0.010 mol of Ag⁺ is added to 1.0 L of 1.0 M NH₃. Find the free \([\text{Ag}^+]\) at equilibrium.

Because \(K_f\) is very large, assume the complexation goes essentially to completion: \([\text{[Ag(NH}_3\text{)}_2\text{]}^+] \approx 0.010\ \text{M}\). Each complex consumes 2 NH₃, so:

\[
[\text{NH}_3]_{\text{eq}} = 1.0 - 2(0.010) = 0.98\ \text{M}
\]

Solve the \(K_f\) expression for the free silver:

\[
[\text{Ag}^+] = \frac{[[\text{Ag(NH}_3\text{)}_2\text{]}^+]}{K_f[\text{NH}_3]^2} = \frac{0.010}{(1.7 \times 10^7)(0.98)^2} = \frac{0.010}{1.63 \times 10^7} = 6.1 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{M}
\]

Free Ag⁺ falls to roughly \(6 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{M}\) — more than seven orders of magnitude below the total silver. This near-total sequestration is why ammonia can dissolve silver chloride (the next topic's coupled-equilibria idea).

### Example 3: Why a solution of FeCl₃ is acidic

Iron(III) chloride dissolves to give hydrated \(\text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_6^{3+}\) ions. The small, highly charged Fe³⁺ center is a Lewis acid that withdraws electron density from the O–H bonds of its six bound waters, weakening them:

\[\text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_6^{3+}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_5\text{(OH)}^{2+}(aq) + \text{H}_3\text{O}^+(aq)
\]

The released hydronium makes the solution acidic even though the salt contains no "obviously acidic" hydrogen. The same reasoning explains the acidity of \(\text{AlCl}_3\), \(\text{CuSO}_4\), and \(\text{CrCl}_3\) solutions: the smaller and more highly charged the cation, the stronger its Lewis acidity.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | The Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Lewis acid | Brønsted–Lowry acid | Lewis acids accept electron pairs; they need not contain or donate H⁺ (\(\text{BF}_3\), \(\text{AlCl}_3\), metal cations) |
| Lewis base | Brønsted–Lowry base | All Brønsted bases are Lewis bases, but any lone-pair donor (e.g., CN⁻ binding a metal) is a Lewis base without proton chemistry |
| Coordinate covalent bond | A special, weaker bond | Once formed, a coordinate bond is identical to any covalent bond; the label only records where the electrons came from |
| Acid = donor / base = acceptor | Acid = acceptor / base = donor | Lewis acid **accepts** the pair; Lewis base **donates** it. Getting this backwards fails every classification |
| "No H⁺, so not acid–base" | Lewis acid–base chemistry | Proton-free reactions like \(\text{BF}_3 + \text{NH}_3\) are acid–base reactions under the Lewis definition |
| Complex ion | Precipitate | Complex ions are soluble, charged species held by coordinate bonds; precipitates are solids governed by \(K_{sp}\) |

## Quick Review

1. State the Lewis definitions of acid and base.
2. Classify each species as a Lewis acid or base: \(\text{NH}_3\), \(\text{BF}_3\), \(\text{H}^+\), \(\text{OH}^-\), \(\text{Fe}^{3+}\), \(\text{CN}^-\).
3. Why is every Brønsted–Lowry acid–base reaction also a Lewis reaction?
4. Why is \(\text{BF}_3\) a Lewis acid even though it has no hydrogen?
5. Explain why a solution of \(\text{AlCl}_3\) is acidic.
6. In Example 2, why does sequestering free Ag⁺ cause AgCl to dissolve?

<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. Lewis acid: electron-pair acceptor. Lewis base: electron-pair donor.
2. Acids: \(\text{BF}_3\), \(\text{H}^+\), \(\text{Fe}^{3+}\). Bases: \(\text{NH}_3\), \(\text{OH}^-\), \(\text{CN}^-\) (all lone-pair donors).
3. Proton transfer always involves the base donating its electron pair to H⁺ — the definition of a Lewis acid–base reaction. The Lewis view is simply more general.
4. Boron in \(\text{BF}_3\) has only 6 valence electrons (a sextet) and an empty orbital; it can accept an electron pair, the defining ability of a Lewis acid.
5. \(\text{Al}^{3+}\) is a small, highly charged Lewis acid. In water it forms \(\text{Al(H}_2\text{O)}_6^{3+}\), whose bound waters have weakened O–H bonds; hydrolysis releases H⁺, acidifying the solution.
6. Complexation consumes free Ag⁺, dropping \([\text{Ag}^+]\) far below the saturation value. With \(Q = [\text{Ag}^+][\text{Cl}^-] < K_{sp}\), the dissolution equilibrium shifts right and AgCl dissolves.
