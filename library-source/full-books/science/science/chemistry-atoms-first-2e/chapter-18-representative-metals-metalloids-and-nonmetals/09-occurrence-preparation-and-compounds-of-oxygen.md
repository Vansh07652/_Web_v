# Occurrence, Preparation, and Compounds of Oxygen

> **Book:** [Chemistry: Atoms First 2e](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 18: Representative Metals, Metalloids, and Nonmetals](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 9  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Oxygen (O, atomic number 8) is the most abundant element in Earth's crust (about 46% by mass), the second most abundant in the atmosphere (about 21% by volume as \( \mathrm{O_2} \)), and roughly 89% of the mass of water. It is the great connector of chemistry: oxygen combines with nearly every other element, and its compounds — oxides — dominate minerals, rocks, soils, and the chemistry of combustion, respiration, and corrosion. The element exists in two important molecular forms: dioxygen, \( \mathrm{O_2} \), the colorless gas we breathe, and ozone, \( \mathrm{O_3} \), the pale-blue gas that shields us from ultraviolet radiation in the stratosphere. This topic covers where oxygen is found, how it is prepared (fractional distillation of liquid air, electrolysis of water, laboratory decompositions), and its compounds organized by oxidation state: −2 in oxides, −1 in peroxides, −½ in superoxides.

## Why This Matters

Oxygen chemistry explains everyday phenomena: combustion is rapid oxidation (why a fire needs fuel, oxygen, and heat); respiration is the controlled "burning" of glucose; hemoglobin carries oxygen and makes blood red. Corrosion (rusting) is slow oxidation of iron that costs billions of dollars a year. Ozone matters twice: in the stratosphere it absorbs harmful UV ("good" ozone), while at ground level it irritates lungs. And classifying oxides as acidic, basic, or amphoteric is one of the most-tested ideas in general chemistry.

## Core Concepts

### Occurrence: everywhere

Oxygen is the most abundant element in Earth's crust and oceans: about 46% of the crust by mass (in silicates, carbonates, and oxides), about 89% of water by mass, and about 21% of the atmosphere by volume as \( \mathrm{O_2} \). Nearly all free \( \mathrm{O_2} \) in the air is produced by photosynthesis — the biological reverse of combustion:

\[
\mathrm{6\,CO_2(g) + 6\,H_2O(l) \xrightarrow{light} C_6H_{12}O_6(aq) + 6\,O_2(g)}
\]

### Preparation: three standard routes

**Fractional distillation of liquid air** is the commercial method: air is liquefied and slowly warmed; \( \mathrm{N_2} \) (−195.8 °C) boils off first, leaving oxygen (−183.0 °C), about 99.5% pure, for hospitals, steelmaking, and rockets.

**Electrolysis of water** produces very pure oxygen (with hydrogen) by passing current through water containing an electrolyte:

\[
\mathrm{2\,H_2O(l) \xrightarrow{electrolysis} 2\,H_2(g) + O_2(g)}
\]

**Laboratory decomposition of hydrogen peroxide** is the classic demo source; manganese dioxide catalyzes it:

\[
\mathrm{2\,H_2O_2(aq) \xrightarrow{MnO_2} 2\,H_2O(l) + O_2(g)}
\]

### Properties: an oxidizing gas with a magnetic secret

Dioxygen is colorless, odorless, and slightly soluble in water (how fish breathe). Two properties deserve attention. First, \( \mathrm{O_2} \) is **paramagnetic** — attracted into a magnetic field — because molecular orbital theory predicts (and experiment confirms) two unpaired electrons; liquid oxygen visibly pours between the poles of a strong magnet. Second, oxygen is a powerful **oxidizing agent**: it accepts electrons from almost every element except the noble gases and a few metals (gold and platinum do not react directly with \( \mathrm{O_2} \) under ordinary conditions) — which is why "burning" is defined as rapid oxidation.

**Ozone**, \( \mathrm{O_3} \), is an allotrope of oxygen: a bent, pale-blue gas, a much stronger oxidizing agent than \( \mathrm{O_2} \), and the absorber of most solar UV-B in the stratosphere. It forms when \( \mathrm{O_2} \) is subjected to an electric discharge (lightning, ozonizers).

### Compounds: the oxidation states of oxygen

Oxygen normally has oxidation state **−2**, as in water (\( \mathrm{H_2O} \)) and magnesium oxide (\( \mathrm{MgO} \)). It is **−1 in peroxides** — compounds with the \( \mathrm{O_2^{2-}} \) ion, such as \( \mathrm{H_2O_2} \) and \( \mathrm{Na_2O_2} \) — and **−½ in superoxides** — containing \( \mathrm{O_2^-} \), such as \( \mathrm{KO_2} \), used in breathing gear because it releases \( \mathrm{O_2} \) while absorbing \( \mathrm{CO_2} \). Only with fluorine, the one element more electronegative than oxygen, does oxygen show a positive oxidation state: +2 in \( \mathrm{OF_2} \).

### Classifying oxides: acidic, basic, amphoteric, neutral

A favorite exam topic with a simple periodic rule:

- **Basic oxides** form with metals (e.g., \( \mathrm{Na_2O} \), \( \mathrm{CaO} \)) and react with water to give bases: \( \mathrm{CaO(s) + H_2O(l) \rightarrow Ca(OH)_2(aq)} \).
- **Acidic oxides** form with nonmetals (e.g., \( \mathrm{CO_2} \), \( \mathrm{SO_2} \), \( \mathrm{NO_2} \)) and react with water to give acids: \( \mathrm{CO_2(g) + H_2O(l) \rightarrow H_2CO_3(aq)} \).
- **Amphoteric oxides** (e.g., \( \mathrm{Al_2O_3} \), \( \mathrm{ZnO} \)) react with both acids and bases.
- **Neutral oxides** (e.g., \( \mathrm{CO} \), \( \mathrm{N_2O} \)) show neither behavior in water.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Oxygen is the "sharing superstar" of chemistry — it grabs electrons from almost everything, and when it does, we call it burning or rusting. Plants make it from sunlight and water; we bottle it by cooling air to a liquid and boiling nitrogen away first. Its twin, ozone, up high acts like sunglasses for Earth.

## High-Yield Points

- Oxygen is the **most abundant element in Earth's crust** (~46% by mass) and **~89% of water by mass**; \( \mathrm{O_2} \) is ~21% of the atmosphere by volume.
- **Commercial preparation:** fractional distillation of liquid air (N₂ boils off at −195.8 °C; O₂ at −183.0 °C).
- \( \mathrm{O_2} \) is **paramagnetic** (two unpaired electrons) — evidence for molecular orbital theory.
- **Oxidation states:** −2 (oxides), −1 (peroxides, \( \mathrm{O_2^{2-}} \)), −½ (superoxides, \( \mathrm{O_2^-} \)), +2 (only in \( \mathrm{OF_2} \)).
- **Oxide classification:** metal → basic; nonmetal → acidic; Al₂O₃/ZnO → amphoteric; CO/N₂O → neutral.
- **Ozone** \( \mathrm{O_3} \): stratospheric UV shield (good) vs ground-level pollutant (bad); stronger oxidizer than \( \mathrm{O_2} \).
- Photosynthesis: \( \mathrm{6\,CO_2 + 6\,H_2O \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6\,O_2} \) — the source of nearly all atmospheric \( \mathrm{O_2} \).

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Dioxygen (\( \mathrm{O_2} \)) | The diatomic, breathable form of oxygen | Supports combustion and respiration |
| Ozone (\( \mathrm{O_3} \)) | Triatomic allotrope of oxygen | Absorbs UV in the stratosphere; strong oxidizer and pollutant at ground level |

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Combustion stoichiometry — burning magnesium

How many grams of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) are required to react completely with 48.6 g of magnesium? Balanced equation: \( \mathrm{2\,Mg(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2\,MgO(s)} \). (Molar masses: Mg = 24.31 g/mol, O₂ = 32.00 g/mol.)

**Step 1 — Grams to moles:**

\[
n(\mathrm{Mg}) = 48.6\ \text{g} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol Mg}}{24.31\ \text{g Mg}} = 2.00\ \text{mol Mg}
\]

**Step 2 — Mole ratio (1 mol O₂ per 2 mol Mg):**

\[
2.00\ \text{mol Mg} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol O}_2}{2\ \text{mol Mg}} = 1.00\ \text{mol O}_2
\]

**Step 3 — Moles to grams:**

\[
1.00\ \text{mol O}_2 \times \frac{32.00\ \text{g O}_2}{1\ \text{mol O}_2} = 32.0\ \text{g O}_2
\]

**Unit check:** g Mg → mol Mg → mol O₂ → g O₂. Answer: 32.0 g O₂.

### Example 2: Gas volume from hydrogen peroxide decomposition

What volume of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) at STP (22.4 L/mol) is produced when 68.0 g of \( \mathrm{H_2O_2} \) decomposes completely? Balanced equation: \( \mathrm{2\,H_2O_2(aq) \rightarrow 2\,H_2O(l) + O_2(g)} \). (Molar mass H₂O₂ = 34.01 g/mol.)

**Step 1 — Grams to moles:**

\[
n(\mathrm{H_2O_2}) = 68.0\ \text{g} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol H}_2\mathrm{O_2}}{34.01\ \text{g H}_2\mathrm{O_2}} = 2.00\ \text{mol H}_2\mathrm{O_2}
\]

**Step 2 — Mole ratio (1 mol O₂ per 2 mol H₂O₂):**

\[
2.00\ \text{mol H}_2\mathrm{O_2} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol O}_2}{2\ \text{mol H}_2\mathrm{O_2}} = 1.00\ \text{mol O}_2
\]

**Step 3 — Moles to volume at STP:**

\[
V = 1.00\ \text{mol O}_2 \times \frac{22.4\ \text{L}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 22.4\ \text{L O}_2
\]

**Check:** this is why 3% hydrogen peroxide fizzes on a cut — blood catalase releases the oxygen.

### Example 3: Percent oxygen in water

Water is about what percent oxygen by mass? (Molar masses: H = 1.008, O = 16.00 g/mol.)

**Step 1 — Formula:**

\[
M(\mathrm{H_2O}) = 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.02\ \text{g/mol}
\]

**Step 2 — Percent:**

\[
\%\mathrm{O} = \frac{16.00\ \text{g O}}{18.02\ \text{g H}_2\mathrm{O}} \times 100\% = 88.8\%
\]

That "89%" quoted in textbooks is exactly this — nearly nine-tenths of the mass of every glass of water is oxygen.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| "Oxygen burns" | "Oxygen supports burning" | O₂ is the oxidizer, not the fuel; a flame needs fuel + O₂ + heat |
| Oxide (\( \mathrm{O^{2-}} \), O = −2) | Peroxide (\( \mathrm{O_2^{2-}} \), O = −1) | Peroxides have an O–O bond and one fewer electron per O; H₂O₂ vs H₂O |

## Quick Review

1. Which element is most abundant in Earth's crust, and roughly what percent is oxygen?
2. Why is \( \mathrm{O_2} \) paramagnetic, and what theory explains it?
3. What are the oxidation states of oxygen in \( \mathrm{MgO} \), \( \mathrm{H_2O_2} \), and \( \mathrm{KO_2} \)?
4. How many grams of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) come from decomposing 170.0 g of \( \mathrm{H_2O_2} \) (H₂O₂ = 34.01 g/mol)?
5. Classify these oxides as acidic, basic, or amphoteric: \( \mathrm{Na_2O} \), \( \mathrm{SO_2} \), \( \mathrm{Al_2O_3} \).

<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. Oxygen (~46% by mass) is the most abundant element in the crust; silicon is second (~28%).
2. O₂ has two unpaired electrons in antibonding π orbitals; molecular orbital theory predicts and explains this (a Lewis structure cannot).
3. MgO: −2; H₂O₂: −1 (each O in the O–O bond); KO₂: −½ (each O in \( \mathrm{O_2^-} \)).
4. Moles H₂O₂ = \( 170.0\ \text{g} \div 34.01\ \text{g/mol} = 5.00\ \text{mol} \); moles O₂ = \( 5.00 \times \frac{1}{2} = 2.50\ \text{mol} \); mass = \( 2.50 \times 32.00 = 80.0\ \text{g O}_2 \).
5. \( \mathrm{Na_2O} \): basic (metal oxide); \( \mathrm{SO_2} \): acidic (nonmetal oxide); \( \mathrm{Al_2O_3} \): amphoteric (reacts with both acids and bases).
