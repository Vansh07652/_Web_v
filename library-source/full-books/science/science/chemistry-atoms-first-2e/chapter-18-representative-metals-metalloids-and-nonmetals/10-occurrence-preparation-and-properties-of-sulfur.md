# Occurrence, Preparation, and Properties of Sulfur

> **Book:** [Chemistry: Atoms First 2e](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 18: Representative Metals, Metalloids, and Nonmetals](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 10  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Sulfur (S, atomic number 16) is the yellow, smelly workhorse of the periodic table — a nonmetal that occurs both free and combined, shows more allotropes than any other element, and whose compounds range from the stench of rotten eggs to the world's most important industrial acid. Free sulfur is found as crown-shaped \( \mathrm{S_8} \) molecules in volcanic regions and salt-dome deposits, while combined sulfur appears in sulfide ores (pyrite, galena, cinnabar) and sulfate minerals (gypsum). Sulfur is extracted by the Frasch process, which melts underground deposits with superheated water and lifts the molten element to the surface with compressed air. Its chemistry rests on oxidation states of −2 (sulfides, \( \mathrm{H_2S} \)), 0 (elemental \( \mathrm{S_8} \)), +4 (sulfur dioxide, sulfites), and +6 (sulfur trioxide, sulfate, sulfuric acid). This topic covers occurrence, preparation, the allotropes, key properties, and the contact process that turns sulfur into sulfuric acid — the most-produced chemical in industry.

## Why This Matters

Sulfuric acid production is a barometer of industrial output: more \( \mathrm{H_2SO_4} \) is made worldwide than any other chemical, and it is used in fertilizers, batteries, petroleum refining, and metal processing. Sulfur also matters for environment and health: burning sulfur-containing coal and oil releases \( \mathrm{SO_2} \), the main cause of acid rain; hydrogen sulfide is a toxic, rotten-egg gas that can paralyze the sense of smell; and sulfate aerosols reflect sunlight and cool the atmosphere. In biology, sulfur is essential — the amino acids cysteine and methionine and the disulfide bonds that hold protein structure together all contain it. On exams, sulfur tests allotropy, oxidation-state bookkeeping, and contact-process stoichiometry, plus the acid–base behavior of its oxides.

## Core Concepts

### Occurrence: free and combined

Sulfur is about the 16th most abundant element in Earth's crust and, besides carbon, the only common nonmetal found free in large deposits. **Native sulfur** occurs in volcanic regions and in salt domes (Gulf Coast of the United States), often as crown-shaped \( \mathrm{S_8} \) ring molecules in orthorhombic crystals. Combined sulfur is widespread:

- **Sulfide ores:** pyrite (\( \mathrm{FeS_2} \), "fool's gold"), galena (\( \mathrm{PbS} \)), cinnabar (\( \mathrm{HgS} \)), sphalerite (\( \mathrm{ZnS} \)).
- **Sulfate minerals:** gypsum (\( \mathrm{CaSO_4 \cdot 2\,H_2O} \)), Epsom salts (\( \mathrm{MgSO_4 \cdot 7\,H_2O} \)).
- **Organic sulfur:** in petroleum, coal, and the amino acids cysteine and methionine.

### Preparation: the Frasch process and roasting

**Frasch process.** Underground sulfur is melted in place by pumping superheated water (about 170 °C) down an outer pipe; molten sulfur (melting point ~115 °C) is forced to the surface through an inner pipe by compressed air. The product is over 99% pure — no chemical purification needed.

**From sulfide ores.** Roasting sulfides to extract metals also produces sulfur dioxide. Roasting pyrite:

\[
\mathrm{4\,FeS_2(s) + 11\,O_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\,Fe_2O_3(s) + 8\,SO_2(g)}
\]

The \( \mathrm{SO_2} \) can be converted back to elemental sulfur (Claus process) or fed into sulfuric acid manufacture.

### Allotropes: one element, many structures

- **Orthorhombic sulfur** (\( \mathrm{S_8} \) rings): the stable form below 95.5 °C; the pale-yellow crystals found in nature.
- **Monoclinic sulfur**: needle-like crystals of \( \mathrm{S_8} \), stable between 95.5 °C and the melting point (~119 °C).
- **Plastic (amorphous) sulfur**: above about 160 °C the \( \mathrm{S_8} \) rings open and link into long chains, making the liquid dark and viscous; rapid cooling traps the chains as a rubbery solid that slowly reverts to crystalline sulfur.

Above ~445 °C sulfur vapor contains \( \mathrm{S_8} \), \( \mathrm{S_6} \), \( \mathrm{S_4} \), and \( \mathrm{S_2} \) molecules — "molecular sulfur" is a family, not a single species.

### Properties and oxidation states

Elemental sulfur is a yellow, brittle solid, insoluble in water but soluble in carbon disulfide, \( \mathrm{CS_2} \). It burns in air with a blue flame to give \( \mathrm{SO_2} \). Its chemistry is built on four oxidation states:

- **−2 (sulfides):** \( \mathrm{H_2S} \) is a weak, toxic acid smelling of rotten eggs; metal sulfides like \( \mathrm{FeS} \) are common minerals.
- **0:** elemental sulfur, \( \mathrm{S_8} \).
- **+4:** \( \mathrm{SO_2} \), a pungent gas forming sulfurous acid, \( \mathrm{H_2SO_3} \), in water; sulfites contain \( \mathrm{SO_3^{2-}} \).
- **+6:** \( \mathrm{SO_3} \) and sulfate, \( \mathrm{SO_4^{2-}} \); the highest and most important state, anchored by sulfuric acid.

Because sulfur spans −2 to +6, its compounds are versatile redox partners: \( \mathrm{H_2S} \) is a reducing agent, \( \mathrm{SO_2} \) can be oxidized or reduced, and concentrated \( \mathrm{H_2SO_4} \) is a strong oxidizer as well as a strong acid.

### The contact process: from sulfur to sulfuric acid

Sulfuric acid is made in three steps:

\[
\mathrm{S(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow SO_2(g)}
\]

\[
\mathrm{2\,SO_2(g) + O_2(g) \xrightarrow{V_2O_5,\ \sim450\,^\circ C} 2\,SO_3(g)}
\]

\[
\mathrm{SO_3(g) + H_2O(l) \rightarrow H_2SO_4(aq)}
\]

The middle step is an equilibrium, so it uses a vanadium(V) oxide catalyst and moderate temperature to balance speed against yield (Le Châtelier's principle). Industrially, \( \mathrm{SO_3} \) is first dissolved in concentrated \( \mathrm{H_2SO_4} \) to make oleum (\( \mathrm{H_2S_2O_7} \)), then diluted with water — adding \( \mathrm{SO_3} \) directly to water is violently exothermic.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Sulfur is the yellow element that smells like rotten eggs and can take many shapes, like a toy that comes as rings, needles, or stretchy rubber. We dig it up by pumping hot water underground to melt it, then blow it to the surface like a bubble through a straw. Its best trick is teaming up with oxygen to make sulfuric acid — the acid in car batteries and a factory workhorse used to make almost everything.

## High-Yield Points

- Native sulfur is **\( \mathrm{S_8} \) crown rings**; allotropes: orthorhombic (stable < 95.5 °C), monoclinic (95.5–119 °C), plastic/amorphous (quenched polymer chains).
- **Frasch process:** superheated water (~170 °C) melts underground S; compressed air lifts it; product >99% pure.
- **Roasting sulfides** produces \( \mathrm{SO_2} \): \( \mathrm{4\,FeS_2 + 11\,O_2 \rightarrow 2\,Fe_2O_3 + 8\,SO_2} \).
- **Oxidation states:** −2 (\( \mathrm{H_2S} \), sulfides), 0 (\( \mathrm{S_8} \)), +4 (\( \mathrm{SO_2} \), \( \mathrm{SO_3^{2-}} \)), +6 (\( \mathrm{SO_3} \), \( \mathrm{SO_4^{2-}} \), \( \mathrm{H_2SO_4} \)).
- **Contact process:** \( \mathrm{S \rightarrow SO_2 \rightarrow SO_3 \xrightarrow{V_2O_5} H_2SO_4} \); oleum (\( \mathrm{H_2S_2O_7} \)) intermediates the \( \mathrm{SO_3 + H_2O} \) step.
- \( \mathrm{H_2SO_4} \) is the **most-produced industrial chemical**; concentrated acid is a strong acid, oxidizer, and dehydrating agent.
- \( \mathrm{SO_2} \) from burning sulfur fuels causes **acid rain**; \( \mathrm{H_2S} \) is toxic and paralyzes the sense of smell.
- Sulfur is essential in biology: cysteine, methionine, and protein disulfide bonds.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Allotrope | Different structural form of the same element | Orthorhombic, monoclinic, and plastic sulfur behave differently |
| Frasch process | Mining molten sulfur with superheated water + compressed air | The main commercial source of native sulfur |
| Sulfide | Compound with \( \mathrm{S^{2-}} \) (S in −2) | Major ore minerals (pyrite, galena) and the toxic gas H₂S |
| Sulfur dioxide | \( \mathrm{SO_2} \), pungent gas with S in +4 | Combustion product, acid-rain precursor, contact-process feedstock |
| Sulfate | \( \mathrm{SO_4^{2-}} \) ion (S in +6) | Dominant form of sulfur in minerals, seawater, and fertilizers |
| Contact process | Catalytic route \( \mathrm{SO_2 + \frac{1}{2}O_2 \rightarrow SO_3 \rightarrow H_2SO_4} \) | How the world's most-produced chemical is made |
| Oleum | \( \mathrm{H_2S_2O_7} \), SO₃ dissolved in H₂SO₄ | Safe way to add SO₃ to water; avoids violent splashing |

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Contact process — how much acid from how much sulfur?

How many grams of \( \mathrm{H_2SO_4} \) can be made from 32.1 g of sulfur, assuming complete conversion? (Molar masses: S = 32.07 g/mol, H₂SO₄ = 98.09 g/mol.)

**Step 1 — Note the atom chain:** S → SO₂ → SO₃ → H₂SO₄. Every sulfur atom is conserved, so the overall mole ratio is 1:1.

**Step 2 — Grams to moles:**

\[
n(\mathrm{S}) = 32.1\ \text{g} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol S}}{32.07\ \text{g S}} = 1.00\ \text{mol S}
\]

**Step 3 — Apply the 1:1 ratio and convert to grams:**

\[
1.00\ \text{mol H}_2\mathrm{SO_4} \times \frac{98.09\ \text{g H}_2\mathrm{SO_4}}{1\ \text{mol H}_2\mathrm{SO_4}} = 98.1\ \text{g H}_2\mathrm{SO_4}
\]

**Check:** 32 g of sulfur becomes about 98 g of acid — mass increases because oxygen and hydrogen were added. The atom-conservation shortcut saves three separate calculations.

### Example 2: Roasting pyrite — sulfur dioxide production

How many grams of \( \mathrm{SO_2} \) are released when 480 g of pyrite, \( \mathrm{FeS_2} \), is roasted? Balanced equation: \( \mathrm{4\,FeS_2(s) + 11\,O_2(g) \rightarrow 2\,Fe_2O_3(s) + 8\,SO_2(g)} \). (Molar masses: FeS₂ = 119.99 g/mol, SO₂ = 64.07 g/mol.)

**Step 1 — Grams to moles:**

\[
n(\mathrm{FeS_2}) = 480\ \text{g} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol FeS}_2}{119.99\ \text{g FeS}_2} = 4.00\ \text{mol FeS}_2
\]

**Step 2 — Mole ratio (8 mol SO₂ per 4 mol FeS₂):**

\[
4.00\ \text{mol FeS}_2 \times \frac{8\ \text{mol SO}_2}{4\ \text{mol FeS}_2} = 8.00\ \text{mol SO}_2
\]

**Step 3 — Moles to grams:**

\[
8.00\ \text{mol SO}_2 \times \frac{64.07\ \text{g SO}_2}{1\ \text{mol SO}_2} = 512.6\ \text{g SO}_2
\]

**Real-world note:** that SO₂ must be captured (scrubbed) or converted to acid; released to air, the same mass contributes directly to acid rain.

### Example 3: Percent sulfur in pyrite

What is the mass percent of sulfur in \( \mathrm{FeS_2} \)? (Molar masses: Fe = 55.85, S = 32.07 g/mol.)

**Step 1 — Formula for molar mass:**

\[
M(\mathrm{FeS_2}) = 55.85 + 2(32.07) = 119.99\ \text{g/mol}
\]

**Step 2 — Percent sulfur:**

\[
\%\mathrm{S} = \frac{2(32.07)\ \text{g S}}{119.99\ \text{g FeS}_2} \times 100\% = 53.5\%
\]

More than half the mass of "fool's gold" is actually sulfur — which is why roasting it produces so much SO₂.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Sulfide \( \mathrm{S^{2-}} \) | Sulfite \( \mathrm{SO_3^{2-}} \) | Sulfide is a bare −2 ion (S in −2); sulfite is an oxoanion (S in +4) |
| Sulfite \( \mathrm{SO_3^{2-}} \) | Sulfate \( \mathrm{SO_4^{2-}} \) | One more oxygen: sulfite (S+4) vs sulfate (S+6) — a classic spelling/charge trap |
| Sulfur dioxide \( \mathrm{SO_2} \) | Sulfur trioxide \( \mathrm{SO_3} \) | SO₂ is a combustion gas; SO₃ is the +6 oxide made catalytically in the contact process |
| H₂S smell = reliable warning | H₂S at deadly concentrations | H₂S paralyzes the olfactory nerve: at high levels you may smell nothing |
| Orthorhombic vs monoclinic sulfur | Different elements | Both are \( \mathrm{S_8} \) — only crystal packing differs; temperature decides which is stable |
| Plastic sulfur | A durable polymer | Amorphous sulfur chains revert to crystalline S₈ over time |
