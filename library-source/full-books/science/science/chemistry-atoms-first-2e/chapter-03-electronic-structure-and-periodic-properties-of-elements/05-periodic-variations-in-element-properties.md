# Periodic Variations in Element Properties

> **Book:** [Chemistry: Atoms First 2e](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 3: Electronic Structure and Periodic Properties of Elements](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 5  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Certain atomic properties change in predictable, repeating patterns across the periodic table. The most important are **atomic radius**, **ionization energy**, **electron affinity**, and **electronegativity**. All four trends trace back to one idea from Topic 4: how tightly the nucleus holds the outermost electrons.

The key quantity is the **effective nuclear charge** (\(Z_{\text{eff}}\)) — the net positive charge felt by an outer electron after inner electrons shield it. Moving across a period, protons increase while shielding stays roughly constant, so \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) rises and outer electrons are pulled in tighter. Moving down a group, a whole new shell is added, so electrons sit farther from the nucleus even though \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) also rises somewhat.

The two "master trends":

- **Across a period (left → right):** \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) increases → atoms shrink, ionization energy and electronegativity increase.
- **Down a group (top → bottom):** new shells add distance → atoms grow, ionization energy and electronegativity decrease.

These trends are why the periodic table predicts chemistry: fluorine is tiny, greedy for electrons, and highly reactive; cesium is huge, loses its single valence electron easily, and is explosively reactive with water.

## Why This Matters

Periodic trends are the predictive engine of chemistry:

- **Atomic radius** tells you how atoms pack, bond, and whether ions fit in crystal lattices (think battery materials, alloys, and drug–receptor geometry).
- **Ionization energy** explains why metals form cations easily (low values) and why noble gases are unreactive (very high values); it sets the stage for ionic compounds in Topic 7.
- **Electron affinity** and **electronegativity** predict whether a bond is ionic, polar covalent, or nonpolar covalent — the basis of solubility, acidity, and reactivity.
- **In biology and medicine**, electronegativity differences explain why water is polar (essential for life), why hydrogen bonds hold DNA together, and why certain elements are toxic (e.g., lead's affinity for sulfur in enzymes).

For exams, trend questions are almost always "which is bigger/smaller/higher/lower" — and the answer follows from \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) and shell number. If you can explain those two drivers, you can answer any trend problem without memorizing individual elements.

## Core Concepts

### Effective nuclear charge and shielding

Inner (core) electrons screen the outer electrons from the full nuclear charge. The effective nuclear charge felt by an outer electron is approximately:

\[
Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - S
\]

where \(Z\) is the atomic number and \(S\) is the shielding constant (roughly the number of core electrons, in the simplest estimate). Across a period, \(Z\) climbs by one each step while \(S\) changes little, so \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) increases left to right. Down a group, the new shell adds distance and shielding, so the outer electron is held more loosely despite the larger \(Z\).

### Atomic radius

The **atomic radius** is half the distance between the nuclei of two identical atoms bonded together (or a comparable measure for metals). Trends:

- **Across a period:** decreases (more protons pull the same shell inward — e.g., Na 186 pm → Cl 99 pm).
- **Down a group:** increases (each new shell adds a layer — e.g., Li 152 pm → Cs 265 pm).

Ionic radii follow the same logic: cations are smaller than their parent atoms (lost an outer shell), anions are larger (added electrons with no new protons). Isoelectronic ions (same electron count) shrink as nuclear charge increases: O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺ for the 10-electron series.

### Ionization energy

**Ionization energy (IE)** is the energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion:

\[
\text{Na}(g) \to \text{Na}^+(g) + e^- \qquad IE_1 = 496\ \text{kJ/mol}
\]

- **Across a period:** increases (rising \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) holds electrons tighter).
- **Down a group:** decreases (outer electrons are farther and more shielded).

Successive ionization energies always increase — and there is a dramatic jump when you try to remove a core electron: Na's \(IE_1 = 496\), \(IE_2 = 4562\ \text{kJ/mol}\). That tenfold jump is the signature of the noble-gas core, explaining why sodium forms Na⁺ and never Na²⁺.

Two famous anomalies come from subshell stability: Mg (\(3s^2\)) has higher \(IE_1\) than Al (\(3s^2 3p^1\)) because removing a p electron is easier than breaking a filled s pair; P (\(3p^3\), half-filled) has higher \(IE_1\) than S (\(3p^4\)).

### Electron affinity

**Electron affinity (EA)** is the energy change when a gaseous atom gains an electron:

\[
\text{Cl}(g) + e^- \to \text{Cl}^-(g) \qquad \Delta E = -349\ \text{kJ/mol}
\]

More negative EA means the atom more strongly attracts an added electron. Trends: generally more negative across a period (halogens are the stars), less negative down a group. Exceptions follow stable configurations: adding an electron to nitrogen (\(3p^3\), half-filled) or to an s² element costs energy rather than releasing it.

### Electronegativity

**Electronegativity** is a measure of an atom's ability to attract shared electrons in a chemical bond (Pauling scale). Trends: increases across a period, decreases down a group. Fluorine (3.98) is the most electronegative element; francium and cesium are among the least. The difference in electronegativity between bonded atoms predicts bond type: large difference → ionic; small difference → polar covalent; near zero → nonpolar covalent.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I’m 10

> Imagine the nucleus is a magnet and electrons are marbles. Across a row, the magnet gets stronger while the marbles stay on the same shelf, so they get pulled in closer — atoms shrink and hold their marbles more tightly. Going down a column, the marbles move to a higher shelf, farther away, so atoms get bigger and let go of their marbles more easily. That is why metals at the bottom-left lose electrons happily, while fluorine at the top-right grabs electrons like a greedy magnet.

## High-Yield Points

- All trends trace to \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) (increases across a period) and shell number (increases down a group).
- Atomic radius: decreases across, increases down.
- Ionization energy: increases across, decreases down; huge jump after removing all valence electrons (core electrons).
- Electron affinity: most negative for halogens; exceptions at filled/half-filled subshells (Be, N).
- Electronegativity: increases across, decreases down; F is highest (3.98).
- Cations are smaller than parent atoms; anions are larger; isoelectronic ions shrink with increasing \(Z\).
- Trend anomalies: Mg > Al in \(IE_1\); P > S in \(IE_1\).
- The electronegativity difference between bonded atoms predicts ionic vs. covalent character.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| effective nuclear charge (\(Z_{\text{eff}}\)) | Net positive charge felt by an outer electron after shielding | The root cause of every periodic trend |
| atomic radius | Half the distance between nuclei of bonded identical atoms | Governs packing, bond length, and ion size |
| ionization energy | Energy to remove an electron from a gaseous atom/ion | Explains cation formation and metallic reactivity |
| electron affinity | Energy change when a gaseous atom gains an electron | Measures how eagerly an atom accepts electrons |
| electronegativity | Ability of an atom to attract shared bonding electrons | Predicts bond polarity and compound behavior |
| shielding | Reduction of nuclear pull by inner electrons | Explains why outer electrons are held loosely |
| isoelectronic | Ions or atoms with the same number of electrons | Allows clean comparisons of size vs. nuclear charge |

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process

To answer any "which is larger/higher/more negative" trend question:

1. Locate both elements on the periodic table.
2. Note whether they differ mainly by row (shell number) or by column (effective nuclear charge).
3. If same row: the one farther right has higher \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) → smaller radius, higher IE, higher electronegativity, more negative EA.
4. If same column: the one lower down has more shells → larger radius, lower IE, lower electronegativity.
5. If both differ (different row and column), compare the dominant factor: shell number usually wins for radius and IE (e.g., K vs Li), while position is judged element by element.
6. Check for exceptions: filled/half-filled subshells (Mg vs Al, P vs S) and the noble-gas jump in successive IEs.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Ranking atomic radii

Rank these elements from largest to smallest atomic radius: Na, Mg, Cl, F.

All four are in period 3 except F, which is in period 2. Compare shell by shell. Fluorine is period 2 (two shells), while Na, Mg, and Cl are period 3 (three shells), so F is the smallest overall.

Within period 3, radius decreases left to right as \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) rises:

\[
\text{Na} > \text{Mg} > \text{Cl}
\]

Combining:

\[
\text{Na}\ (186\ \text{pm}) > \text{Mg}\ (160\ \text{pm}) > \text{Cl}\ (99\ \text{pm}) > \text{F}\ (71\ \text{pm})
\]

The logic: Na has the fewest protons pulling its three shells; each step right adds a proton to the same shell, tightening the electron cloud.

### Example 2: Predicting the first ionization energy jump

Why does sodium's second ionization energy (~4562 kJ/mol) dwarf its first (496 kJ/mol)?

Na⁺ has the electron configuration of neon, \(1s^2 2s^2 2p^6\) — a filled noble-gas core. Removing the next electron means breaking into that stable core, which sits closer to the nucleus and feels a much higher \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) (the 2p electrons are shielded by almost nothing and held by 11 protons). The ~10-fold jump in energy is the classic evidence for shell structure and explains why sodium's chemistry is dominated by the 1+ ion.

### Example 3: Isoelectronic ion sizes

Order these isoelectronic ions by increasing radius: Na⁺, Mg²⁺, F⁻, O²⁻.

All four have 10 electrons (like neon). The electron cloud is pulled by a different number of protons:

| Ion | Protons | Radius (pm) |
|---|---|---|
| O²⁻ | 8 | 140 |
| F⁻ | 9 | 133 |
| Na⁺ | 11 | 102 |
| Mg²⁺ | 12 | 72 |

More protons pull the same 10 electrons tighter, so radius decreases with increasing \(Z\):

\[
\text{O}^{2-} > \text{F}^- > \text{Na}^+ > \text{Mg}^{2+}
\]

This is why Mg²⁺ is a tiny, highly charged ion — and why it binds water so strongly in biological systems.

## Common Confusions

| Common Confusion | Correct Understanding |
|---|---|
| "More protons always means bigger atom." | Across a period, more protons mean a *smaller* atom (higher \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) pulls the same shell inward). |
| Ionization energy increases down a group. | It *decreases* down a group because new shells place electrons farther out with more shielding. |
| Cations are larger than their atoms. | Cations are smaller — they lose their outermost shell and feel higher \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) per remaining electron. |
| Electron affinity is "how easily an atom loses electrons." | EA is about *gaining* electrons (energy change on adding one); ionization energy is about losing them. |
| Fluorine has the highest electron affinity. | Chlorine actually has the most negative EA; fluorine's small size makes the added electron experience strong repulsion. |
| Electronegativity and electron affinity are the same. | Both measure electron attraction, but EA is for a free atom gaining an electron; electronegativity is for atoms in a bond. |
| Trend rules have no exceptions. | Subshell stability causes real exceptions (Mg > Al, P > S in IE₁; N and Be in EA). |

## Quick Review

1. Why does atomic radius decrease across a period?
2. Which has the higher first ionization energy: Mg or Al, and why?
3. Arrange N³⁻, O²⁻, F⁻ by increasing radius (all 10 electrons... except N³⁻ has 10, O²⁻ 10, F⁻ 10 — which is largest?).
4. Why is fluorine the most electronegative element?
5. What causes the huge jump between IE₁ and IE₂ for sodium?
6. Which bond is more ionic: Na–Cl or C–Cl? Justify with electronegativities.

<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. Moving across a period adds protons without adding a shell, so \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) rises and the same shell is pulled inward.
2. Mg (738 kJ/mol) > Al (578 kJ/mol): removing a 3p electron from Al is easier than breaking the filled 3s² pair in Mg.
3. Increasing radius: F⁻ < O²⁻ < N³⁻ — fewer protons pulling the same 10-electron cloud means a looser, larger ion.
4. Fluorine has the highest \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) among the small, nearly unscreened second-period atoms, so it attracts bonding electrons most strongly (Pauling value 3.98).
5. After losing its single 3s valence electron, Na⁺ has a filled neon core; removing a core electron requires breaking into a stable, tightly held shell — roughly ten times more energy.
6. Na–Cl: electronegativity difference ≈ 3.16 − 0.93 ≈ 2.2 (largely ionic). C–Cl: difference ≈ 3.16 − 2.55 ≈ 0.6 (polar covalent). The larger the difference, the more ionic the bond.
