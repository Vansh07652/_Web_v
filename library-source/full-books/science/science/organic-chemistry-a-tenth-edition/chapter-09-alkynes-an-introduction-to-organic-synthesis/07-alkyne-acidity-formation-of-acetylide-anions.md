# Alkyne Acidity: Formation of Acetylide Anions

> **Book:** [Organic Chemistry: A Tenth Edition](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 9: Alkynes: An Introduction to Organic Synthesis](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 7  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Terminal alkynes — alkynes with a hydrogen on the triple-bond carbon, \(\mathrm{R{-}C{\equiv}CH}\) — are the most acidic hydrocarbons. Their \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) is about **25**, compared with roughly 44 for the \(\mathrm{sp^2}\) C–H of an alkene and about 50 for the \(\mathrm{sp^3}\) C–H of an alkane. That is still weak compared with water (\(\mathrm{p}K_a\) 15.7), but it is acidic enough that a sufficiently strong base can remove the terminal proton and generate an **acetylide anion**, \(\mathrm{R{-}C{\equiv}C^-}\):

\[
\mathrm{R{-}C{\equiv}CH + NaNH_2 \rightarrow R{-}C{\equiv}C^-Na^+ + NH_3}
\]

The key to this reaction is choosing the right base. Sodium amide, \(\mathrm{NaNH_2}\), works because its conjugate acid, ammonia, has a \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) of about 38 — higher than 25 — so the equilibrium favors the acetylide. Sodium hydroxide does **not** work, because water's \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) (15.7) is lower than the alkyne's; the equilibrium lies the other way. This topic explains why alkynes are unusually acidic, how hybridization controls acidity, and how to form the acetylide anions that power the carbon–carbon bond-forming reactions of the next topic.

## Why This Matters

The acetylide anion is one of the most important carbon nucleophiles in organic synthesis — a negatively charged carbon that attacks an electrophile to form a new carbon–carbon bond. That is the fundamental way chemists build larger skeletons, and it is exactly what the next topic exploits to convert simple alkynes into complex internal alkynes. Beyond synthesis, the acidity trend sp > sp² > sp³ is a cornerstone of organic theory: it shows how hybridization, electronegativity, and the stability of the conjugate base are connected. Understanding why acetylene is acidic also explains real-world chemistry, from biochemical deprotonations to industrial metal acetylides.

## Core Concepts

### Why terminal alkynes are acidic: the role of s-character

Acidity is governed by the stability of the conjugate base. When the proton is removed from a terminal alkyne, the negative charge sits on a carbon that is **sp-hybridized**. An sp hybrid orbital has 50% s-character (one s orbital mixed with one p orbital), and s orbitals hold electrons closer to the nucleus than p orbitals do. The closer the electron pair sits to the nucleus, the better the carbon can stabilize the negative charge:

- \(\mathrm{sp}\) carbon: 50% s-character → most electronegative → most stable anion → most acidic (\(\mathrm{p}K_a\) ≈ 25)
- \(\mathrm{sp^2}\) carbon: 33% s-character → intermediate (\(\mathrm{p}K_a\) ≈ 44)
- \(\mathrm{sp^3}\) carbon: 25% s-character → least electronegative → least stable anion → least acidic (\(\mathrm{p}K_a\) ≈ 50)

This is a clean example of a general rule: **increasing s-character increases acidity of the C–H bond**. The same logic explains why the C–H bonds of alkenes and arenes are more acidic than those of alkanes.

### Acetylide ions: structure and properties

Removing the terminal proton leaves the acetylide ion, \(\mathrm{R{-}C{\equiv}C^-}\), with a full negative charge on the linear sp carbon, stabilized by s-character and the adjacent triple bond. Acetylides are both strongly basic and strongly nucleophilic — powerful but selective reagents. Because they react with water and even air's moisture, they are generated and used in anhydrous conditions (liquid ammonia or ether solvents). As a general laboratory principle, strong-base work requires proper PPE, a fume hood where appropriate, and exclusion of water.

### Choosing the base: a pKa comparison

The rule for deprotonation is that a base whose conjugate acid has a **higher** pKa than the acid being deprotonated will drive the equilibrium toward products. Compare:

- \(\mathrm{NaNH_2}\) (conjugate acid \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) ≈ 38): **works** — 38 > 25, so the acetylide forms.
- \(\mathrm{NaH}\) (conjugate acid \(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) ≈ 35): **works**.
- \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) or \(\mathrm{KOH}\) (conjugate acid \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) ≈ 15.7): **does not work** — 15.7 < 25, so the alkyne stays protonated.
- Alcohols/alkoxides (conjugate acid \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) ≈ 16–18): **do not work** for the same reason.

Sodium amide in liquid ammonia is the standard reagent: strong enough to deprotonate the alkyne, while the ammonia solvent keeps the acetylide stable and manageable.

### Terminal versus internal alkynes

Only **terminal** alkynes have an acidic hydrogen. An internal alkyne such as 2-butyne, \(\mathrm{CH_3C{\equiv}CCH_3}\), has no hydrogen on either triple-bond carbon, so it cannot form an acetylide under these conditions. This distinction matters enormously in synthesis: the ability to deprotonate is what allows selective functionalization of one end of a molecule, and it is why terminal alkynes are the workhorses of alkyne chemistry.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Think of a hydrogen atom on the end of a molecule as a ball sitting on a hill. On a normal alkane the hill is very steep, so the ball stays put — the hydrogen almost never comes off. On a terminal alkyne the hill is gentler, so with a strong "shove" (a strong base like sodium amide) the ball rolls off, leaving the molecule with a negative charge — that's the acetylide ion. The steeper the hill, the harder it is to knock the ball off; the gentler the hill, the easier. Triple-bond carbons make the gentlest hills, which is why alkynes are the most acidic hydrocarbons.

## High-Yield Points

- **Terminal alkyne \(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 25\)** — the most acidic hydrocarbon C–H.
- **Acidity trend:** sp (25) > sp² (44) > sp³ (50), driven by s-character and conjugate-base stability.
- **NaNH₂ works; NaOH does not.** Base selection is a pKa comparison: the base's conjugate acid must have a higher pKa than the alkyne (38 > 25 for NH₃; 15.7 < 25 for H₂O).
- **Only terminal alkynes** (\(\mathrm{RC{\equiv}CH}\)) form acetylides; internal alkynes have no acidic proton.
- **Product:** acetylide anion \(\mathrm{R{-}C{\equiv}C^-}\) — a carbon nucleophile and strong base.
- **Lab principle:** acetylides react with water/moisture; generate and use them in anhydrous conditions with standard PPE and hood use where required.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Terminal alkyne | Alkyne with a hydrogen on the triple-bond carbon (\(\mathrm{RC{\equiv}CH}\)) | The only alkynes with an acidic proton |
| Acetylide anion | The conjugate base \(\mathrm{R{-}C{\equiv}C^-}\) | A carbon nucleophile used to build C–C bonds |
| pKa | A number measuring acid strength (lower = stronger acid) | The tool for predicting which base deprotonates what |
| s-character | Fraction of s-orbital character in a hybrid orbital | Controls carbon electronegativity and anion stability |
| Conjugate base | What remains after an acid loses its proton | Its stability determines the acid's strength |
| Sodium amide | \(\mathrm{NaNH_2}\), a strong base whose conjugate acid is NH₃ (pKa 38) | The standard reagent for forming acetylides |

## Example: Deprotonation and Base Selection

**Worked example 1 (base selection).** Can \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) deprotonate 1-hexyne, \(\mathrm{CH_3(CH_2)_3C{\equiv}CH}\)? Compare pKa values. The alkyne's \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) is 25; water's \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) is 15.7. Since 15.7 < 25, the equilibrium

\[
\mathrm{R{-}C{\equiv}CH + OH^- \rightleftharpoons R{-}C{\equiv}C^- + H_2O}
\]

lies to the **left** — the hydroxide cannot pull the proton off. With sodium amide instead, the conjugate acid (NH₃) has \(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 38 > 25\), so the equilibrium lies to the right and the acetylide forms:

\[
\mathrm{R{-}C{\equiv}CH + NaNH_2 \rightarrow R{-}C{\equiv}C^-Na^+ + NH_3}
\]

**Worked example 2 (stoichiometry with dimensional analysis).** How many grams of \(\mathrm{NaNH_2}\) are needed to deprotonate 10.0 g of 1-hexyne completely? The reaction is 1:1 in moles. First the moles of alkyne, then the moles and mass of amide:

\[
n(\mathrm{1\text{-}hexyne}) = \frac{10.0\ \mathrm{g}}{82.15\ \mathrm{g/mol}} = 0.122\ \mathrm{mol}
\]

\[
n(\mathrm{NaNH_2}) = 0.122\ \mathrm{mol} \times \frac{1\ \mathrm{mol\ NaNH_2}}{1\ \mathrm{mol\ alkyne}} = 0.122\ \mathrm{mol}
\]

\[
m(\mathrm{NaNH_2}) = 0.122\ \mathrm{mol} \times 39.01\ \mathrm{g/mol} = 4.75\ \mathrm{g}
\]

So **4.75 g of NaNH₂** is required for complete deprotonation (theoretical; practice often uses a slight excess to drive the equilibrium).

**Worked example 3 (conceptual).** Rank these C–H bonds by acidity: ethane, ethylene, acetylene. Answer: acetylene (sp, pKa ≈ 25) > ethylene (sp², pKa ≈ 44) > ethane (sp³, pKa ≈ 50). The reason is the increasing s-character of the C–H carbon along that series, which stabilizes the negative conjugate base.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | The Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Terminal alkyne acidity | Internal alkyne "acidity" | Only RC≡CH has an acidic proton; internal alkynes cannot form acetylides |
| pKa | pH | pKa is an intrinsic property of a molecule (acidity); pH describes a solution's H⁺ level |
| NaNH₂ | NaOH | NaNH₂'s conjugate acid (NH₃, pKa 38) is weaker than the alkyne, so it works; NaOH's conjugate acid (H₂O, pKa 15.7) is stronger, so it fails |
| "Acidic alkyne" | Strong acid | pKa ≈ 25 is still very weak in everyday terms; it means "acidic for a hydrocarbon," not like HCl |
| Acetylide as base | Acetylide as nucleophile | It is both; which role it plays depends on the reaction partner (proton donors vs. electrophilic carbons) |
