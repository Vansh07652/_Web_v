# Oxidative Cleavage of Alkynes

> **Book:** [Organic Chemistry: A Tenth Edition](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 9: Alkynes: An Introduction to Organic Synthesis](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 6  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Oxidative cleavage tears the alkyne apart at the triple bond, converting the two alkyne carbons into carboxylic acid carbons. The standard reagents are ozone (\(\mathrm{O_3}\)) followed by a water workup, or hot, concentrated potassium permanganate (\(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\)). The general patterns are:

- **Internal alkyne** \(\mathrm{R{-}C{\equiv}C{-}R'}\) → two carboxylic acids, \(\mathrm{RCOOH}\) and \(\mathrm{R'COOH}\).
- **Terminal alkyne** \(\mathrm{R{-}C{\equiv}CH}\) → one carboxylic acid \(\mathrm{RCOOH}\) plus \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (the terminal \(\mathrm{CH}\) carbon becomes carbon dioxide).

Because each carbon of the triple bond becomes a carboxyl carbon, the products are like a "carbon-counting fingerprint" of the original alkyne. This makes oxidative cleavage a classic structure-determination tool: run the reaction, identify the acids, and deduce the alkyne. It also complements the cleavage of alkenes (which give ketones/aldehydes) and completes the picture of how carbon skeletons are broken down by oxidation.

## Why This Matters

Oxidative cleavage answers two questions chemists ask constantly: "What is this molecule?" and "How do I make a carboxylic acid?" When an unknown alkyne is cleaved, the identities of the carboxylic acids reveal the length and branching of the alkyl groups on each side of the triple bond — no spectroscopy required, just careful product identification. In synthesis, cleavage converts alkynes into valuable diacids and monoacids; adipic acid, a precursor to nylon, and many pharmaceutical carboxylic acids are built from this kind of carbon-skeleton logic. For exams, the carbon-counting rule (every alkyne carbon ends up as a \(\mathrm{COOH}\) carbon, except a terminal \(\mathrm{CH}\) which becomes \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)) is a favorite trap that rewards careful bookkeeping.

## Core Concepts

### The cleavage reaction with ozone

Ozonolysis of an alkyne uses ozone followed by water. The triple bond is completely broken, and each alkyne carbon is oxidized to the +3 oxidation state of a carboxylic acid carbon:

\[
\mathrm{R{-}C{\equiv}C{-}R' \xrightarrow{1)\ O_3 \quad 2)\ H_2O} R{-}COOH + R'{-}COOH
\]

For a terminal alkyne, the terminal carbon is oxidized all the way to carbon dioxide:

\[
\mathrm{R{-}C{\equiv}CH \xrightarrow{1)\ O_3 \quad 2)\ H_2O} R{-}COOH + CO_2
\]

The reaction works because ozone is a strong oxidizing agent; the unstable molozonide-type intermediate collapses in the aqueous workup to the acid products. Note the contrast with alkene ozonolysis, which stops at aldehydes or ketones because the alkene carbons are only oxidized to the +1 or +2 oxidation state.

### Cleavage with potassium permanganate

Hot, concentrated \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) (often with acid) also cleaves alkynes to carboxylic acids. The stoichiometry for an internal alkyne can be written with permanganate as the oxidant:

\[
\mathrm{3\,R{-}C{\equiv}C{-}R' + 8\,KMnO_4 + 4\,H_2O \rightarrow 3\,R{-}COOH + 3\,R'{-}COOH + 8\,KOH + 8\,MnO_2}
\]

Terminal alkynes again give a carboxylic acid plus \(\mathrm{CO_2}\). Permanganate cleavage is harsher than ozonolysis — it can also oxidize other easily oxidized groups present in the molecule — so ozonolysis is often preferred for delicate substrates. Both reagents share the same product logic: each triple-bond carbon becomes a carboxyl carbon.

### Carbon counting and structure determination

The single most useful skill in this topic is working backward: given the cleavage products, reconstruct the alkyne. The rule is simple:

- The two carbons of the triple bond become the two carboxyl carbons of the products.
- Every carbon attached to the triple bond stays attached to its carboxyl carbon.
- A terminal \(\mathrm{CH}\) on the alkyne becomes \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (one carbon, no alkyl group).

For example, if cleavage of an unknown alkyne gives only acetic acid (\(\mathrm{CH_3COOH}\)), the alkyne must have been 2-butyne, \(\mathrm{CH_3C{\equiv}CCH_3}\). If it gives acetic acid and hexanoic acid, the alkyne was 2-octyne, \(\mathrm{CH_3C{\equiv}C(CH_2)_4CH_3}\). This bookkeeping converts an "unknown" problem into simple addition: the alkyne is the two acids minus their two \(\mathrm{OH}\) groups, joined at the carboxyl carbons.

### Why cleavage is useful in synthesis

Oxidative cleavage is a way to "cut and label" a carbon skeleton. Because the products are strongly functionalized acids, cleavage is used to prepare dicarboxylic acids (e.g., from cyclic or di-alkynes), to degrade natural products for structural analysis, and to generate short-chain acids from long-chain alkynes. The reaction also illustrates the general principle that strong oxidants push carbon to its highest common oxidation state (+3 in carboxylic acids; terminal carbons go to +4 in \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)).

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Imagine a triple bond is a stick with a candy on each end, and the stick is cut exactly in the middle. Each cut end grows a "COOH" tag, which is what a carboxylic acid looks like. If one end of the stick had no candy (that's a terminal alkyne), that bare end turns into fizzy carbon dioxide gas instead. If you know what the two tagged pieces look like, you can figure out how long the original stick was — that's how chemists use cleavage to identify unknown molecules.

## High-Yield Points

- **Internal alkyne** cleavage → **two carboxylic acids** (one from each side of the triple bond).
- **Terminal alkyne** cleavage → **one carboxylic acid + CO₂** (the terminal CH carbon becomes \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)).
- **Reagents:** \(\mathrm{O_3}\) then water, or hot concentrated \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\).
- **Carbon-counting rule:** each triple-bond carbon becomes a \(\mathrm{COOH}\) carbon; count carbons carefully in both directions (product → alkyne and alkyne → product).
- **Alkene vs. alkyne cleavage:** alkenes give ketones/aldehydes; alkynes give carboxylic acids (+ \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) for terminal) because alkynes are oxidized further.
- **Symmetric alkynes** give one acid twice — a useful simplification in problems.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Oxidative cleavage | Breaking a C–C bond while oxidizing both carbons | Converts alkynes into carboxylic acids; key structure tool |
| Ozonolysis | Reaction with ozone followed by an aqueous workup | Standard, controllable way to cleave alkynes and alkenes |
| Carboxylic acid | Compound with a \(\mathrm{COOH}\) group | The characteristic product of alkyne cleavage |
| Carbon dioxide | \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), the fate of a terminal alkyne carbon | Terminal CH carbons are lost as gas — an exam trap |
| Internal alkyne | Triple bond between two nonterminal carbons | Cleaves to two acids (possibly identical if symmetric) |
| Terminal alkyne | Triple bond at the end of a chain, \(\mathrm{RC{\equiv}CH}\) | Cleaves to one acid plus \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) |

## Example: Cleavage and Structure Determination

**Worked example 1 (product prediction).** Cleave 4-octyne, \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2C{\equiv}CCH_2CH_2CH_3}\), with ozone followed by water. Each side of the triple bond is a butyl group, so the products are two molecules of butanoic acid:

\[
\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2C{\equiv}CCH_2CH_2CH_3 \xrightarrow{1)\ O_3 \quad 2)\ H_2O} 2\,CH_3CH_2CH_2COOH}
\]

**Worked example 2 (structure determination, backward reasoning).** An unknown alkyne is cleaved to give only acetic acid, \(\mathrm{CH_3COOH}\). Since both carboxyl carbons came from the triple bond and each bears a methyl group, the alkyne must be 2-butyne, \(\mathrm{CH_3C{\equiv}CCH_3}\). If instead the products are propanoic acid (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2COOH}\)) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), the alkyne was 1-butyne, \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2C{\equiv}CH}\): the propyl side becomes propanoic acid and the terminal CH carbon becomes \(\mathrm{CO_2}\).

**Worked example 3 (stoichiometry with dimensional analysis).** How many grams of acetic acid can be produced (theoretically) by cleaving 10.0 g of 2-butyne, \(\mathrm{CH_3C{\equiv}CCH_3}\)? Each mole of alkyne gives 2 mol of acetic acid:

\[
n(\mathrm{2\text{-}butyne}) = \frac{10.0\ \mathrm{g}}{54.09\ \mathrm{g/mol}} = 0.185\ \mathrm{mol}
\]

\[
n(\mathrm{acetic\ acid}) = 0.185\ \mathrm{mol} \times \frac{2\ \mathrm{mol}}{1\ \mathrm{mol\ alkyne}} = 0.370\ \mathrm{mol}
\]

\[
m(\mathrm{acetic\ acid}) = 0.370\ \mathrm{mol} \times 60.05\ \mathrm{g/mol} = 22.2\ \mathrm{g}
\]

The theoretical yield is **22.2 g of acetic acid** (actual yield will be lower; the calculation assumes complete cleavage and recovery).

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | The Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Alkyne cleavage products | Alkene cleavage products | Alkynes → carboxylic acids (+ CO₂ if terminal); alkenes → ketones/aldehydes |
| Terminal alkyne fate | Internal alkyne fate | Terminal CH carbon becomes CO₂ (lost as gas); internal carbons both stay as COOH |
| Ozonolysis of alkynes | Ozonolysis of alkenes | Same reagent, different oxidation levels: alkynes go further, to acids |
| \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) carbon | Carboxylic acid carbon | The terminal carbon is oxidized to +4 (CO₂); the other carbons end at +3 (COOH) |
| "Cleavage" | "Hydrogenation" | Cleavage breaks the C–C bond and oxidizes; hydrogenation adds H₂ without breaking the skeleton |

4. Why do alkynes give carboxylic acids on ozonolysis while alkenes give aldehydes or ketones?
5. How many moles of acetic acid form from 1 mol of 2-butyne on complete cleavage?
6. Name two reagent systems that cleave alkynes to carboxylic acids.
