# Acids and Bases: The Brønsted–Lowry Definition

> **Book:** [Organic Chemistry: A Tenth Edition](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 2: Polar Covalent Bonds; Acids and Bases](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 7  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

The Brønsted–Lowry definition recasts acid–base chemistry in terms of proton transfer. An acid is any species that donates a proton (a hydrogen ion, \(\text{H}^+\)), and a base is any species that accepts one. Every acid–base reaction therefore produces a conjugate base (the acid minus its proton) and a conjugate acid (the base plus a proton). This definition works in any solvent and covers far more than the Arrhenius acids and bases of general chemistry — including the carbon acids, ammonium salts, and protonated heteroatoms that dominate organic mechanisms. The strength of an acid is quantified by its acid-dissociation constant \(K_a\) and its \(pK_a\), which are introduced here and developed in Topic 8.

## Why This Matters

Proton transfer is the most frequent elementary step in organic chemistry. Nearly every mechanism — acid-catalyzed ester hydrolysis, enamine formation, aldol condensation, enzyme catalysis — begins or ends with a proton being handed from one molecule to another. In medicine, the fraction of a drug that is neutral rather than ionized at physiological pH (about 7.4) determines whether it crosses membranes and reaches its target; that fraction is controlled by the drug's \(pK_a\) through the Henderson–Hasselbalch relationship. The same equilibrium chemistry maintains blood pH through bicarbonate buffers. Understanding Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases is therefore not just a chapter exercise — it is the operating system for organic reactions and the key to drug absorption and physiological buffering.

## Core Concepts

### The definition

A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton donor; a Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor. The proton is a bare hydrogen nucleus, \(\text{H}^+\), with no electrons, so it can never exist alone in solution: it must be transferred from one bond to another. In water, an acid transfers its proton to a water molecule, forming the hydronium ion, \(\text{H}_3\text{O}^+\).

### Conjugate pairs

When an acid \(\text{HA}\) loses its proton it becomes \(\text{A}^-\), the conjugate base of \(\text{HA}\). When a base \(\text{B}\) gains a proton it becomes \(\text{BH}^+\), the conjugate acid of \(\text{B}\). Acid and conjugate base always differ by one proton; base and conjugate acid always differ by one proton. Every acid–base reaction has two conjugate pairs: \(\text{HA}/\text{A}^-\) and \(\text{BH}^+/\text{B}\). A strong acid has a weak conjugate base, and a strong base has a weak conjugate acid.

### Water: amphoteric behavior

Water can act as an acid (donating a proton to ammonia to give \(\text{OH}^-\) and \(\text{NH}_4^+\)) or as a base (accepting a proton from HCl to give \(\text{H}_3\text{O}^+\) and \(\text{Cl}^-\)). Its self-ionization,

\[
2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_3\text{O}^+ + \text{OH}^-
\]

has the equilibrium constant \(K_w = [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+][\text{OH}^-] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}\) at 25 °C. This single constant anchors all pH and \(pK_a\) calculations.

### Acid strength and Ka

For an acid \(\text{HA}\) in water, \(\text{HA} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_3\text{O}^+ + \text{A}^-\), the acid-dissociation constant is:

\[
K_a = \frac{[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+][\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}
\]

A larger \(K_a\) means more dissociation, hence a stronger acid. Because \(K_a\) values span many orders of magnitude, chemists use \(pK_a = -\log_{10} K_a\): the smaller the \(pK_a\), the stronger the acid. Water itself has \(pK_a \approx 15.7\) (using \([\text{H}_2\text{O}] = 55.5\ \text{M}\)), acetic acid has \(pK_a \approx 4.74\), and HCl has \(pK_a \approx -7\).

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

> A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a molecule that hands a proton (a tiny hydrogen core) to a partner, and a base is the partner that catches it. It works like a relay race: the acid passes the baton and becomes the empty-handed conjugate base, while the base catches it and becomes the loaded conjugate acid. Every great acid makes a calm, weak conjugate base — the better the pass, the more comfortable the catcher.

## High-Yield Points

- Brønsted–Lowry acid = proton donor; base = proton acceptor; water is both (amphoteric).
- Acid and conjugate base differ by one proton; base and conjugate acid differ by one proton.
- Every acid–base reaction contains two conjugate pairs and proceeds by proton transfer.
- \(K_a = [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+][\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}]\); \(pK_a = -\log_{10} K_a\); smaller \(pK_a\) = stronger acid.
- \(K_w = [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+][\text{OH}^-] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}\) at 25 °C; pure water has \([\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] = 1.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{M}\).
- Strong acid ⇄ weak conjugate base; strong base ⇄ weak conjugate acid.
- A proton has no electrons; it cannot exist free in solution and must transfer directly from one bond to another.
- Carboxylic acids, ammonium salts, and protonated alcohols are the acid forms most common in organic mechanisms.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Brønsted–Lowry acid | Any species that donates a proton, \(\text{H}^+\). | Defines acidity by proton transfer, covering carbon acids and charged species. |
| Brønsted–Lowry base | Any species that accepts a proton. | Includes neutral amines, anions, and water. |
| conjugate base | What remains after an acid loses a proton (\(\text{A}^-\) from \(\text{HA}\)). | Its stability determines acid strength. |
| conjugate acid | What forms when a base gains a proton (\(\text{BH}^+\) from \(\text{B}\)). | Its \(pK_a\) measures the base's strength. |
| proton | A hydrogen nucleus, \(\text{H}^+\), with no electrons. | The transferred particle in every Brønsted–Lowry reaction. |
| amphoteric | Able to act as both acid and base (e.g., water). | Explains water's central role in acid–base equilibria. |
| \(K_a\) / \(pK_a\) | Acid-dissociation constant and its negative base-10 log. | The quantitative scale for acid strength. |
| \(K_w\) | Ion-product constant of water, \(1.0 \times 10^{-14}\) at 25 °C. | Anchors pH, \(K_a\), and \(pK_a\) calculations. |

## Example 1: Labeling a Proton-Transfer Reaction

Write the reaction of acetic acid with ammonia and label all four participants:

\[
\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{NH}_3 \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- + \text{NH}_4^+
\]

Acetic acid donates a proton to ammonia, so acetic acid is the acid and ammonia is the base. The acetate ion is acetic acid's conjugate base; the ammonium ion is ammonia's conjugate acid. The two conjugate pairs are \(\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}/\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-\) and \(\text{NH}_4^+/\text{NH}_3\). Because acetic acid is a much stronger acid than ammonium ion (\(pK_a\) 4.74 vs. 9.3), the equilibrium lies to the right — acetic acid protonates ammonia almost completely.

## Example 2: Hydronium Ion and the pH of a Strong Acid

Hydrochloric acid is a strong acid: in water it ionizes completely, \(\text{HCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{H}_3\text{O}^+ + \text{Cl}^-\). For a \(0.010\ \text{M}\) HCl solution, the hydronium concentration equals the acid concentration:

\[
[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] = 0.010\ \text{M}
\]

The pH is defined by \(\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+]\). Substituting:

\[
\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(0.010) = 2.00
\]

Unit check: \([\text{H}_3\text{O}^+]\) has units mol/L, and the log of a dimensionless ratio yields a dimensionless pH, so the result carries no units. The strong-acid case is the fastest pH calculation in chemistry and the benchmark against which weak-acid problems are compared.

## Example 3: Percent Ionization of a Weak Acid

Acetic acid in water does not dissociate fully. Its \(K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}\) at 25 °C. For a \(1.0\ \text{M}\) solution, let \(x = [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+]\). Then the equilibrium expression is:

\[
K_a = \frac{x^2}{1.0 - x} \approx \frac{x^2}{1.0}
\]

The approximation is valid because \(x\) is small compared with 1.0 M. Solving:

\[
x = \sqrt{K_a \times 1.0\ \text{M}} = \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-5} \times 1.0\ \text{M}^2} = \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}^2} = 4.2 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M}
\]

Dimensional analysis confirms the algebra: \(K_a\) has units M, multiplied by 1.0 M gives M², and the square root restores M — a concentration. Only about 0.42% of the acetic acid is ionized:

\[
\frac{4.2 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M}}{1.0\ \text{M}} \times 100\% = 0.42\%
\]

Weak acids are mostly undissociated, which is why vinegar tastes sour rather than burning: the concentration of free \(\text{H}_3\text{O}^+\) stays low.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Acid strength | Acid concentration | Strength is an equilibrium property (\(K_a\)); concentration is how much acid is present. |
| Conjugate base | A base in general | The conjugate base is specifically the acid minus a proton; any species can act as a base. |
| Large \(K_a\) | Large \(pK_a\) | Larger \(K_a\) = stronger acid; larger \(pK_a\) = weaker acid. They move in opposite directions. |
| \(\text{H}^+\) free in solution | \(\text{H}_3\text{O}^+\) in water | Protons cannot exist alone; in water they are always attached to water as hydronium. |
| Brønsted–Lowry acid | Lewis acid | Brønsted acids donate protons; Lewis acids accept electron pairs (Topic 11). |
| "Strong" acid | "Dangerous" acid | Strong means fully ionized; hazards depend on concentration, quantity, and exposure. |
