# Spectroscopy of Aldehydes and Ketones

> **Book:** [Organic Chemistry: A Tenth Edition](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 19: Aldehydes and Ketones: Nucleophilic Addition Reactions](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 14  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Spectroscopy is the art of reading molecular structure from how a molecule interacts with light or fragments in a mass spectrometer. The carbonyl group \(\text{C}=\text{O}\) is one of the most distinctive structural "reporters" in organic chemistry: it absorbs infrared light strongly, its carbon and nearby protons appear at extreme shifts in NMR spectra, and its mass-spectral fragmentation is highly predictable. Because both families contain the carbonyl group, they share most spectral features; the aldehyde's extra \(\text{C}-\text{H}\) bond provides the clearest distinction. This topic covers what to look for in IR, \({}^1\text{H}\) NMR, \(^{13}\text{C}\) NMR, and mass spectrometry, and how to combine the evidence to identify an unknown carbonyl compound.

## Why This Matters

Structure determination is a daily task in research, pharmaceutical quality control, and forensic chemistry, and spectroscopy is the fastest way to do it. Aldehydes and ketones are everywhere in biology and industry: the aldehyde retinal captures light in our eyes, acetone is a common solvent and biological "ketone body," and many drugs and flavors are carbonyl compounds. Confirming "this is an aldehyde, not a ketone" from spectra is a classic exam skill and a genuine workplace skill.

## Core Concepts

### Infrared spectroscopy: the carbonyl stretch

The \(\text{C}=\text{O}\) double bond absorbs infrared light strongly near 1715–1725 cm⁻¹. Saturated aliphatic ketones absorb near 1715 cm⁻¹; aldehydes absorb slightly higher, near 1725 cm⁻¹. Two structural changes shift this band predictably: conjugation with a \(\text{C}=\text{C}\) double bond or aromatic ring lowers the frequency by about 30–40 cm⁻¹ (to roughly 1685 cm⁻¹ for a conjugated ketone), and ring strain raises it. Aldehydes also show a distinctive pair of weak \(\text{C}-\text{H}\) stretching bands near 2720 and 2820 cm⁻¹ — a "fingerprint" ketones lack.

### \({}^1\text{H}\) NMR: the aldehyde proton

The proton bonded to the carbonyl carbon of an aldehyde appears far downfield, at about \(\delta\) 9–10 ppm — a region where almost no other protons resonate. The \(\alpha\)-protons (adjacent to the carbonyl) appear at \(\delta\) 2.1–2.5 ppm in both families, and the aldehyde proton couples to them with a small coupling constant (1–3 Hz), so it often appears as a triplet or doublet. A ketone shows no proton at \(\delta\) 9–10; its only telltale is the \(\alpha\)-proton signal.

### \(^{13}\text{C}\) NMR: the carbonyl carbon

The carbonyl carbon is the most downfield carbon in virtually any organic molecule, appearing at \(\delta\) 190–220 ppm. Aldehyde carbonyl carbons resonate near \(\delta\) 200 and ketones slightly further downfield, near \(\delta\) 205–210. For comparison, acids appear near \(\delta\) 178–180 and esters near 165–175, so the shift immediately distinguishes a ketone or aldehyde from its oxidized or derivatized relatives.

### Mass spectrometry: molecular ion and fragmentation

The molecular ion \(\text{M}^{+}\) gives the molecular mass and, with high-resolution mass spectrometry, the formula. Two fragmentation pathways dominate for ketones. In \(\alpha\)-cleavage, the bond between the carbonyl carbon and an adjacent carbon breaks, ejecting the largest alkyl radical and leaving an acylium ion \(\text{RCO}^{+}\). In the McLafferty rearrangement, a ketone with a hydrogen on a carbon three bonds away (a \(\gamma\)-hydrogen) transfers that hydrogen through a six-membered-ring transition state, giving an enol radical cation.

### Combining the evidence

No single spectrum is conclusive. A practical workflow: use IR to confirm the carbonyl, \({}^1\text{H}\) NMR to decide aldehyde versus ketone and count protons, \(^{13}\text{C}\) NMR to confirm the shift, and MS to fix the formula and support the skeleton. IR answers the functional-group question fastest, but \({}^1\text{H}\) NMR usually settles aldehyde versus ketone.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

> Every molecule has a fingerprint, just like people do. The carbonyl group leaves a strong mark in the infrared spectrum, and the aldehyde's "nose" hydrogen shouts from far downfield in the NMR spectrum. Collecting several kinds of spectra is like a detective gathering clues: each clue is weak alone, but together they identify the molecule.

## High-Yield Points

- IR: strong \(\text{C}=\text{O}\) stretch at 1715–1725 cm⁻¹; aldehydes near 1725, ketones near 1715; conjugation lowers it to about 1685 cm⁻¹.
- Aldehyde IR "doublet" at about 2720 and 2820 cm⁻¹ is unique to aldehydes.
- \({}^1\text{H}\) NMR: aldehyde proton at \(\delta\) 9–10 ppm (unique); \(\alpha\)-protons at \(\delta\) 2.1–2.5; ketones show no \(\delta\) 9–10 signal.
- \(^{13}\text{C}\) NMR: carbonyl carbon at \(\delta\) 190–220; aldehydes near 200, ketones near 205–210.
- MS: \(\alpha\)-cleavage gives an acylium ion \(\text{RCO}^{+}\); McLafferty rearrangement (requires a \(\gamma\)-hydrogen) gives an enol radical cation, e.g., m/z 58 for 2-hexanone.
- A broad IR \(\text{O}-\text{H}\) band at 2500–3300 cm⁻¹ rules out a simple aldehyde or ketone.
- Never rely on one spectrum alone: IR finds the carbonyl, NMR distinguishes aldehyde from ketone, MS gives the formula.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| wavenumber (cm⁻¹) | The unit of IR frequency, proportional to energy. | Lets you compare carbonyl stretches (1715–1725) with other bonds. |
| \(\text{C}=\text{O}\) stretch | The carbonyl vibration that absorbs IR light strongly. | The single most useful IR signal for identifying carbonyl compounds. |
| chemical shift (\(\delta\)) | Position of an NMR signal in ppm, reflecting the local magnetic environment. | The aldehyde proton's \(\delta\) 9–10 and the carbonyl carbon's \(\delta\) 190–220 are diagnostic. |
| \(\alpha\)-proton | A proton on the carbon directly attached to the carbonyl carbon. | Appears at \(\delta\) 2.1–2.5 ppm in both aldehydes and ketones. |
| \(\alpha\)-cleavage | MS fragmentation in which the bond next to the carbonyl breaks, giving \(\text{RCO}^{+}\). | Produces characteristic mass fragments used to identify ketones. |
| McLafferty rearrangement | MS rearrangement in which a \(\gamma\)-hydrogen transfers to the carbonyl oxygen, giving an enol radical cation. | Requires a \(\gamma\)-hydrogen; gives a hallmark fragment such as m/z 58 for 2-hexanone. |
| n→π\* transition | Low-energy electronic transition of the carbonyl oxygen lone pair into the \(\pi^*\) orbital. | Gives aldehydes and ketones a weak UV absorption near 270–290 nm. |

## Example 1: Propanal or Acetone?

An unknown has the formula \(\text{C}_3\text{H}_6\text{O}\). Its IR shows a strong band at 1725 cm⁻¹ and weak bands near 2720 and 2820 cm⁻¹. Its \({}^1\text{H}\) NMR shows a triplet (1H) at \(\delta\) 9.80, a multiplet (2H) at \(\delta\) 2.4, and a triplet (3H) at \(\delta\) 1.1.

First, the unsaturation count:

\[
\text{DU} = \frac{2C + 2 - H}{2} = \frac{2(3) + 2 - 6}{2} = 1
\]

One degree of unsaturation plus a strong 1725 cm⁻¹ band means a carbonyl, not a \(\text{C}=\text{C}\) bond. The 2720/2820 cm⁻¹ bands and the proton at \(\delta\) 9.80 identify an aldehyde, and the 1:2:3 integration matches an ethyl group next to the aldehyde carbon. The compound is propanal, \(\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CHO}\). Had it been acetone, the IR band would sit near 1715 cm⁻¹ and the NMR would show a 6H singlet near \(\delta\) 2.1 with no \(\delta\) 9–10 proton.

## Example 2: Three Isomeric C₆ Compounds by IR

Three bottles — cyclohexanone, cyclohexene, and cyclohexanol — have lost their labels; IR identifies each. Cyclohexanone shows a strong \(\text{C}=\text{O}\) band near 1715 cm⁻¹ and no \(\text{O}-\text{H}\) band. Cyclohexene shows a weak \(\text{C}=\text{C}\) stretch near 1650 cm⁻¹ (a nearly symmetrical double bond absorbs weakly) and no carbonyl band. Cyclohexanol shows a broad \(\text{O}-\text{H}\) band near 3300 cm⁻¹ plus a \(\text{C}-\text{O}\) band near 1060 cm⁻¹, and no carbonyl band. Three IR clues — carbonyl, alkene, hydroxyl — separate the isomers in seconds.

## Example 3: Identifying 2-Hexanone by Mass Spectrometry

A ketone gives a molecular ion at m/z 100 (formula \(\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}\)), a base peak at m/z 58, and a strong fragment at m/z 43. The m/z 58 fragment is the enol radical cation \(\text{CH}_3\text{C(OH)}=\text{CH}_2^{+\bullet}\) from the McLafferty rearrangement, which requires a \(\gamma\)-hydrogen — so the carbonyl sits near the end of the chain. The m/z 43 fragment is the acylium ion \(\text{CH}_3\text{CO}^{+}\). Together these identify 2-hexanone, \(\text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_3\); a 3-hexanone would give different fragments, showing how fragmentation reveals the carbonyl's position.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Aldehyde IR band (~1725 cm⁻¹) | Ketone IR band (~1715 cm⁻¹) | The difference is small; confirm with the aldehyde \(\text{C}-\text{H}\) bands (2720/2820 cm⁻¹) and the \(\delta\) 9–10 NMR proton. |
| \(\text{C}=\text{O}\) stretch (~1715 cm⁻¹, strong) | \(\text{C}=\text{C}\) stretch (~1650 cm⁻¹, weak) | Carbonyl bands are intense and higher in wavenumber. |
| ppm (NMR shift) | cm⁻¹ (IR wavenumber) | NMR shifts are relative (ppm); IR frequencies are absolute (cm⁻¹). |
| Aldehyde proton at \(\delta\) 9–10 | Acid proton at \(\delta\) 10–12 | The acid also shows a broad IR \(\text{O}-\text{H}\) band and a \(^{13}\text{C}\) shift near 180, not 200. |
| Conjugation lowering the C=O frequency | Conjugation raising it | Conjugation spreads electron density and lowers the stretch; ring strain raises it. |
