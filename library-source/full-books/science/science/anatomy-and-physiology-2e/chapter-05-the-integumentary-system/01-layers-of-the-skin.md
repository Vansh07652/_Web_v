# Layers of the Skin

> **Book:** [Anatomy and Physiology 2e](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 5: The Integumentary System](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 1  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

The skin is the body's largest organ — roughly 1.5 to 2 square meters in an average adult — built in three main layers. The outermost, the **epidermis**, is a waterproof, protective covering of keratinized stratified squamous epithelium. Beneath it, the **dermis** is the thick connective-tissue layer that gives skin strength and elasticity and houses its blood vessels, nerves, and glands. Below both lies the **hypodermis** (subcutaneous layer), a fatty connective-tissue layer anchoring skin to muscle and bone — technically not part of the skin proper, but inseparable from it in function.

The key is knowing what lives in each layer: the epidermis is *avascular* (nourished by diffusion from the dermis), the dermis is *vascular* and sensory-rich, and the hypodermis stores fat, insulates, and cushions.

## Why This Matters

The layers of the skin are the map for almost everything this chapter covers. **Burn severity is classified by depth**: superficial burns involve only the epidermis; partial-thickness burns reach the dermis; full-thickness burns destroy all layers into the hypodermis — deeper burns heal worse and scar more. **Skin cancers** arise in specific epidermal cells: basal cell carcinoma from the stratum basale, squamous cell carcinoma from the stratum spinosum, melanoma from melanocytes — the layers tell you where each starts.

Everyday procedures depend on layer anatomy too: **intradermal injections** (like TB skin tests) go in the dermis; **subcutaneous injections** (like insulin) go in the hypodermis; **tattoo ink** sits in the dermis, which is why tattoos are permanent — the epidermis sheds, the dermis doesn't. **Fingerprints** come from **dermal papillae**, the ridges where dermis meets epidermis. The layers also explain why the epidermis blocks infection and water loss, why the dermis holds touch, pain, and temperature receptors, and why pressure injuries develop when dermal blood supply is compromised. ## Core Concepts

### The epidermis: the waterproof shield

The epidermis is keratinized **stratified squamous epithelium** — many layers thick, with surface cells filled with **keratin**, a tough protein that makes skin waterproof and abrasion-resistant. It is avascular; nutrients diffuse up from the dermis. From deep to superficial, the strata of **thick skin** (palms and soles) are:

1. **Stratum basale** — deepest layer of dividing cells; keratinocytes are born here, melanocytes (pigment) and Merkel cells (touch) live here.
2. **Stratum spinosum** — maturing keratinocytes; Langerhans immune cells patrol here.
3. **Stratum granulosum** — cells fill with keratin and die; waterproofing begins.
4. **Stratum lucidum** — clear thin layer, thick skin only.
5. **Stratum corneum** — 15–30 layers of dead, flattened, keratin-filled cells forming the tough surface; most of the "skin" you see and shed is this layer.

**Thin skin** (most of the body) lacks the stratum lucidum and has a thinner stratum corneum. Cells take roughly 4–6 weeks to travel from basale to surface and be shed (a commonly-taught reference range).

### The dermis: strength, sensation, and blood supply

The dermis is dense irregular connective tissue rich in **collagen** (strength) and **elastic fibers** (stretch and recoil), with two layers:

- **Papillary layer** — thin and superficial, forming **dermal papillae**, finger-like projections into the epidermis; on fingertips they create fingerprints. Contains capillaries, touch receptors, and nerve endings.
- **Reticular layer** — thick and deep, with collagen and elastin networks, blood vessels, glands, hair follicles, and pressure receptors.

Here blood vessels nourish the avascular epidermis above, sensory receptors detect touch, pressure, pain, and temperature, and sweat and oil glands and hair follicles are rooted.

### The hypodermis: the shock absorber

The **hypodermis** (subcutaneous layer) lies below the dermis, made mostly of **adipose tissue** and loose connective tissue. It attaches skin to muscle and bone, insulates against heat loss, stores energy, cushions against shock, and carries the major vessels and nerves entering the skin. Because it is not part of the skin proper (sometimes called superficial fascia), it is the usual site for subcutaneous injections.

### Skin color: three pigments, one story

Skin color comes from **melanin** (made by melanocytes in the stratum basale; brown-black pigment that absorbs UV), **carotene** (a yellow-orange dietary pigment stored in the corneum and fat), and **hemoglobin** (red blood pigment in dermal capillaries, visible through the epidermis). Melanin increases with sun exposure, which is why skin tans. Differences in skin color reflect the amount and type of melanin, not melanocyte number.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Your skin is like a three-layer house. The **epidermis** is the roof — waterproof, takes all the weather, and made of tiles that wear out and get replaced from underneath. The **dermis** is the walls — strong, stretchy, full of pipes (blood vessels), wires (nerves), and workers (glands and hair roots). The **hypodermis** is the basement insulation — soft fat that keeps you warm and cushions bumps.

## High-Yield Points

- **Three layers:** epidermis (avascular epithelium), dermis (vascular connective tissue), hypodermis (fatty subcutaneous layer, not part of skin proper).
- **Epidermis = keratinized stratified squamous epithelium.** Keratin makes it waterproof and tough.
- **Strata, deep to superficial:** basale → spinosum → granulosum → lucidum (thick skin only) → corneum. Mnemonic: "Come Let's Get Sun Burned."
- **Only the stratum basale has dividing keratinocytes** — where new skin cells originate and melanocytes live.
- **Dermis has two layers:** papillary (dermal papillae → fingerprints; touch receptors, capillaries) and reticular (dense irregular CT, collagen + elastin, glands, follicles, pressure receptors).
- **The epidermis has no blood vessels** — it depends on diffusion from the dermis, which is why superficial scrapes don't bleed.
- **Skin color = melanin + carotene + hemoglobin.** Everyone has a similar number of melanocytes; differences are in melanin amount/type.
- **Tattoos are permanent because the ink sits in the dermis**, which does not shed like the epidermis.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| epidermis | Outermost avascular layer; keratinized stratified squamous epithelium | The waterproof barrier against infection, chemicals, and water loss |
| dermis | Middle layer; dense connective tissue with blood vessels, nerves, and glands | Gives strength and elasticity; nourishes the epidermis; houses receptors |
| hypodermis | Fatty subcutaneous layer below the dermis (not part of the skin proper) | Insulates, stores energy, cushions, and anchors skin to deeper structures |
| keratin | Tough structural protein produced in epidermal cells | Makes surface cells hard, waterproof, and abrasion-resistant |
| stratum basale | Deepest epidermal layer where keratinocytes divide | Source of all new epidermal cells; where melanocytes live |
| stratum corneum | Outermost layer of dead, keratin-filled cells | The tough surface you see and shed; main barrier |
| stratum lucidum | Clear thin layer present only in thick skin | The easy way to tell thick skin from thin |
| melanocyte | Pigment cell in the stratum basale | Produces melanin, which colors skin and absorbs UV |
| dermal papilla | Finger-like projection of the dermis into the epidermis | Creates fingerprints; increases contact surface for nourishment |
| keratinocyte | The main epidermal cell; produces keratin | Forms the bulk of the epidermis and its protective surface |
| subcutaneous injection | Injection delivered into the hypodermis | A routine route for medications like insulin |

## Example: Reading a Burn by Depth

A patient sustains a burn on the forearm. Using the layers as a map, trace what each depth means:

- **Superficial burn (epidermis only):** red, painful, dry skin, like a mild sunburn. Because the stratum basale survives, new keratinocytes replace the damaged surface within days — full regeneration, no scar.
- **Partial-thickness burn (into the dermis):** epidermis and part of the dermis destroyed. Blisters form; the wound is intensely painful because dermal nerve endings are exposed. Healing relies on regenerating epidermis from surviving basale cells — slower, with possible scarring.
- **Full-thickness burn (through dermis into hypodermis):** all layers destroyed. The wound may look white, waxy, or charred and is often *painless* because the nerve endings are destroyed — a dangerous sign, not a good one. Healing requires grafting, and scar tissue forms.

The same logic explains why a superficial scrape doesn't bleed (no vessels in the epidermis) but a scratch deep enough to reach the dermis does. (Burn classification here is the commonly-taught textbook framework; consult current burn-care guidelines for clinical practice.)

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Epidermis | Dermis | Epidermis is avascular epithelium (no bleeding when scraped); dermis is vascular connective tissue |
| Hypodermis | A layer of the skin | The hypodermis is technically subcutaneous — below the skin proper — but anchors and nourishes the skin |
| Stratum corneum | Stratum basale | Corneum is the dead, tough surface layer; basale is the living, dividing deep layer |
| Keratinocytes | Melanocytes | Keratinocytes make keratin (structure); melanocytes make melanin (pigment) |
| Thick skin | Thin skin | Thick skin (palms, soles) has a stratum lucidum and thicker corneum; thin skin covers the rest of the body |
| Papillary layer | Reticular layer | Papillary is thin and superficial (papillae, touch receptors); reticular is thick and deep (collagen, elastin, glands, pressure receptors) |
