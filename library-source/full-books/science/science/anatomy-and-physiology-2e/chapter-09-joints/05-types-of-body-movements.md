# Types of Body Movements

> **Book:** [Anatomy and Physiology 2e](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 9: Joints](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 5  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Anatomy needs a precise language for motion. This topic provides it: the standard terms for every synovial-joint movement, grouped into four families — **gliding**, **angular movements**, **rotation**, and **special movements**. Nearly all are defined relative to the **anatomical position** (standing upright, facing forward, arms at the sides, palms forward) — the fixed starting point that makes "flexion" mean the same thing to a therapist, nurse, and surgeon.

The angular movements — flexion and extension, abduction and adduction, circumduction — describe changes in the angle between bones; rotation turns a bone around its own long axis. The special movements — dorsiflexion, plantar flexion, inversion, eversion, protraction, retraction, elevation, depression, opposition — apply to particular regions like the foot, jaw, shoulders, and thumb. Because these words appear constantly in clinical documentation, mastering them is practical. Facts here are standard reference concepts — verify against your current text.

## Why This Matters

- **Clinical documentation depends on these words:** Nurses charting range of motion and therapists documenting "limited abduction" use this exact vocabulary.
- **Rehabilitation and exercise:** Rehab plans are written as sets of movements — flexion, extension, abduction, rotation — so the terms *are* the plan.
- **Exam gold:** Movement terms are among the most-tested anatomy concepts, especially the easy-to-swap pairs (abduction/adduction, pronation/supination).
- **Injury descriptions:** "I twisted my ankle" becomes meaningful when you can say the foot was **inverted** — the direction of most ankle sprains.

## Core Concepts

### Start from anatomical position

Every movement term is defined from the **anatomical position**: body upright, feet slightly apart, arms at the sides, palms forward. "Flexion" decreases the angle from that position; "extension" returns to it or increases it beyond. The palms-forward convention is why pronation and supination depend on palm direction — without the shared starting point, one person's "bending the arm" could be another's "straightening the arm."

### Gliding

**Gliding** is the simplest movement: flat or nearly flat bone surfaces slide past one another with no significant angle change. It happens at **plane (gliding) joints** — between the small bones of the wrist and ankle and between vertebral articular processes. Each glide is small, but many small joints moving together add up, as when the wrist arches.

### Angular movements: changing the angle between bones

**Flexion** decreases the angle between two bones (bending the elbow, curling the knee, bending the head forward); **extension** increases it, straightening toward anatomical position. **Hyperextension** carries extension past anatomical position — tilting the head back or bending the wrist backward. **Lateral flexion** is the vertebral column's angular movement: bending the trunk sideways, ear toward shoulder.

**Abduction** moves a body part *away* from the midline (lifting the arm out, spreading the fingers); **adduction** moves it *toward* the midline. For fingers and toes, the reference is the middle digit: fingers abduct by spreading away from it.

**Circumduction** is the distal end of a limb moving in a circle while the proximal end stays fixed — the "windmill" motion of the arm, sweeping a cone-shaped space. It is not a single new movement but a sequence: flexion, abduction, extension, and adduction performed one after another.

### Rotation: turning around a long axis

**Rotation** is movement of a bone around its own longitudinal axis. **Medial (internal) rotation** turns the anterior surface of the limb toward the midline; **lateral (external) rotation** turns it away. Examples: turning the head (atlantoaxial joint) and rotating the arm at the shoulder.

Two rotation terms belong to the forearm. **Pronation** rotates the radius across the ulna so the palm faces down or back; **supination** returns the radius to parallel so the palm faces up or forward — think of carrying a bowl of soup.

### Special movements: terms for specific regions

- **Dorsiflexion** lifts the toes up toward the shin; **plantar flexion** points them down (tiptoe, pressing a pedal).
- **Inversion** turns the sole inward; **eversion** turns it outward — the usual ankle-sprain direction is inversion.
- **Protraction** moves a part forward (thrusting the jaw out, rounding the shoulders forward); **retraction** pulls it back.
- **Elevation** moves a part upward (shrugging the shoulders, closing the jaw); **depression** moves it downward.
- **Opposition** is the thumb touching the tip of another finger — the grasp movement, unique to the saddle joint of the thumb; **reposition** returns the thumb.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Imagine your body in a "robot start position" — straight up, arms out at your sides, palms forward. Every movement has a name from that spot: bending your elbow is flexion, straightening it is extension; moving your arm away is abduction, bringing it back is adduction; spinning your arm like a windmill is circumduction. Your forearm can do a magic trick too — pronation rolls the radius over the ulna so your palm faces down, and supination rolls it back so your palm faces up, like holding soup.

## High-Yield Points

- **All terms are defined from anatomical position** — that is why pronation/supination depend on palm direction.
- **Flexion/extension/hyperextension** = decreasing, increasing, or overshooting the angle between bones.
- **Abduction = away from the midline; adduction = toward it** — for fingers and toes, the reference is the middle digit.
- **Circumduction = flexion + abduction + extension + adduction in sequence**, the distal limb tracing a cone.
- **Rotation is around the bone's own long axis**; medial = toward midline, lateral = away.
- **Pronation = palm down/back; supination = palm up/front** — the radius crosses the ulna during pronation.
- **Foot/ankle pairs:** dorsiflexion (toes up) vs. plantar flexion (toes down); inversion (sole in) vs. eversion (sole out) — inversion is the classic ankle-sprain direction; **opposition** is the thumb touching the fingertips at the saddle joint.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-language definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Anatomical position | Reference posture: upright, arms at sides, palms forward | The fixed starting point for every movement term |
| Flexion / extension | Decreasing / increasing the angle between bones | The most common movement pair; bending vs. straightening |
| Abduction / adduction | Away from / toward the body's midline | Classic exam pair; describes arm, leg, finger, and toe movements |
| Circumduction | Distal limb tracing a circle, proximal end fixed | Combines four angular movements; e.g., windmill arm swing |
| Rotation | Bone turning around its own long axis | Head turning, arm twisting; medial vs. lateral direction matters |
| Pronation / supination | Forearm rotation: palm down/back vs. up/front | The radius crosses the ulna in pronation; palm direction is the test |
| Dorsiflexion / plantar flexion | Lifting the toes up / pointing them down | Everyday actions — walking on heels vs. standing on tiptoe |
| Inversion / eversion | Turning the sole inward / outward | Inversion is the most common ankle-sprain direction |
| Opposition | Thumb touches the fingertips | The grasp movement unique to the thumb's saddle joint |

## Example: Documenting One Shoulder, Then One Ankle

A clinician must document, in precise movement terms, an exam of a person injured in a fall.

**Shoulder.** The person raises the arm out to the side to shoulder level: **abduction**; lowering it back: **adduction**. With the elbow bent, the person swings the forearm across the body — **medial (internal) rotation** — and out to the side — **lateral (external) rotation**. Finally the examiner asks for a big circle with the straightened arm: **circumduction** — flexion, abduction, extension, adduction in sequence. The documentation reads: "Shoulder abduction to 90°, full adduction, rotation intact, circumduction complete."

**Ankle.** The person pulls the toes up — **dorsiflexion** — then pushes them down like pressing a pedal — **plantar flexion**. The examiner turns the sole inward — **inversion** — and outward — **eversion**; the person winces at inversion, which makes sense: the foot was forced into inversion in the fall, the usual ankle-sprain direction. The documentation reads: "Dorsiflexion and plantar flexion full; inversion painful and limited; eversion intact."

In one paragraph, the examiner has communicated both joints' functional status.

## Common Confusions

| Do not confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Flexion | Extension | Flexion decreases the angle; extension increases it — bending vs. straightening |
| Abduction | Adduction | Abduction moves *away* from the midline; adduction moves *toward* it — "addition" brings parts back |
| Pronation | Supination | Pronation turns the palm down/back (radius crosses ulna); supination turns it up/front — the "soup bowl" test |
| Dorsiflexion | Plantar flexion | Dorsiflexion lifts the toes toward the shin; plantar flexion points them down — heels vs. tiptoe |
| Inversion | Eversion | Inversion turns the sole inward; eversion outward — sprains usually invert |
| Rotation | Circumduction | Rotation spins a bone on its own axis (head turning); circumduction swings the limb in a cone (arm circles) |
| Hyperextension | Extension | Hyperextension continues *past* anatomical position — e.g., tilting the head back beyond neutral |
| Medial rotation | Lateral rotation | Medial turns the anterior surface toward the midline; lateral turns it away |
