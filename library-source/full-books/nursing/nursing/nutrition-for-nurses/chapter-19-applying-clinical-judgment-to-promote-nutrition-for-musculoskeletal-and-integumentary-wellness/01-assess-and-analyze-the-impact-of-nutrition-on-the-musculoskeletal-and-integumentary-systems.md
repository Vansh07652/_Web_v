# Assess and Analyze the Impact of Nutrition on the Musculoskeletal and Integumentary Systems

> **Book:** [Nutrition for Nurses](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 19: Applying Clinical Judgment to Promote Nutrition for Musculoskeletal and Integumentary Wellness](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 1  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

The body's framework and its covering tell a story about nutrition. Bones, muscles, joints, skin, hair, and nails are built and repaired from the nutrients a person eats, and when intake falls short, these systems are often the first to show it. **Assessment** is the step where the nurse gathers data about a patient's nutrition and these systems; **analysis** is the step where the nurse connects the dots — recognizing, for example, that a patient with a slowly healing wound also reports eating very little protein, or that an older adult who has lost weight and strength is at higher risk for falls and fractures. This topic is about how the nurse looks, asks, and reasons: what to assess (diet history, physical examination, functional measures, and team-ordered labs), what the findings suggest in general terms, and when to escalate to the provider and registered dietitian (RD). It is an educational overview of assessment concepts — not a set of treatment instructions, and not a substitute for the individualized evaluation the care team performs.

## Why This Matters

Musculoskeletal and integumentary problems are among the most common and costly reasons for hospitalization and disability: falls and fractures in older adults, pressure injuries in people with limited mobility, and wounds that will not heal after surgery. Nutrition is a modifiable piece of all of these. A nurse who can assess protein and energy intake, recognize muscle loss and poor skin condition, and connect those findings to risk is practicing prevention, not just reaction. Malnutrition is often under-recognized, and it is strongly associated with worse outcomes — slower healing, more infections, longer stays. Assessment is also squarely within nursing scope: taking a diet history, measuring weight, examining the skin, and observing function are everyday nursing activities, while diagnosis and diet prescription belong to the provider and RD. On exams, questions about musculoskeletal and integumentary nutrition reward knowing which assessment findings point to which nutrient themes.

## Core Concepts

### Nutrient themes for bone health

Bone is living tissue that is constantly remodeled — old bone is broken down and new bone is built, and the balance shifts with age. Nutrition supports this process through several general themes. **Calcium** is a primary building block of bone mineral; **vitamin D** supports the body's ability to absorb and use calcium; **protein** contributes to the bone matrix; and other nutrients play supporting roles. Intake across the whole lifespan matters because peak bone mass is built early in life and bone is gradually lost later, especially in people after menopause. Assessment for bone health includes questions about dairy and calcium-rich food intake, time spent outdoors and vitamin D sources, and risk factors such as limited mobility, low body weight, and prior fractures. The nurse does not prescribe calcium or vitamin D — supplementation decisions are made by the provider based on the full picture, including labs. The analysis concept: a person with several bone-health risk factors plus poor dietary intake warrants flagging for evaluation and education, and possibly a bone-density workup by the provider.

### Nutrient themes for muscle

Muscle mass and strength depend on **protein**, **energy (calories)**, and **physical activity** working together — nutrients alone do not build muscle without the stimulus of movement. **Sarcopenia** is the term for age-related loss of muscle mass and strength, and it matters enormously because it drives falls, fractures, and loss of independence. Assessment includes questions about protein-rich foods (meat, fish, eggs, dairy, legumes), appetite and intake, weight trends, and function: how the patient rises from a chair, walks, carries groceries, or grips objects. The nurse can observe muscle wasting in the hands, shoulders, and thighs and note difficulty with everyday tasks. The analysis concept: unintentional weight loss in an older adult is not "normal aging" — when it includes lost muscle, it is a red flag that deserves a full nutrition assessment by the RD and a provider workup.

### Nutrient themes for skin, hair, and nails

The integumentary system — skin, hair, and nails — is built and repaired continuously, and it is the most visible mirror of nutrition. **Protein** is the structural basis of skin and collagen; **vitamin C** supports collagen formation and wound repair; **zinc** participates in cell division and healing; and **adequate fluids** keep skin and tissues hydrated. Assessment is largely visual and tactile: skin color, turgor, moisture, texture, and breakdown; wounds and their healing trajectory; hair that is thinning, dull, or brittle; nails that are ridged, brittle, or slow-growing. None of these findings alone proves a deficiency — but a pattern of poor healing plus poor intake is exactly the kind of connection analysis should surface. Wound healing is a major nursing focus: nutrition assessment is part of pressure injury prevention and wound care, and people with pressure injuries or slow-healing wounds are typically referred for individualized nutrition planning with the RD and wound care team.

### Where the assessment data come from

A thorough nutrition-focused assessment of these systems combines several sources:

- **Diet history and 24-hour recall:** what the person typically eats, portion sizes, appetite changes, chewing or swallowing problems, food access and preferences.
- **Weight and body measures:** current weight, weight trends over time, and — when performed by trained staff per facility policy — anthropometric measures such as height and weight-based indices; these are screening tools, not diagnoses.
- **Physical examination:** skin, hair, nails, oral health, muscle wasting, edema, and wounds, using the nurse's observational skills.
- **Functional assessment:** mobility, gait, balance, grip, and ability to perform daily activities — often with standardized tools chosen by the facility.
- **Laboratory values:** ordered and interpreted by the provider; the nurse gathers the data, knows what the team is tracking, and reports trends.

### Analyzing: connecting findings into a picture

Analysis is pattern recognition with judgment. The nurse clusters findings and asks: does the patient's intake support the demands on their body? A person with a healing surgical wound has increased needs for protein and energy — if intake is poor, healing will lag. A person who has lost weight and strength is at risk regardless of the reason — the risk is the finding. Malnutrition risk screening tools are used in many facilities to standardize this judgment; a positive screen triggers a full assessment by the RD. Importantly, analysis stays within the nurse's scope: the nurse identifies risk and patterns and communicates them; diagnosis and treatment planning belong to the team.

### Scope, language, and referral

Assessment and analysis are core nursing work, but they have boundaries: nurses screen, observe, and educate; dietitians perform comprehensive nutrition assessment and prescribe MNT; providers diagnose and order tests. Scope varies by institution and region, so the nurse follows facility policy. Person-first language is essential — "a person with a pressure injury," "an older adult with sarcopenia risk," not labels that reduce the person to a condition. Referral is a sign of good analysis, not failure: any patient with poor intake plus slow healing, weight loss, or new functional decline should be connected with the RD, provider, and — when mobility is the issue — physical or occupational therapy.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Your bones, muscles, and skin are like a house that is always being fixed up — they need building materials from food, like bricks (protein) and cement (calcium). If the materials run low, the house starts to look tired: bones get weak, muscles get small, and cuts heal slowly. A nurse looks and asks questions to find out if the "house" is getting enough materials, then tells the food expert so the plan can be fixed.

## High-Yield Points

- **Bone health themes:** calcium, vitamin D, and protein support bone remodeling across the lifespan; assessment includes calcium-rich intake, vitamin D sources, and risk factors.
- **Muscle needs protein, energy, AND activity** — sarcopenia (age-related muscle loss) drives falls and fractures and is a major assessment target.
- **Skin, hair, and nails mirror nutrition:** protein, vitamin C, zinc, and hydration support repair; slow wound healing plus poor intake is a pattern to connect, not ignore.
- **Assessment sources:** diet history, weight trends, physical exam, functional status, and team-ordered labs — screening tools are not diagnoses.
- **Unintentional weight loss with strength loss in an older adult is a red flag**, not normal aging — refer for full assessment.
- **Nurses screen, observe, and educate; the RD and provider diagnose and prescribe** — scope varies by institution.
- **Person-first language:** "a person with a pressure injury," never a label.
- **Poor intake + slow healing or decline = referral**, to the RD and provider, with therapy when mobility is affected.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Sarcopenia | Age-related loss of muscle mass and strength | A key driver of falls, fractures, and loss of independence |
| Remodeling | The ongoing process of breaking down and rebuilding bone | Explains why lifelong calcium, vitamin D, and protein intake matter |
| Collagen | The structural protein that gives skin and connective tissue strength | The reason protein and vitamin C are tied to wound healing |
| Pressure injury | Skin or tissue damage from prolonged pressure, often over bony areas | Nutrition status influences risk and healing; prevention is a nursing priority |
| Diet history | Information about what, how much, and how often a person eats | The starting point for linking intake to physical findings |
| Malnutrition risk screening | A standardized check for risk factors, done per facility protocol | A positive screen triggers full assessment by the registered dietitian |

## Clinical-Reasoning Example: The Fall That Almost Happened Twice

A nurse admits an 80-year-old person who fell at home. The physical exam shows thin, fragile skin, a small non-healing wound on the forearm from a previous fall, and visible muscle wasting in the hands and thighs. The diet history reveals the person "doesn't cook much anymore," eats mostly toast and tea, and has lost weight over the past several months without trying. The nurse clusters these findings: low protein and energy intake, muscle loss, weight loss, poor skin condition, and a wound that is not healing — a pattern that raises the risk of another fall, a fracture, or a pressure injury during the hospital stay. The nurse does not diagnose malnutrition but flags the risk, requests a nutrition consult from the RD, communicates the fall risk to the care team for mobility precautions, and begins gentle education about protein-rich foods and community meal resources. Analysis turned a routine admission into a prevention plan — and the nurse's documentation gives the whole team a shared picture.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Sarcopenia | Simple weight loss | Weight loss can be fat, muscle, or fluid; sarcopenia is specifically muscle mass and strength loss |
| "A calcium supplement fixes bone loss" | Multifactorial bone health | Bone health also involves vitamin D, protein, activity, hormones, and medications — supplements are one piece, decided by the provider |
| "Slow wound healing is just part of getting older" | A nutrition-related pattern worth evaluating | Poor healing plus poor intake is a signal, not an inevitability |
| "More vitamin C speeds healing dramatically" | Adequate vitamin C supports healing | Extra beyond adequacy does not multiply the effect; nutrient adequacy, not megadosing, is the concept |
| "Skin breakdown is unavoidable in bed" | A preventable, nutrition-influenced risk | Pressure injury risk is managed with positioning, skin care, and nutrition support |
| Malnutrition risk screening | A malnutrition diagnosis | Screening flags risk; the RD and provider make the diagnosis |
