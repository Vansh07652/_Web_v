# Physical Assessment of Digestive Organs

> **Book:** [Nutrition for Nurses](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 4: The Digestive Process](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 2  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Physical assessment of the digestive organs is the systematic collection of data about how a person's GI system is working — what they tell you (history) and what you observe and feel (exam). It is a nursing skill: gathering and documenting accurate information and reporting findings, not diagnosing — interpreting abnormal findings and directing treatment are the provider's job, with the registered dietitian (RD) managing the nutrition plan.

The abdominal exam follows a fixed order: **inspection, then auscultation, then percussion, then palpation** — "look, listen, then feel." Listening must come before percussion and palpation because touching the abdomen can change bowel sounds; the order is also a reliable test question. A full picture goes beyond the abdomen: the mouth (chewing, swallowing, hydration), weight trends, and a careful history of eating patterns, bowel habits, and medications — including over-the-counter products and supplements people forget to mention.

## Why This Matters

- **Early detection:** subtle changes — new tenderness, changed bowel sounds, distention, or weight loss — are often noticed first by the nurse; a careful first assessment makes later changes interpretable.
- **Patient safety:** recognizing and promptly reporting red-flag cues (severe pain, vomiting blood, black or tarry stool) can change a person's outcome.
- **Nutrition link:** digestion and nutrition are inseparable; the GI assessment feeds directly into nutrition screening and the RD's work.
- **Exam relevance:** the order of the abdominal exam, quadrant locations, and normal vs. abnormal findings are classic nursing and NCLEX-style items.

## Core Concepts

### History first: what the person tells you

Assessment begins with questions, not hands. A focused GI history covers the reason for seeking care and any pain (location, quality, timing, aggravating and relieving factors); bowel habits (frequency, consistency, color, blood, straining); nausea, vomiting, and appetite; weight changes, especially unintentional ones; medications, supplements, and herbs; previous surgeries; and chronic conditions. A **24-hour dietary recall** gives an intake snapshot. Certain symptoms are urgent cues to report immediately: vomiting blood, black or tarry stool, bright red blood from the rectum, severe or worsening pain, or a rigid abdomen.

### Preparation: privacy, positioning, and consent

Before touching anything, explain what you will do and why, obtain consent, provide privacy, and have the person empty their bladder (a full bladder distorts the abdomen). Position the person supine with knees slightly bent to relax the abdominal muscles, warm your hands, and use person-first language: you are assessing *a person who has* abdominal discomfort, not "a belly." Drape for dignity and narrate every step.

### Inspection: look before you listen

Inspection begins the moment you enter: observe contour (flat, rounded, distended, or sunken/scaphoid), skin (color, scars, striae, lesions), umbilicus, visible pulsations, peristalsis, or masses, and any stoma or drainage device; ask the person to take a breath or cough and watch for bulges suggesting a hernia.

### Auscultation: listen before you touch

Because percussion and palpation can alter bowel sounds, auscultation comes second: listen in all four quadrants with the diaphragm, at least a minute per area if sounds are quiet. Bowel sounds are described qualitatively — normal, hyperactive (loud, frequent, rushing; audible without a stethoscope as "borborygmi"), hypoactive, or absent — and the provider interprets them in context. Also listen with the bell for vascular sounds (bruits) over the aorta and renal arteries.

### Percussion and palpation: tap and feel, tender areas last

Percussion — tapping the abdomen and interpreting the note — maps what is underneath: **tympany** (drum-like) over gas-filled bowel is normal; **dullness** suggests fluid or solid structures such as the liver. Palpation proceeds light first, then deep, systematically through the quadrants. Guarding (involuntary muscle tension), rigidity, masses, and tenderness are documented objectively: where, how tender, what the person did or said. The tenderest area is palpated last so protective reactions do not mask findings elsewhere. Deep palpation and specialized maneuvers fall within the provider's examination; the nurse palpates within scope and policy.

### Beyond the abdomen: mouth, swallowing, and nutrition measures

The GI story continues above the abdomen: inspect the lips, gums, teeth (or dentures), mucosa, and tongue — poor dentition or dry mucosa affects chewing, swallowing, and intake. Note difficulty chewing or swallowing, coughing with meals, or food pocketing, and report swallowing concerns promptly. Weight trends (including unintentional change) and hydration cues round out the picture; laboratory values, if ordered, are interpreted by the provider.

### Documentation and scope

Document what you actually observed — objective descriptions ("abdomen soft, mildly distended; bowel sounds hyperactive in all four quadrants; tenderness reported in right lower quadrant on light palpation"), the person's own words, and what was reported to whom. Never document a diagnosis. Scope varies by state, facility, and setting — what an RN, LPN/LVN, or advanced practice nurse may do differs — so nurses follow their own scope and institutional policy. When in doubt, ask.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Checking the tummy is like being a detective who looks, listens, and only then touches — because touching first would mess up the clues you need to hear. First you look and ask questions; then you listen with a stethoscope to the food factory's sounds; then you tap to find which parts are full of air and which are solid; and only at the very end do you gently press, saving the sore spot for last. The detective's job is to write down every clue exactly as found and tell the doctor — not to decide what the crime is — while explaining everything so the person isn't scared.

## High-Yield Points

- **Order of the abdominal exam: inspection → auscultation → percussion → palpation** — auscultate before percussion/palpation because touching changes bowel sounds.
- **History comes first:** pain, bowel habits, diet recall, medications and supplements, surgeries, weight changes; urgent cues (blood in vomit or stool, severe pain, rigid abdomen) are reported immediately.
- **Auscultation:** listen in all four quadrants; describe sounds qualitatively (normal, hyperactive, hypoactive, absent); borborygmi are loud, rumbling, hyperactive sounds.
- **Percussion:** tympany over gas-filled bowel, dullness over fluid/solid structures.
- **Assessment is data collection, not diagnosis;** scope varies by role, state, and facility — nurses act within their own scope and follow institutional policy.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Inspection | Visual examination of the abdomen and surrounding area | The first step; catches contour, skin, and stoma findings |
| Auscultation | Listening with a stethoscope (bowel sounds, bruits) | Must precede percussion/palpation to avoid altering sounds |
| Percussion | Tapping the abdomen and interpreting the sound | Distinguishes gas (tympany) from fluid/solid (dullness) |
| Palpation | Gentle pressing to assess tenderness, guarding, and masses | Light before deep; tender area last |
| Bowel sounds | Sounds of gas and fluid moving through the intestine | Described qualitatively; provider interprets significance |
| Guarding | Involuntary tensing of abdominal muscles | A protective response; distinguishes true tenderness from anxiety |

## Example: An Admission Assessment With a Twist

A person is admitted with abdominal discomfort. The nurse:

1. **History:** "Tell me about the discomfort — where is it, when did it start, what makes it better or worse?" The person points to the right lower quadrant. The nurse asks about bowel habits, appetite, last meal, medications, and supplements; nothing is urgent, but the nurse notes the person forgot to mention an herbal product until asked directly.
2. **Preparation:** explain the exam, obtain consent, provide privacy, position supine with knees bent.
3. **Inspection:** abdomen slightly rounded, no distention, a healed scar in the right lower quadrant.
4. **Auscultation:** active bowel sounds in all four quadrants — documented, not interpreted.
5. **Percussion:** tympany throughout, expected dullness over the liver.
6. **Palpation:** light, then deep, saving the right lower quadrant for last; mild tenderness there, no guarding.
7. **Wrap-up:** document objectively — "abdomen soft, mildly tender in RLQ on deep palpation; bowel sounds active ×4 quadrants; no guarding; supplements include [name]" — and report to the provider, pharmacist, and RD.

The nurse never concludes "the appendix is inflamed" — the data is passed up the team, exactly how the system is supposed to work.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Auscultating after palpation | Auscultating before palpation | Percussion/palpation can change bowel sounds; listen first — "look, listen, then feel" |
| Tympany | Dullness | Tympany = gas-filled (normal over most of the abdomen); dullness = fluid/solid (expected over the liver) |
| Guarding | Voluntary tensing | Guarding is involuntary protection; voluntary tensing stops when the person relaxes with explanation |
