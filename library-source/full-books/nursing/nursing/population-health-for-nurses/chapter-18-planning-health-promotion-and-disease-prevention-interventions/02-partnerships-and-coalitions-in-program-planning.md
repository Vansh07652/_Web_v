# Partnerships and Coalitions in Program Planning

> **Book:** [Population Health for Nurses](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 18: Planning Health Promotion and Disease Prevention Interventions](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 2  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

No single organization — not a hospital, not a clinic, not a health department — can improve the health of a whole community by itself. The conditions that shape health (housing, food access, schools, jobs, recreation) sit outside any one institution's walls. That is why **partnerships and coalitions** are the delivery engine of population health programs: they are how organizations and community members combine resources, reach, and credibility around a shared goal.

A **partnership** is a working relationship between two or more parties pursuing shared goals with defined roles and mutual benefit. A **coalition** is a more formalized alliance — typically many organizations across sectors — formed around a specific issue, with structures such as a steering committee, bylaws, or a written agreement. In program planning, partnerships and coalitions answer "who else must be at the table for this program to work?"

## Why This Matters

- **Programs fail without buy-in.** An intervention no community organization trusts, promotes, or sustains dies when grant funding ends.
- **Health is multi-sector.** A diabetes program that ignores food access, a vaccination campaign that ignores schools, and an asthma program that ignores housing all miss the levers that move community health.
- **Funders and agencies expect collaboration.** Grants, health department plans, and accreditation frameworks routinely require documented partnerships.
- **Partnerships build trust.** People are more likely to use services offered through organizations they already know and trust.
- **Exam payoff.** Expect scenario questions distinguishing partnership types, identifying stakeholders, and placing the nurse's role in collaborative work.

## Core Concepts

### A spectrum of collaboration

Collaboration ranges from loose to tight. **Networking** means exchanging information; **coordination** aligns activities and referrals; **cooperation** shares resources for separate goals; **collaboration** jointly plans and delivers shared work; and **partnership/coalition** is the most formal end — shared goals, written agreements, shared decision-making, and mutual accountability. A clinic that faxes referrals to a food bank has a referral relationship, not a partnership; they become partners when they jointly design a referral pathway and share data to improve it. Knowing where a relationship sits on the spectrum keeps planners from over- or under-formalizing it.

### Who belongs at the table

Population health programs typically involve several sectors:

- **Community members and people with lived experience** — the priority population itself. They are partners, not audiences; programs planned without them miss barriers and cultural realities.
- **Community-based organizations** — local nonprofits, faith communities, food pantries, mutual-aid groups with existing trust and reach.
- **Schools and early-childhood programs** — for child and family health.
- **Health care organizations** — clinics, hospitals, community health centers, pharmacies.
- **Public health and government** — health departments, housing authorities, parks and recreation.
- **Businesses and employers** — worksites, grocery stores, landlords.
- **Academic and media partners** — evaluation support and communication reach.

Include the people and organizations whose decisions shape the issue the program targets, plus those who will help deliver and sustain it.

### How coalitions grow

Coalitions typically move through recognizable stages (labels vary by model, but the logic is consistent):

1. **Formation** — someone identifies the issue, recruits partners, and builds a shared understanding of the problem.
2. **Capacity building** — the group defines mission, membership, decision-making rules, leadership, and a written **memorandum of understanding (MOU)** stating each partner's roles and contributions.
3. **Action** — the coalition plans and implements activities and mobilizes resources.
4. **Maintenance and institutionalization** — funding diversifies and activities are absorbed into partner agencies' routine operations.
5. **Renewal or ending** — the coalition reassesses whether to continue, refocus, or dissolve gracefully.

### What makes partnerships succeed or fail

Success factors recur across settings: a **shared vision**, **mutual benefit** (each partner gets something real), **trust** built through consistent communication, **clear roles**, and **shared decision-making**. Failure factors are the mirror image: unequal power (one funder or agency controls decisions), competing priorities never aired, unclear roles, and **tokenism** — inviting community members to "represent" their community without real influence. Time and unstable funding also drain coalitions; they require ongoing investment.

### The nurse's role

Community health nurses often act as **convener** (bringing people together), **facilitator** (keeping meetings productive and inclusive), **liaison** (translating between clinical, community, and policy worlds), **content expert** (interpreting data and evidence), and **advocate** (amplifying community voices). Two cautions: collaborating does not expand a license — clinical decisions remain governed by license, employer policy, and jurisdiction — and the nurse serves the partnership rather than controlling it. Data-sharing, contracting, and conflict-of-interest rules should be reviewed with the employer before any agreement is signed.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

A coalition is like a neighborhood team where the school, the clinic, the grocery store, and the church agree to play together to make the neighborhood healthier. Each one can only do a little by itself — but when they share a plan and each does their part, they can change something big, like making sure everyone can get fresh food or learn about a health issue.

## High-Yield Points

- **Partnerships and coalitions are the "who" of program delivery** — they combine resources, reach, trust, and credibility no single agency has.
- **Coalitions are formalized, multi-organization alliances** around a specific issue; partnerships can be simpler and bilateral.
- **Community members with lived experience are partners, not just audiences** — tokenism (presence without influence) is a recognized failure mode.
- **Success factors:** shared vision, mutual benefit, trust, clear roles, shared decision-making, consistent communication.
- **An MOU documents each partner's roles, contributions, and expectations** — preventing role confusion and satisfying funders.
- **Coalitions develop through stages** — formation, capacity building, action, maintenance/institutionalization, renewal or ending.
- **The nurse's collaborative roles** — convener, facilitator, liaison, content expert, advocate — stay inside license, employer policy, and jurisdiction.
- **Sustainability is planned from the start** — diversified funding and institutionalization into routine operations.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-language definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Partnership | A working relationship between two or more parties pursuing shared goals with defined roles | The basic unit of collaborative program delivery |
| Coalition | A formalized, multi-sector alliance around a specific issue | Lets many partners act as one coordinated force |
| Stakeholder | Anyone with an interest in or influence over a program, including those affected | Full stakeholder identification prevents blind spots |
| Memorandum of understanding (MOU) | Written agreement stating each partner's roles, contributions, expectations | Prevents role confusion; documents commitment for funders |
| Lived experience | Firsthand knowledge of the issue or conditions a program addresses | The irreplaceable expertise community partners bring |
| Community-based organization (CBO) | A nonprofit or grassroots group rooted in and trusted by a local community | Often the most trusted bridge to residents |
| Tokenism | Including community members symbolically without real decision-making power | Erodes trust and skews plans |
| Sustainability | A program's capacity to keep operating after initial funding or champions end | The reason partnerships and institutionalization are planned from day one |

## Example: A coalition to address type 2 diabetes

A public health nurse notices that diabetes-related admissions concentrate in one neighborhood where few residents use the county clinic and grocery options are limited. Rather than launching a clinic-only class, she starts by listening: residents tell her transportation and food access are the real barriers, and name the organizations they trust.

She convenes a first meeting of likely partners — the community center, a faith organization, a small grocery store, the school district, the clinic's dietitian, and three residents with diabetes. The group drafts an MOU: the clinic provides the dietitian's time; the grocery store offers produce discounts for participants; the faith organization hosts classes; the school district lends its kitchen for a cooking series; residents co-facilitate sessions. A steering committee of five, including two residents, makes decisions — no single agency controls the agenda.

Within the first year the coalition runs a monthly cooking-and-education series and a referral pathway from the clinic. The nurse documents participation and referrals, and the coalition reviews progress quarterly. Her role stays within scope: she facilitates and coordinates, the dietitian delivers clinical nutrition education, and residents lead what needs community knowledge. When grant funding ends, the partners choose which activities to continue inside their own budgets — the test of institutionalization. (This scenario illustrates the planning process; it is not a model of any specific program.)

## Common Confusions

| Do not confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| A partnership | A referral relationship | Referrals are coordination; partners jointly plan, share resources, and share accountability |
| A coalition | A committee inside one agency | A coalition is a formal alliance of *independent* organizations and community members |
| Participation | Partnership | Attending or being consulted is participation; partnership means shared decision-making |
| A partnership | A merger | Partners stay separate organizations; a merger combines them into one |
| The nurse convening | The nurse controlling | The nurse facilitates; the community and partners own the program |
| Grant-funded collaboration | A sustainable coalition | Funded projects can dissolve when money ends; sustainability requires diversified support |
