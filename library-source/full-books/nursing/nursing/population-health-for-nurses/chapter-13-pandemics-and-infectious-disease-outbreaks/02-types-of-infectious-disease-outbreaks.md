# Types of Infectious Disease Outbreaks

> **Book:** [Population Health for Nurses](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 13: Pandemics and Infectious Disease Outbreaks](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 2  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

When cases of a disease suddenly rise above the expected level in a community, public health calls it an **outbreak** — but not all outbreaks behave the same way. Understanding the *type* is the first step in stopping it: the type tells investigators where the pathogen came from and how it is moving.

Outbreaks fall into two broad families. **Common-source outbreaks** arise when many people are exposed to the same contaminated source, such as a food item, a water supply, or a piece of equipment — everyone who touches the source can get sick. **Propagated (or progressive) outbreaks** arise when a pathogen spreads directly from person to person, so each infected person can become a source for the next round of cases. Many real-world outbreaks are **mixed**, starting with a common source and then spreading person to person. This topic explains these categories, the transmission modes behind them, and how nurses read the "shape" of an outbreak to guide the response.

## Why This Matters

The type of outbreak determines the intervention. If cases come from a common source, the priority is to find and remove it — recall the food, fix the water, clean the equipment. If cases are propagating person to person, the priority is interrupting contact — isolation, quarantine, masking, and vaccination. Choosing the wrong intervention wastes time and lets the outbreak grow. Nurses are often the first to notice unusual clusters, document what investigators need, report to public health authorities, and educate patients and communities about how a disease actually spreads — replacing rumor with evidence.

## Core Concepts

### The outbreak vocabulary

Public health uses precise terms for disease patterns. **Sporadic** cases occur rarely, without a pattern. **Endemic** disease is constantly present at expected levels (seasonal influenza is endemic). **Epidemic** is a rise above expected levels; **outbreak** is often used interchangeably but usually describes a more localized rise; **cluster** is a group of cases linked by time or place that may or may not be a true outbreak. **Pandemic** is an epidemic crossing international borders. An *epidemic curve* (epi curve) — a bar chart of cases by date of illness onset — is the single most useful picture of outbreak type.

### Common-source outbreaks

In a common-source outbreak, all affected people share one exposure source. Subtypes are named by the exposure pattern:

- **Point source:** everyone is exposed at roughly the same time, so cases rise sharply and fall quickly — a single spike on the epi curve. A wedding banquet where contaminated food makes dozens of guests ill within hours is a classic example.
- **Continuous source:** the source keeps contaminating, so cases keep appearing — a repeatedly contaminated water supply produces a plateau of cases.
- **Intermittent source:** exposure comes and goes, producing a jagged epi curve.

The incubation period (time from exposure to symptoms) shifts the curve right; investigators look back one incubation period from the case cluster to find the exposure.

### Propagated outbreaks

In a propagated outbreak, the pathogen passes from person to person, so each case can generate new cases. The epi curve builds gradually, with successive "generations" of cases peaking about one incubation period apart. Without intervention, propagated outbreaks can grow exponentially in susceptible populations — measles in an under-vaccinated community is a classic example. Control therefore focuses on interrupting contact and reducing susceptibility (for example, through vaccination).

### How pathogens travel: transmission modes

The type of outbreak is closely tied to how the pathogen moves:

- **Direct contact:** person-to-person spread via skin, mucous membranes, or body fluids.
- **Indirect contact:** spread through contaminated objects (**fomites**) or surfaces.
- **Droplet:** larger respiratory droplets from coughing, sneezing, or talking, traveling short distances.
- **Airborne:** smaller particles that can remain suspended in air and travel farther (for example, measles).
- **Vehicle-borne:** spread through a contaminated medium — food, water, blood, or drugs; foodborne and waterborne outbreaks are vehicle-borne.
- **Vector-borne:** spread by living organisms such as mosquitoes or ticks.
- **Zoonotic:** the pathogen's reservoir is animals, with spillover to humans (for example, rabies).

One pathogen can have multiple modes; that is one reason the same disease may produce very different outbreak patterns in different settings.

### Outbreak investigation in practice

Although the full investigation is led by public health epidemiologists, every nurse should understand the logic: confirm the diagnosis, establish a case definition, count and locate cases, draw the epi curve, hypothesize about source and mode, test those hypotheses, implement control measures, and communicate findings. Nurses contribute case reports, exposure histories, and community access. Reporting is legally mandated for many diseases, but the list of reportable conditions and the reporting pathway differ by state/province and country — nurses must know their local requirements.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I’m 10

An outbreak is when lots of people get sick at once. Sometimes everyone got sick from the *same thing* — like one bad batch of potato salad at a picnic — and that's called a common-source outbreak. Other times, sick people pass the germ to healthy people, who pass it to more people, like a game of tag that never ends — that's a person-to-person outbreak. Figuring out which kind it is tells grown-ups whether to throw away the potato salad or keep people apart.

## High-Yield Points

- Outbreak types: **common source** (point, continuous, intermittent) vs **propagated** (person to person) vs **mixed**.
- Point-source epi curve: single sharp spike; propagated: gradual climb with successive peaks.
- Common-source control = find and remove the source; propagated control = interrupt person-to-person contact and reduce susceptibility.
- Transmission modes: contact (direct/indirect/fomite), droplet, airborne, vehicle (food/water), vector, zoonotic.
- An epi curve is drawn by date of illness onset; incubation period shifts the curve.
- Nurses are frontline detectors and reporters — reportable-disease lists and reporting rules vary by jurisdiction.
- A "cluster" of cases may or may not be an outbreak; investigation confirms it.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Outbreak | More cases of a disease than expected in a place and time | The signal that something needs investigation and action |
| Common-source outbreak | Many people exposed to one shared source (food, water, equipment) | Control = find and remove the source |
| Point source | A common-source outbreak where exposure happened at one time | Produces a single spike epi curve; exposure likely one incubation period before the peak |
| Propagated outbreak | Pathogen spreads person to person, generating successive rounds of cases | Control = interrupt contact and reduce susceptibility |
| Epi curve | Bar chart of cases by date of symptom onset | The quickest read on outbreak type and growth |
| Index case | The first case identified in an outbreak | Starting point for tracing who was exposed to whom |

## Example: Two epi curves, two responses

A school nurse logs absences during the same week in two fictional districts.

**District A:** On Friday, 34 students and staff who attended the same district-wide science fair picnic develop nausea and diarrhea within 24 hours. The epi curve is one tall spike. Analysis: point-source outbreak — likely a contaminated food item at the picnic. Control: identify and remove the implicated food, interview attendees, and stop the supplier from distributing the item.

**District B:** A student comes to school with a rash and fever (the index case). A week later two classmates develop the same illness; the week after, eight more; the week after, twenty. The epi curve climbs stepwise, with peaks about one incubation period apart. Analysis: propagated outbreak — person-to-person spread in a susceptible population. Control: exclude ill students, identify and quarantine contacts, and check vaccination coverage.

Same word — outbreak — but the epi curves point to completely different control strategies. That is why the type matters.

## Common Confusions

| Do not confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Outbreak | Cluster | A cluster is cases grouped by time/place; it *may* be an outbreak — investigation decides. |
| Common-source outbreak | Propagated outbreak | Common source = one shared exposure; propagated = chains of person-to-person spread. |
| Droplet transmission | Airborne transmission | Droplets travel short distances and fall quickly; airborne particles hang in the air and travel farther. |
| Index case | Primary case | The index case is the *first identified* case; the primary case *started* the chain — they are not always the same person. |
| Foodborne disease | Contagious disease | Foodborne disease is typically not spread person to person; contagious diseases are — control measures differ. |
| Epidemic | Endemic | Epidemic is a rise *above expected*; endemic is disease *constantly present* at expected levels. |
