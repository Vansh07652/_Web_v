# Geographical Disparities

> **Book:** [Population Health for Nurses](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 9: Health Disparities](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 4  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Geographical disparities are systematic, avoidable differences in health outcomes, health risks, and access to care that are tied to **where people live**. Health is not spread evenly across a map: outcomes differ between rural and urban areas, between regions of a country, between neighborhoods of the same city, and between countries. "Place matters" captures the core idea — where a person lives influences everything from the air they breathe and the food available nearby to whether a clinic or hospital is within reach.

Geography is not a cause in itself; it is a **packaging of conditions**. A given place concentrates resources or deficits — jobs, housing quality, transit, grocery stores, green space, pollution, medical facilities, and social networks. A place-based disparity is usually the visible result of many social, economic, and policy factors stacking up in one location. Like other disparities, geographical disparities are avoidable and unfair: they reflect how resources and opportunities are distributed, not any inherent quality of the people who live there.

## Why This Matters

- **Nurses practice in places.** Community health nurses assess communities defined by geography — a county, a school district, a housing development. Understanding how place shapes health is the foundation of community assessment.
- **Access is more than distance.** Rural residents may face long drives to care, but access also means transportation, insurance, clinic hours, and whether specialists exist nearby.
- **Facility trends reshape care.** Hospital and clinic closures — which have affected many rural communities — change when and where people can get care and shift burdens onto remaining facilities and nurses.
- **Policy varies by place.** State and local decisions (Medicaid eligibility, public health funding, land use) create different health environments for otherwise similar people.
- **Telehealth changes the map.** Remote care can close distance gaps — but only where broadband, devices, and digital skills exist, which is itself unequally distributed.

## Core Concepts

### Rural versus urban health

Rural and urban populations differ in health-relevant ways, but neither is a uniform group. Rural areas are often characterized by longer travel distances to care, smaller and older populations, fewer specialists, and more jobs in agriculture, mining, and other hazardous industries. Rural communities also tend to have fewer transit options and, in many places, limited broadband. Some rural populations face higher rates of certain chronic conditions, injuries, and mental health concerns; however, both categories contain wide internal variation that differs by region, racial and ethnic group, and time.

### The built environment and neighborhood resources

Within cities, health can differ dramatically between neighborhoods only miles apart. The **built environment** — housing quality, walkability, green space, lighting, traffic safety, and the presence of grocery stores versus fast-food outlets — shapes activity, diet, injury risk, and mental health. **Environmental exposures** (air pollution, contaminated water or soil, proximity to industrial sites and highways) are also unequally distributed, with lower-income communities and communities of color disproportionately affected — the concern of the **environmental justice** movement. The term "food desert" is widely used for areas with limited food access, but many researchers prefer "limited food access" because it emphasizes conditions (cost, transportation, store placement) rather than implying the area is a natural desert.

### Regional and state-level variation

Health outcomes and life expectancy vary across regions and states, and policy choices are a major reason. Whether a state expanded Medicaid eligibility, how it funds public health and schools, tobacco and alcohol regulation, housing policy, and transportation investment all shape the health environment. Because these differ by jurisdiction, a person's health prospects can depend on which side of a state line they live on — an avoidable, policy-driven disparity.

### Global geography

At the global scale, geography tracks with enormous health differences: infectious disease burden is higher in many low- and middle-income countries, and climate, conflict, and economic conditions concentrate risk in particular regions. Global disparities also include unequal access to clean water, sanitation, vaccines, and essential medicines. Nurses working internationally — or caring for immigrants and refugees — benefit from understanding the health contexts people come from, without stereotyping any country or region.

### Measuring place: tools for nurses

Nurses use **community health needs assessments** and mapping (GIS) to describe a community's geography of health: where people live, where services are, and where the gaps are. A common saying — that a person's ZIP code may predict health better than their genetic code — is a reminder that place data are powerful, though it oversimplifies: ZIP codes are administrative boundaries, not neighborhoods, and no single measure captures a place.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

> Imagine two kids who both love swimming. One lives next door to a pool with a coach and clean locker rooms; the other lives an hour from any pool and has no way to get there. The kids are not different — their towns are. Geographical disparities are like that: people can be healthier or sicker mostly because of what their neighborhood, town, or country does or doesn't have.

## High-Yield Points

- **Place is a social determinant of health.** Geography packages resources, exposures, and access together.
- **Rural vs. urban is a real pattern, but neither group is uniform.** Always look within the category.
- **Access ≠ distance.** Transportation, hours, insurance, and workforce all shape real access.
- **Neighborhoods within cities differ as much as regions do.** Built environment and environmental exposures are unequally distributed.
- **Policy varies by place.** Jurisdictional rules create different health environments; know your own.
- **Use assessment tools.** Community health needs assessments and mapping turn "place matters" into actionable data.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Geographical disparity | Avoidable health difference tied to where people live | Identifies place-based patterns that can be changed |
| Built environment | Human-made surroundings: housing, roads, parks, stores | Shapes activity, diet, injury risk, and mental health daily |
| Health professional shortage area (HPSA) | Designation for places lacking enough providers | Flags communities where access to care is structurally limited |
| Environmental justice | Fair distribution of environmental benefits and burdens | Addresses how pollution and hazards concentrate in certain communities |
| Limited food access | Areas where healthy food is hard to get (cost, distance, transport) | More precise than "food desert"; points to fixable conditions |
| Community health needs assessment | Systematic look at a community's health and resources | The standard nursing tool for place-based planning |
| Telehealth | Health care delivered at a distance using technology | Can shrink distance gaps — but only where broadband and skills exist |
| GIS (mapping) | Software that layers health and place data on maps | Makes geographical patterns visible for planning |

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process

Using a place-based lens in community health nursing:

1. **Define the geography.** Choose the unit you are studying: county, census tract, school district, or catchment area. Be explicit about boundaries.
2. **Gather data.** Collect health outcome data (where available) alongside community data: demographics, income, housing, transit, food access, environmental exposures, and provider locations.
3. **Map and compare.** Look for patterns: where are outcomes worse, and what resources or hazards coincide with those places?
4. **Ask the community.** Data show where; residents explain why. Pair maps with listening.
5. **Plan with partners.** Target interventions at the gaps found — services, transit, food access, policy — not just at individual behavior.
6. **Evaluate and revisit.** Re-assess whether patterns change; geographical disparities are persistent and need sustained attention.

Clinical actions always require the appropriate supervision, scope, orders, and institutional procedure.

## Example

A county health department observes that residents of the county's eastern region have higher rates of emergency department visits for asthma than residents of the western region. A nurse-led team maps the data and finds the eastern region sits near a highway and an industrial corridor, has older housing with known mold issues, and has one clinic that closes at 5 p.m. — while the west has newer housing, more green space, and a hospital. Rather than telling eastern residents to "manage their asthma better," the team partners with the housing authority, schools, and local government to address triggers, extends clinic hours, and works with a community group on plans for a health center. The fix targets the place, not the people — the essence of addressing geographical disparities.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| A place being correlated with outcomes | The place causing the outcomes | Geography packages many conditions; causation needs investigation |
| Distance to care | Access to care | Access also includes transit, hours, insurance, and workforce |
| Rural (or urban) as one group | The diversity within rural and urban populations | Each category varies widely by region, race, and income |
| ZIP code | Neighborhood | ZIP codes are postal boundaries, not communities; maps can mislead |
| "Food desert" | Lack of any food | The issue is usually cost, transport, and store placement — fixable conditions |
