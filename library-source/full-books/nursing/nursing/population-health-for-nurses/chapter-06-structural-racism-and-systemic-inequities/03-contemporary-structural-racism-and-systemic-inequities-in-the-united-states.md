# Contemporary Structural Racism and Systemic Inequities in the United States

> **Book:** [Population Health for Nurses](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 6: Structural Racism and Systemic Inequities](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 3  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

If the previous topic asked *how did we get here?*, this topic asks *what does structural racism look like right now?* The answer: ordinary systems operating as designed — housing markets that still sort people by race, schools funded by neighborhood wealth, workplaces with unequal opportunity, environmental hazards concentrated in certain communities, and a criminal-legal system with unequal impacts. None of this requires a villain; each is a set of rules, incentives, and routines that reproduce the patterns built by history.

The most useful frame in population health: **a person's ZIP code predicts health more powerfully than many individual behaviors** — because ZIP code carries housing, environment, food, schools, jobs, safety, and care access. Structural racism is the machinery that keeps ZIP codes unequal.

## Why This Matters

- **It reframes "nonadherence."** A patient who "doesn't follow the plan" may face a pharmacy that closes when their shift ends or a landlord who won't fix mold.
- **It guides community assessment.** Public-health nurses assess neighborhoods for environmental hazards, food access, housing quality, and transportation — the domains where structural inequity shows up.
- **It justifies advocacy.** A cluster of asthma near a highway calls for environmental and policy action, not just inhaler education.
- **It is exam content.** Expect scenarios asking which *system* produces a described inequity.

## Core Concepts

### Housing and neighborhood segregation

Decades after legal segregation ended, neighborhoods remain segregated — and maintained, not merely inherited: **exclusionary zoning** (rules barring affordable housing types), discriminatory lending, real-estate steering, and landlord practices keep it going. Segregation concentrates poverty, which concentrates exposure: substandard housing, underfunded schools, fewer grocery stores, less green space, longer commutes. Asthma, lead exposure, and chronic stress are classic consequences — always verify current local data before citing rates.

### The wealth gap

Income is what you earn; **wealth** is what you own — savings, home equity, investments — and wealth is what carries a family through a job loss, illness, or down payment. The racial wealth gap far exceeds the income gap and compounds: wealth buys education, care, healthier neighborhoods, and the ability to take risks. Intergenerational transfers (inheritance, down-payment help) keep the gap moving forward.

### Education and opportunity

Public schools are funded largely through local property taxes, so school quality tracks neighborhood wealth — and neighborhood wealth tracks segregation. Unequal schools mean unequal access to the credentials and networks that shape lifetime earnings and health. A nominally neutral funding formula, operating on segregated neighborhoods, reproduces inequity.

### Employment and economic opportunity

Discrimination in hiring, promotion, and pay persists even when qualifications are equal — documented in repeated field studies (for current data, see the U.S. EEOC and academic audit studies). Occupational segregation funnels workers from some groups into jobs with lower pay, fewer benefits, and higher physical hazard — with direct health consequences.

### Environmental racism

**Environmental racism** describes the disproportionate siting of hazardous facilities — industrial plants, waste sites, highways — near communities of color and low-income communities. The **environmental-justice movement** grew out of community organizing against this pattern and now shapes policy at federal, state, and local levels (see EPA EJ resources for current programs). Health consequences include asthma, cardiovascular and respiratory disease, and the stress of living with pollution.

### The criminal-legal system and health

Policing, arrest, and sentencing patterns fall unequally across racial groups — documented in government statistics (verify current figures against sources such as the Bureau of Justice Statistics). Incarceration harms health directly and indirectly: it disrupts families, housing, employment, and income, and a criminal record creates lifelong barriers. Because incarceration concentrates in certain communities, its health effects ripple through neighborhoods.

### Food, digital, and care deserts

Contemporary systems also create *access* deserts: **food deserts** (little access to affordable, nutritious food), pharmacy deserts, and broadband deserts. Each is produced by market decisions interacting with neighborhood income and race. Consequences (diet-related disease, medication access, telehealth exclusion) are population-level and policy-responsive.

### How inequities are measured

Population health measures these patterns with surveillance data: life expectancy, mortality, chronic-disease prevalence, and hospitalization rates by race/ethnicity, geography, and income. *Because rates change yearly and definitions vary by source, always check current reports (e.g., CDC, state health departments, KFF) before quoting a number.* The exam-relevant habit: **disparity** is a measurable difference between groups; **inequity** is a difference that is avoidable and unfair. A difference alone is not proof of inequity — the analyst must show it is produced by unjust systems.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Two kids are born in the same city. One lives in a neighborhood with a park, a grocery store, and a good school. The other lives a few miles away in a neighborhood with a highway on one side, a factory on the other, a store with only snacks, and a crowded school. Nobody chose the second neighborhood "to be mean" — but rules made years ago, plus today's prices and policies, keep the neighborhoods different. The kids' health will differ long before either makes a single "health choice."

## High-Yield Points

- **ZIP code predicts health:** neighborhood carries housing, environment, food, schools, jobs, and care access.
- **Segregation is maintained today** by exclusionary zoning, lending discrimination, steering, and landlord practices.
- **Wealth gaps outpace income gaps:** wealth absorbs shocks and transfers advantage across generations.
- **School funding follows property taxes,** so education quality tracks neighborhood wealth.
- **Environmental racism** concentrates hazards near communities of color; environmental-justice policy is the corrective.
- **The criminal-legal system** has documented unequal impacts; incarceration's effects spread to families and neighborhoods.
- **Disparity ≠ inequity:** inequity is an avoidable, unfair difference produced by unjust systems.
- **Verify all current statistics** against surveillance sources; rates change and definitions vary.

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process

**Assessing a community for structural inequities:**

1. **Describe the population and place** — housing stock, built environment, income profile.
2. **Map the systems** — how housing, environment, food, schools, employment, transit, policing, broadband interact.
3. **Look for the pattern** — any exposure or resource distributed unequally *by race or income* is a structural signature.
4. **Check the data** — pull current surveillance data and verify before citing rates.
5. **Identify the lever** — which policy or market decision produces the pattern, and who has authority to change it?
6. **Act within scope** — document, connect families to resources, advocate at the level your role and jurisdiction allow.

## Example

A community-health nurse maps asthma-related emergency visits and finds a tight cluster in one district: an older rental neighborhood beside a busy highway and a former industrial site. The nurse sees buildings with visible mold and peeling paint, one convenience store and no full-service grocery within a mile, a bus line running every 45 minutes, and a school beside the highway. Individually, each observation is mundane; together they are the contemporary machinery of structural inequity. Housing policy and landlord practices produced the building conditions; zoning and market decisions produced the food and transit gaps; siting decisions placed the highway and industry. The nurse's response is two-level: connect residents to resources (asthma education, lead-testing referrals, food assistance) *and* join community efforts for housing-code enforcement, transit improvements, and environmental review — because the cluster will not be fixed by inhalers alone.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | The Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Disparity | Inequity | Any measurable difference vs. an avoidable, unfair one from unjust systems |
| Individual choices | Structural constraints | Choices happen inside constraints; a group-wide pattern signals structure |
| Correlation | Causation | Co-occurrence needs mechanism and evidence |
| Race as the cause | Racism as the cause | Race is a category; exposure to discrimination harms health |
| De facto segregation | Voluntary clustering | Maintained by ongoing practices, not just preference |
| One bad actor | A bad system | Persists through routine operation — no single-person fix |
