# The LGBTQIA+ Community

> **Book:** [Population Health for Nurses](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 27: Caring for Vulnerable Populations and Communities](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 3  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

**LGBTQIA+** is an umbrella term for people whose sexual orientation, gender identity, or gender expression differs from what society treats as the default: lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer or questioning, intersex, asexual, and other identities. As a population health topic, the community is defined less by identity itself and more by a shared social experience: growing up and living in a society that has historically stigmatized, pathologized, and excluded them. That shared experience — **minority stress** — is the key to understanding this population's health.

Crucially, being LGBTQIA+ is not itself a health risk. The disparities seen in this population — higher rates of depression, anxiety, suicidality, and substance use, plus lower rates of some types of screening — are driven by discrimination, stigma, violence, rejection, and barriers to care. Population health nursing therefore examines the social conditions that produce harm and creates health care environments where people feel safe enough to seek care, disclose their history, and stay engaged. Every nurse cares for LGBTQIA+ people whether they know it or not — and the quality of that care depends on knowledge, language, and trust.

## Why This Matters

The LGBTQIA+ community experiences persistent health disparities that are **preventable and structural** — a textbook population health problem. Research consistently links these patterns to stigma, discrimination, violence, family rejection, and lack of access to knowledgeable, affirming care.

For nurses, the stakes are clinical, interpersonal, systemic, and ethical. **Clinical:** a patient who fears judgment will not disclose sexual history, gender identity, or embarrassing symptoms — and undisclosed information produces missed diagnoses. **Interpersonal:** simple practices — using a person's chosen name and pronouns, inclusive intake forms — measurably improve trust and engagement. **Systemic:** organizations decide whether their environments are safe through policies, forms, staff training, and data collection. **Ethical:** nursing's professional codes call for care that is respectful of the person and attentive to the social conditions that affect health. LGBTQIA+ health is not a specialty interest; it is core nursing.

## Core Concepts

### Language that matters

Clear vocabulary is the foundation of respectful care:

- **Sex assigned at birth** is the label (typically male or female) given at birth based on anatomy.
- **Gender identity** is a person's internal sense of their own gender — man, woman, nonbinary, or another identity. It cannot be observed from the outside.
- **Gender expression** is how a person presents gender through clothing, name, and mannerisms — a presentation, not proof of identity.
- **Sexual orientation** describes whom a person is attracted to (lesbian, gay, bisexual, pansexual) — a different dimension from gender identity.
- **Transgender** people's gender identity differs from the sex assigned at birth; **cisgender** people's identity matches it. **Nonbinary** people do not identify exclusively as man or woman. **Intersex** people are born with sex characteristics that do not fit typical male/female categories.

The practical rule for nurses: never assume — ask what name and pronouns a person uses, and use them. This is not political; it is accurate communication, and accuracy builds trust.

### Minority stress: the mechanism behind the disparities

The **minority stress model** explains how social conditions get under the skin. People in stigmatized groups experience chronic, socially based stress: prejudice events (slurs, discrimination, violence), expectation of rejection, concealment of identity (the exhausting work of hiding), and internalized negative messages from growing up in a stigmatizing culture. Chronic stress is in turn associated with mental health problems, substance use, and physical disease. The model explains why disparities persist even when access to care looks similar — the stress is the exposure. It also points to the solution: reducing stigma, increasing acceptance and family support, and creating safe environments — interventions at the social level, not just the individual.

### Health disparities across the lifespan

Patterns differ by subgroup and life stage, and nurses should understand the landscape without overgeneralizing:

- **Youth:** LGBTQIA+ young people, especially transgender youth, face elevated rates of bullying, family rejection, homelessness (a large share of youth experiencing homelessness identify as LGBTQIA+), and suicidality — yet family acceptance and school support are strongly protective.
- **Adults:** elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and substance use; HIV and other STIs affect gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men and transgender people at higher rates, and prevention and testing are public health priorities.
- **Older adults:** more likely to live alone and to have carried decades of concealment; they often need culturally safe long-term care.
- **Transgender people of all ages:** face some of the largest disparities, driven by discrimination, violence, and barriers to gender-affirming care; access varies widely by jurisdiction.

These are population patterns — they describe groups, not individuals — and clinical decisions are always individualized.

### Barriers to care: the "don't ask, don't tell" clinic

Many LGBTQIA+ people delay or avoid care because of past discrimination, fear of judgment, or the exhaustion of educating providers. Concrete barriers include: intake forms that only offer "male/female" with no chosen-name field; insurance and billing systems that misgender people or deny coverage (rules vary by jurisdiction); providers who assume heterosexuality and skip relevant sexual history; and a shortage of trained providers. When a patient must repeatedly correct the record or explain their own identity, they may simply stop coming. The remedy is systemic: inclusive forms, visible non-discrimination policies, trained staff, and confidentiality practices that respect privacy — especially for youth, whose safety may depend on what is disclosed to parents (disclosure rules vary by jurisdiction).

### Affirming care in practice

**Affirming care** treats a person's identity as a fact to be respected, not a problem to be solved. Concrete practices: use the person's chosen name and pronouns everywhere (and gently correct the record); ask about sexual orientation and gender identity as part of routine history, in a neutral way, explaining why you ask; use gender-neutral language until preferences are known ("your partner" rather than "your husband or wife"); provide respectful sexual health education and screening conversations; and know local referral resources for gender-affirming care, mental health, and peer support. Affirming care also means not conflating identity with mental illness — and not making identity the center of every visit: an LGBTQIA+ person with a sore throat wants the sore throat treated.

### Data, policy, and the nurse as advocate

You cannot improve what you do not measure, and until recently most health systems did not routinely collect **sexual orientation and gender identity (SOGI) data**, making disparities invisible. Collecting SOGI data respectfully, with a stated purpose and privacy protections, is now recommended in many settings, though practices and laws vary by jurisdiction. Nurses advocate at every level: for inclusive organizational policies and staff training; for safe, non-discriminatory environments; for research that includes this population; and for the mental health and social supports (family acceptance programs, anti-bullying policies, housing and homeless services, suicide prevention) that address root causes. Scope of practice, consent rules, and available services vary by state and jurisdiction — nurses practice accordingly and refer appropriately.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Some people are treated badly just because of who they love or how they feel about being a boy or a girl — and being treated badly for a long time can make people sick, sad, or scared to see a doctor. A nurse helps by treating everyone with respect, asking what name and pronouns they use, and making the clinic a safe place. When people feel safe, they come for care earlier, tell the truth about their health, and get better sooner.

## High-Yield Points

- **Identity is not a risk factor — stigma is:** disparities are driven by minority stress, discrimination, and barriers to care.
- **Know the vocabulary:** sex assigned at birth, gender identity, gender expression, and sexual orientation are four different things; never assume any of them.
- **Use chosen names and pronouns:** accuracy builds trust; asking is respectful, and correcting the record quietly is professional.
- **Minority stress** — prejudice events, expectation of rejection, concealment, internalized stigma — is the mechanism linking stigma to poor health.
- **Disparities vary by subgroup and life stage:** youth, older adults, and transgender people have distinct needs; population patterns never dictate individual care.
- **Inclusive systems matter:** chosen-name fields, visible non-discrimination policies, and staff training change whether people seek care.
- **Confidentiality is a safety issue,** especially for youth; disclosure rules to parents vary by jurisdiction — know your local rules.
- **The nurse's role:** ask respectfully, document accurately, refer to knowledgeable providers, and advocate for policies that reduce stigma.

## Example: The Intake Form That Changed Everything

A community clinic notices that its LGBTQIA+ patients report low trust and that many transgender patients drive an hour to a different city for care. A nurse leads a review of the patient experience and finds the problems are small and fixable. The intake form offers only "Male ☐ Female ☐" with a "Legal name" field and no place for a chosen name; the electronic record has no pronoun field; and several staff admit they have never been trained on LGBTQIA+ health. The clinic updates the form to include a chosen-name field, a pronoun question, and an optional gender identity question with a "prefer not to say" option, explains why the data are collected, and trains staff on respectful language and confidentiality. Within months, a transgender patient named Alex — whose record previously showed only a former name and "Female" — is called in as "Alex" and asked for pronouns. Alex discloses a family history of breast cancer that was never documented before, because previous visits ended with Alex too uncomfortable to share. The screening conversation that follows is ordinary — but it is happening years earlier than it would have. The nurse did not deliver dramatic care; the nurse fixed the environment, and the environment changed the care.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Sexual orientation | Gender identity | Orientation is whom you're attracted to; identity is who you are — they are separate dimensions |
| Transgender | Sexual orientation | Transgender is about gender identity; a transgender person may be straight, gay, bisexual, or anything else |
| Gender expression | Gender identity | A masculine-presenting person may not identify as a man; expression is presentation, identity is internal |
| "LGBTQIA+ people all have the same needs" | Diverse subgroups with different needs | Youth, older adults, and transgender people face different disparities; population patterns are not individual predictions |
| Asking about identity is intrusive | Asking respectfully is standard history-taking | The difference is in how you ask, why you explain, and how you use the answer — always with confidentiality |
| Affirming care = special treatment | Affirming care = accurate, respectful care | Using a chosen name and pronouns is correct communication — the same respect offered to everyone |
