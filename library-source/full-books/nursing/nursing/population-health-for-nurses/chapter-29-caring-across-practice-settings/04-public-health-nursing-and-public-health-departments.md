# Public Health Nursing and Public Health Departments

> **Book:** [Population Health for Nurses](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 29: Caring Across Practice Settings](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 4  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Public health nursing (PHN) applies nursing knowledge to the health of **populations** rather than single patients. Public health nurses (PHNs) promote health, prevent disease and injury, and protect communities through clinical care, education, advocacy, and policy work, combining nursing science with public health science such as epidemiology, biostatistics, environmental health, and health policy. The specialty has deep roots: Lillian Wald and Mary Brewster founded the Henry Street Settlement in New York City in 1893, providing home nursing in tenement neighborhoods and popularizing the term "public health nurse." Today PHNs staff local and state health departments, school health programs, occupational health services, and disaster response. A defining feature is that **the community is the client**: even one-on-one care is planned and evaluated against the health of the larger population — an "upstream" approach that emphasizes prevention and equity.

## Why This Matters

PHNs are the frontline workforce of the public health system, delivering immunizations, outbreak investigation, maternal–child health programs, and emergency response. Knowing how health departments are organized explains who does what in a crisis: local departments conduct case investigation and clinics, state departments coordinate data and guidance, and federal agencies such as the CDC provide surveillance support, laboratory expertise, and funding. The topic is also high yield for exams because it links recurring themes — the three core functions of public health, the 10 Essential Public Health Services, the roles a population-focused nurse can fill, and scope-of-practice rules that vary by state, employer, and position.

## Core Concepts

### What makes public health nursing different

PHN differs from hospital nursing in four practical ways. The **unit of care is the population** — the "client" may be a neighborhood, school district, or county. The **emphasis is prevention**, from primary prevention (stopping problems before they start) through screening (secondary) to limiting the impact of established disease (tertiary). The work is **interdisciplinary**, partnering with epidemiologists, health educators, schools, housing authorities, and community organizations. And practice is **grounded in population data** — surveillance, community assessments, and evidence about what works for groups.

### The three core functions of public health

Since the 1988 Institute of Medicine report *The Future of Public Health*, practice has been organized around three core functions: **assessment** (systematically gathering and analyzing data about the community's health and needs), **policy development** (using evidence to create and advance plans, policies, and programs), and **assurance** (making sure essential services are available and accessible to everyone, and evaluating performance).

### The 10 Essential Public Health Services

These core functions are operationalized in the 10 Essential Public Health Services (revised 2020): (1) assess and monitor population health; (2) investigate and address health hazards; (3) communicate effectively; (4) strengthen communities and partnerships; (5) create and implement policies and laws; (6) utilize legal and regulatory actions; (7) assure equitable access to services; (8) build a diverse public health workforce; (9) improve through evaluation and research; and (10) build strong organizational infrastructure. Exam questions often ask you to match a nurse's activity to one of these services.

### How health departments are organized

The U.S. public health system is layered. **Local health departments** (city, county, or district) are closest to communities: they run clinics, investigate local outbreaks, conduct inspections, and coordinate local emergency response. **State health departments** set statewide priorities, collect and analyze data, license facilities and professionals, administer many federal grants, and support local departments. **Federal agencies** within the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services — the CDC, Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA), Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), National Institutes of Health (NIH), Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) — provide funding, research, guidance, and regulation; the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) covers environmental health. Each layer has distinct authority, and the split of responsibilities differs from state to state.

### Roles, the Intervention Wheel, and scope of practice

A PHN wears many hats: **clinician** (immunizations, screening, protocol-based treatment), **educator**, **case manager**, **advocate**, **collaborator**, **researcher**, and **program planner/administrator**. The Intervention Wheel (from the Minnesota Department of Health) organizes population-based practice as **17 public health interventions in five wedges**: surveillance and investigation; outreach, screening, and case finding; referral, follow-up, and case management; health teaching and counseling; and collaboration, community organizing, advocacy, and policy work. Its key teaching point: every intervention can be applied at the **individual, family, or community level**, so case management can serve one person or an entire population through systems change.

Health departments are funded by local and state appropriations, federal grants (such as preventive health block grants), fees, and program reimbursements. Scope of practice is governed by the state nurse practice act plus agency policy; some PHN positions require a BSN, and some employers value public health certification (CPH) or an MPH. Because rules and funding differ by jurisdiction, nurses must confirm the expectations of their own position.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

A public health nurse is like the crossing guard for the whole neighborhood. A regular nurse takes care of one sick person at a time, but a public health nurse watches over everyone at once — checking what's making people sick, teaching families how to stay well, and helping the town's health department fix problems before they spread. Their "patient" is the whole community.

## High-Yield Points

- **The community is the client**: PHN practice is population-focused even when it looks like one-on-one care.
- **Three core functions**: assessment, policy development, assurance — memorize this triad.
- **The 10 Essential Public Health Services** carry out the core functions; match activities to services on exams.
- **Lillian Wald and the Henry Street Settlement (1893)** are the classic historical anchor.
- **Layered system**: local departments deliver most direct services; state departments coordinate; federal agencies (CDC, HRSA, CMS, NIH, FDA, SAMHSA) fund, guide, and regulate.
- **Upstream focus**: PHNs prioritize primary prevention and health equity over treating established disease.
- **Intervention Wheel**: one intervention (e.g., case management) can be delivered at individual, family, and community levels.
- **Scope varies**: state nurse practice acts, employer policy, and position descriptions determine what a PHN may legally do.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Public health nursing | Nursing practice focused on promoting and protecting the health of populations | Defines the specialty and its population-centered mission |
| Population-focused practice | Care planned and evaluated at the group level, not just for individual patients | The core distinction between PHN and hospital nursing |
| Core functions | Public health's three big jobs: assessment, policy development, assurance | Organizing framework for the whole system |
| Essential Public Health Services | The 10 concrete activities that bring the core functions to life | Exam favorite; links every PHN task to the system |
| Local health department | The city/county agency delivering clinics and outbreak response | First point of contact for most population services |
| CDC | Federal agency providing surveillance, lab expertise, guidance, and funding | Coordinates national response and data |
| Intervention Wheel | Framework of 17 interventions applied at individual, family, and community levels | Shows how population-based practice actually operates |
| Upstream approach | Acting on root causes of illness before disease develops | Core philosophy: prevention beats treatment |

## Scenario: A County Health Department Responds to a School Outbreak

A county health department receives reports that several students at the same elementary school have a vaccine-preventable disease. Maria, a PHN, begins with **surveillance**: how many cases, which classrooms, who is unvaccinated or immunocompromised — assessment in action. Next she practices **case finding and outreach**: she and her team contact exposed families, offer **health teaching** about what to watch for, and open a **screening and vaccination clinic** at the school (primary prevention for classmates behind on vaccines). She **collaborates** with the school nurse and principal and **advocates** for an extra evening so working parents can attend, while tracking new cases and reporting trends to the state health department. Finally the team **evaluates**: Did cases stop? Did families get what they needed? Maria vaccinated children and counseled parents, but her decisions, data, and goals were population-level the whole time — that is public health nursing.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Public health nursing | Community health nursing | Often interchangeable, but PHN emphasizes population focus; "community health" may include individual home health care |
| Core functions | Essential services | Three broad functions vs. the 10 concrete services carrying them out |
| Local health department | State health department | Local delivers direct services and outbreak response; state coordinates data, licensing, and grants |
| CDC | Local public health | The CDC funds, guides, and provides surveillance/lab support; it does not run local clinics |
| Individual care by a PHN | Individual-focused nursing | A PHN can give one-on-one care, but it is always planned to serve the population's health |

3. Why is public health nursing described as "upstream" practice?
4. How do local, state, and federal agencies differ during an outbreak?
5. What is the key teaching point of the Public Health Intervention Wheel?

3. Because it acts on the causes of illness before disease develops, emphasizing primary prevention rather than treating established disease.
