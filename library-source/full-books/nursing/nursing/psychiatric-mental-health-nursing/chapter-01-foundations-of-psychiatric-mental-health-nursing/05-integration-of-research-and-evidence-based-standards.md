# Integration of Research- and Evidence-Based Standards

> **Book:** [Psychiatric-Mental Health Nursing](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 1: Foundations of Psychiatric-Mental Health Nursing](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 5  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Healthcare has not always been guided by evidence. Treatments were once passed down by tradition, authority, and anecdote — sometimes for centuries, even when they did more harm than good. **Evidence-based practice (EBP)** is the modern answer: a problem-solving approach to care that integrates three things — the **best available research evidence**, the **clinician's expertise**, and the **patient's values and preferences**. The classic definition comes from physician and epidemiologist David Sackett and colleagues (1996): "the conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients."

EBP is not "research only," and it is not "cookbook medicine." It is a disciplined way of asking questions, finding and weighing evidence, and then combining that evidence with clinical judgment and the person's own goals. Around EBP sits a wider ecosystem of **standards**: professional scope and standards documents, clinical practice guidelines, regulatory requirements, and facility policies that define good care in each setting. This topic explains how research, EBP, and standards fit together at the bedside.

## Why This Matters

- **Patient safety and outcomes.** Care based on solid evidence works better and harms less; tradition-based care can fail or hurt.
- **Accountability.** Nursing is accountable to the public through professional standards, state nurse practice acts, and accrediting bodies. Practicing within evidence-based protocols is part of that accountability.
- **It is a nursing competency.** Research literacy and EBP are expected of nurses at every level — from using evidence-based protocols at the bedside to leading evidence synthesis in advanced practice.
- **Mental health care changes fast.** New treatments and guidelines appear regularly; EBP skills separate signal from noise.

## Core Concepts

### Evidence-based practice: three pillars

EBP rests on three equal partners:

1. **Best research evidence** — findings from well-designed studies, ideally synthesized in systematic reviews.
2. **Clinical expertise** — the nurse's education, experience, and ability to assess the individual situation.
3. **Patient values and preferences** — what matters to the person: their goals, culture, fears, and choices.

Leave out any pillar and the decision suffers: evidence without expertise can be applied rigidly; expertise without evidence can become habit; and either without patient input ignores the person at the center of care.

### Research, EBP, and quality improvement: three different jobs

These terms are often confused:

- **Research** generates *new* knowledge (e.g., testing a new screening tool).
- **Evidence-based practice** *uses* existing knowledge to make care decisions (e.g., adopting a validated screening tool).
- **Quality improvement (QI)** improves *local* processes and outcomes (e.g., measuring how often your unit actually uses the tool, then fixing barriers).

Nurses participate in all three at different levels; understanding which job you are doing matters for ethics, oversight, and even regulatory classification.

### The five steps of EBP: the "5 A's"

EBP is usually taught as a five-step process:

1. **Ask** a focused clinical question using **PICO(T)**: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, (Time). Example: "In adults on an inpatient psychiatric unit (P), does daily 1:1 check-in (I), compared with check-ins on admission only (C), reduce self-harm events (O) during the stay (T)?"
2. **Acquire** evidence by searching trusted databases (e.g., PubMed, CINAHL, Cochrane Library) with a search strategy built from the PICO terms.
3. **Appraise** the evidence: Is the study valid? Is the effect meaningful? Does it apply to *this* patient and setting?
4. **Apply** the evidence, integrated with clinical judgment and the patient's values — and with facility policy and available resources.
5. **Assess** the outcome: Did it work? Should the practice continue?

### Levels of evidence: the hierarchy, used wisely

Evidence is commonly ranked in a **hierarchy**, from strongest to weakest:

- **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses** (rigorous syntheses of multiple studies) — top of the pyramid.
- **Randomized controlled trials (RCTs)** — experiments that randomly assign participants to interventions.
- **Cohort studies** (groups followed over time) and **case-control studies** (comparing people with and without an outcome).
- **Case series and case reports** — detailed descriptions of individual cases.
- **Expert opinion and editorials** — informed views, but the weakest evidence.

Two cautions. First, the hierarchy ranks study *designs*; a badly conducted RCT can be worse evidence than a careful cohort study, so quality matters as much as design. Second, some questions (e.g., about lived experience) are best answered by qualitative research, which has its own quality standards rather than a slot in this pyramid.

### A brief history: from Lind's trial to modern EBP

The idea of testing treatments by observation is old. In 1747, British naval surgeon **James Lind** conducted what is often called the first controlled clinical trial: aboard HMS *Salisbury*, he divided twelve sailors with scurvy into groups receiving different treatments and found that the group given citrus fruit recovered dramatically. Read with historical context, this was a genuine breakthrough — systematic comparison where anecdote had ruled — but it was far from modern methods: a tiny sample, no randomization or blinding as we understand them, and no statistical analysis. EBP as a named movement emerged in the 1990s (Sackett and colleagues), partly in response to research showing that clinicians often ignored evidence that had long been available.

### Ethical evidence: research with human participants

"Evidence" only counts if it was produced ethically. Modern research with human participants is governed by protections rooted in responses to historical abuses — most famously the U.S. Public Health Service syphilis study in Tuskegee (1932–1972), in which Black men with syphilis were denied treatment so researchers could observe the disease's course, a profound ethical failure that helped shape today's rules. In the United States, Institutional Review Boards (IRBs), informed consent requirements, and the Belmont Report's principles (respect for persons, beneficence, justice) now govern human-subjects research. When nurses read studies — including older classics — they should ask about ethics and context, not just results.

### Standards: guidelines, scope, and policy

Evidence becomes practice through **standards**:

- **Professional standards**, such as the American Nurses Association's *Scope and Standards of Practice*, define what competent nursing looks like.
- **Clinical practice guidelines** distill evidence into recommendations (for example, guidelines from psychiatric specialty organizations on assessing suicide risk). They are tools, not absolute mandates: clinicians apply them with judgment, and a recommendation may be inappropriate for a specific individual.
- **Regulatory and institutional requirements** — state nurse practice acts, accrediting bodies (such as The Joint Commission in the U.S.), and facility policies — set enforceable expectations that vary by jurisdiction and organization.

The nurse's job is to know the evidence *and* the local standards: evidence tells you what is likely to work; policy and law tell you what you may do in your setting. Where they conflict, the nurse flags the concern through appropriate channels (charge nurse, educator, ethics committee) rather than silently choosing one.

### Barriers and how nurses overcome them

Common barriers include limited time, weak search skills, poor access to full-text literature, cultures that value "how we've always done it," and fear of change. Facilitators include EBP mentors, journal clubs, point-of-care resources, and institutional support for asking questions. A single nurse can start small: pick one recurring unit question, run it through the 5 A's, and bring the evidence to the team.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Evidence-based practice is like baking with a recipe instead of guessing. The recipe is the best research — tested and compared many times. The chef's skill is the nurse's experience, and the person eating the cake gets to say what they like. You need all three — a great recipe, a skilled cook, and a happy eater — or the cake probably won't turn out right.

## High-Yield Points

- EBP = best research evidence + clinical expertise + patient values/preferences (Sackett et al., 1996).
- The **5 A's**: Ask (PICO[T]), Acquire, Appraise, Apply, Assess.
- **Research** creates knowledge; **EBP** uses it; **QI** improves local processes.
- Evidence hierarchy: systematic reviews → RCTs → cohort → case-control → case series → expert opinion; quality matters as much as design.
- James Lind's 1747 scurvy trial is the classic early controlled comparison — a breakthrough with major methodological limits by modern standards.
- Human-subjects research is governed by IRBs, informed consent, and the Belmont principles (respect for persons, beneficence, justice) — prompted in part by the Tuskegee study's abuses.
- Guidelines inform but do not override clinical judgment; nurse practice acts and facility policy are enforceable and vary by jurisdiction.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-language definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence-based practice | Care decisions integrating best research evidence, clinical expertise, and patient values | The standard for safe, effective modern care |
| PICO(T) | Framework for clinical questions: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Time | Turns a vague concern into a searchable question |
| Systematic review | Rigorous synthesis of all quality studies on a question | The strongest single source of evidence |
| Randomized controlled trial | Experiment with random assignment to interventions | The gold-standard design for testing treatments |
| Clinical practice guideline | Evidence distilled into recommendations by experts | Translates research into usable guidance — not a mandate |
| IRB | Institutional Review Board that approves and oversees human-subjects research | Protects participants' rights and safety |
| Quality improvement | Local efforts to measure and improve processes and outcomes | How evidence gets reliably implemented on a unit |

## Example: The 5 A's in action

A nurse on an inpatient psychiatric unit notices the fall rate is above the facility benchmark. The nurse turns this observation into a question: "In adults on an inpatient psychiatric unit (P), does hourly rounding (I), compared with on-demand response (C), reduce fall rates (O) during hospitalization (T)?" — the **Ask** step. She searches CINAHL and the Cochrane Library with those terms and finds a systematic review of hourly rounding trials (**Acquire**). **Appraising**, she checks that the review included randomized trials, that the effect was consistent, and that the studies' units resemble her own — noting the review's own limitations. **Applying** the finding, she brings the evidence to the unit council, which adapts a rounding protocol for the unit's layout and acuity, documenting the change with the nurse manager per facility policy. **Assessing** the outcome, the unit tracks fall rates for three months and reviews the data with the quality committee. Every step stayed inside her role, and the whole process was governed by facility policy and the nurse practice act — not individual preference.

## Common Confusions

| Do not confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| EBP | Research | Research creates new knowledge; EBP applies existing knowledge to decisions |
| EBP | Quality improvement | QI improves local processes; EBP is the evidence-informed decision framework that QI often implements |
| Systematic review | A literature review | A systematic review uses exhaustive, reproducible methods; a narrative review can be selective |
| Higher evidence level | Always better evidence | A poor-quality RCT can be worse than a strong cohort study; quality matters |
| Guidelines | Mandates | Guidelines are tools applied with judgment; law and facility policy are enforceable |
| James Lind's trial | A modern RCT | It was an early controlled comparison — small, unblinded, and unrandomized by modern standards |
