# Pica

> **Book:** [Psychiatric-Mental Health Nursing](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 20: Eating Disorders](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 6  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

**Pica** is the persistent eating of non-nutritive, non-food substances — things with no nutritional value that are not food — such as earth or clay (geophagia), ice (pagophagia), starch (amylophagia), paper, chalk, soap, sand, or hair (trichophagia). For the diagnosis, the behavior must persist at least one month, be developmentally inappropriate, not be culturally sanctioned, and — when it occurs alongside another condition — be severe enough to warrant additional clinical attention. Pica can appear in children, in pregnant people, and in people with intellectual or developmental disabilities or autism; it can also accompany iron-deficiency anemia, with which ice craving has a well-documented association — though the direction of that relationship is still debated.

Crucially, not every mouthful of a non-food item is pica. Infants and toddlers explore the world by mouthing objects — normal development, not pica. Likewise, some communities have long-standing cultural practices of eating earth or clay, and the criteria explicitly exclude culturally sanctioned practices. Deciding what counts as pica requires knowing the person's developmental stage, culture, and medical picture — which is why the diagnosis belongs to qualified providers.

The word "pica" comes from the Latin name of the magpie, a bird said to eat almost anything. Historians note that 19th-century descriptions of geophagy in the American South were sometimes entangled with the racial ideologies of the era — a reminder that ideas about "normal" versus "disordered" eating are shaped by culture and power.

## Why This Matters

Pica matters because the substances people eat can be genuinely dangerous. Lead-based paint chips and contaminated soil can cause lead poisoning; hair can form a bezoar that blocks the intestine; soil can carry parasites and bacteria; sharp objects can injure the mouth and teeth. Nurses meet pica across settings — pediatric clinics, prenatal care, school health, and inpatient units where environmental safety is a constant concern. In inpatient psychiatric settings, pica is a safety issue: the nurse's role includes environmental precautions, observation, documentation, and reporting per policy. Because pica can signal an underlying condition, recognizing and reporting it can uncover a treatable problem.

## Core Concepts

### The diagnostic picture

The essential features are eating non-nutritive, non-food substances; persistence for at least one month; behavior inappropriate to the person's developmental level; and exclusion of culturally sanctioned or socially normative practices. The substance eaten must not be food, and the behavior must be severe enough to need clinical attention on its own when it occurs alongside another condition. Diagnosis is made by qualified providers after assessment, usually including medical evaluation for complications.

### Common substances and their particular risks

Different substances carry different dangers. **Geophagia** (earth, clay): soil can contain lead, parasites, and bacteria; in pregnancy, soil-borne infection is a special concern. **Pagophagia** (ice): usually harmless, but persistent ice craving is associated with iron-deficiency anemia — one of the most consistent findings in the pica literature — and is worth investigating. **Amylophagia** (starch): supplies calories without nutrients. **Trichophagia** (hair): hair is not digested and can form a **trichobezoar** — a hairball-like mass causing abdominal pain, vomiting, and bowel obstruction. **Paint chips and lead-contaminated materials**: a classic cause of childhood lead poisoning (follow local screening guidelines).

### Populations and contexts

Pica is seen in young children (beyond normal mouthing), pregnant people, people with intellectual or developmental disabilities and autism, and people with schizophrenia; it can also accompany iron-deficiency anemia. Explanations include micronutrient-deficiency theories and sensory or cultural accounts; the evidence for each is mixed, and the deficiency hypothesis has been debated for decades.

### Nursing considerations: safety, assessment, and education

Nursing care is built on observation, environmental safety, education, and teamwork. The nurse takes a careful history — what substances, how much, how often, for how long, in what settings — and documents observations neutrally; checks for complications (abdominal pain, vomiting, constipation, dental injury, lead-exposure risk) and reports them; and follows facility policy on environmental precautions. Education for caregivers focuses on harm reduction: keeping toxic substances out of reach, offering safe alternatives for mouthing, and seeking medical evaluation rather than punishment. When a toxic substance has been ingested, the nurse escalates immediately per facility policy, which may include contacting the regional poison center.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Pica is when a person keeps eating things that are not food — like dirt, chalk, ice, or paper — for a month or more, even when it can make them sick. Babies put everything in their mouths to learn, and some cultures eat certain earths on purpose, so it is only pica when it is not normal for that person's age or culture. Eating non-food stuff can be dangerous, so the person needs help to stay safe.

## High-Yield Points

- Pica = persistent eating of non-nutritive, non-food substances for at least one month, developmentally inappropriate, and not culturally sanctioned.
- Common forms: geophagia (earth/clay), pagophagia (ice), amylophagia (starch), trichophagia (hair).
- Normal mouthing in infants and toddlers is **not** pica; culturally sanctioned geophagy is **not** pica.
- Ice craving (pagophagia) is associated with iron-deficiency anemia — a treatable clue worth reporting.
- Hair can form a trichobezoar causing obstruction; paint chips and contaminated soil can cause lead poisoning; soil can carry parasites.
- Pica occurs in children, pregnant people, people with developmental disabilities, and people with schizophrenia.
- The deficiency theory of pica has mixed evidence — treat explanations as hypotheses, not settled facts.
- Nursing role: observe, document, keep the environment safe per policy, educate caregivers without blame, and escalate ingestions (e.g., poison center per policy).

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-language definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Pica | Persistent eating of non-nutritive, non-food substances for at least a month | The core topic; dangerous because of the substances involved |
| Geophagia | Eating earth, clay, or soil | Can expose the person to lead, parasites, and bacteria; culturally sanctioned in some communities |
| Pagophagia | Eating ice | Associated with iron-deficiency anemia — a clue worth reporting for evaluation |
| Amylophagia | Eating starch (laundry starch, cornstarch) | Supplies calories without nutrients and can displace nutritious food |
| Trichophagia | Eating hair | Can form a trichobezoar that obstructs the bowel |
| Culturally sanctioned practice | Eating behavior that is an accepted part of a community's tradition | Excluded from the diagnosis — culture must be considered before labeling |

## Example / Scenario

A pediatric nurse is doing a routine well-child visit for a 4-year-old. The mother mentions, half-laughing, "He eats dirt in the backyard all the time. I tell him to stop, but he just does it anyway." The nurse takes this seriously without alarming the mother: she asks how long it has been happening and how often — the answers point to daily dirt-eating over several months. The nurse explains that mouthing is normal for babies, but at four, daily eating of dirt deserves a look, especially because soil can contain lead or parasites. She asks about the home environment (older paint, lead risk), documents the history, and discusses an evaluation that may include lead screening per local guidelines. She gives the mother practical, non-judgmental harm-reduction steps and reassures her this is not a parenting failure. Her role: take the history, recognize when mouthing has crossed into something concerning, report, and support the family — not diagnose or punish.

## Common Confusions

| Do not confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Pica | Normal mouthing in infants and toddlers | Mouthing is developmentally expected exploration; pica is persistent, developmentally inappropriate, and lasts at least a month |
| Pica | Culturally sanctioned geophagy | Eating earth as an accepted community practice is explicitly excluded from the diagnosis |
| Pagophagia (ice eating) | "Just liking ice" | Persistent ice craving warrants checking for iron-deficiency anemia — the association is well documented |
| Pica | Rumination disorder | Pica is eating non-food substances; rumination is regurgitating and re-chewing food — separate diagnoses |
| Trichophagia | Trichotillomania (hair-pulling disorder) | Trichophagia is eating hair; trichotillomania is pulling hair out — different behaviors that sometimes co-occur |
| Pica as misbehavior | Pica as a clinical condition | Scolding does not treat pica; it needs evaluation for causes, complications, and safety planning |
