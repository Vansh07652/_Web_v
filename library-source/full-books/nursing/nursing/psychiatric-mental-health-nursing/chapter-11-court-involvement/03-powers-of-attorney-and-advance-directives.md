# Powers of Attorney and Advance Directives

> **Book:** [Psychiatric-Mental Health Nursing](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 11: Court Involvement](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 3  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

A **power of attorney (POA)** is a legal document in which one person — the **principal** — authorizes another person — the **agent** or attorney-in-fact — to act on the principal's behalf for specified matters. An **advance directive** is a broader category of documents that records a person's wishes about future health care in case they later lose the ability to communicate or decide. The two ideas overlap: a durable power of attorney for health care *is* a type of advance directive, alongside living wills and psychiatric advance directives (PADs).

These documents matter in psychiatric-mental health nursing because many mental health conditions are **fluctuating**: a person may have full decision-making capacity during a period of stability and lose it temporarily during an episode of illness. Advance planning lets a person's own values and treatment preferences guide care even when they cannot speak for themselves. The documents do not take rights away — they *extend* a person's self-determination into a future moment when they need help exercising it. Specific rules (who may witness, when a document takes effect, whether a PAD is honored) vary by state and by country, so nurses always apply their facility's policy and local law.

## Why This Matters

Nurses are often the first clinicians to ask about advance directives on admission, to place the documents in the chart, and to notice when a planned treatment conflicts with a documented preference. Honoring advance directives protects client autonomy and prevents unwanted treatment; ignoring them can cause harm, distress, and legal liability. In court-involvement situations, a valid advance directive can also *prevent* a guardianship proceeding — the least restrictive way to protect a person's choices is often the document the person already signed. For the NCLEX and practice, this topic tests the difference between capacity and competency, the types of POAs, and the nurse's role in witnessing, educating, and reporting — not making legal determinations.

## Core Concepts

### Capacity versus competency

**Capacity** is a clinical, decision-specific judgment: does this person, right now, understand the relevant information, appreciate the situation, reason through options, and express a choice? A nurse or provider assesses capacity for a *specific* decision (e.g., "can this client consent to this medication?"), and capacity can fluctuate. **Competency** is a legal status decided by a judge about overall decision-making rights. Nurses assess and document capacity; courts decide competency. A person can have capacity for some decisions and not others, and capacity can change hour to hour — which is exactly why advance planning exists.

### Living wills and health care directives

A **living will** is an instruction directive: it records what treatments a person would or would not want in future situations (for example, preferences about resuscitation, feeding tubes, or psychiatric treatment). It speaks for the person only when they cannot. A **durable power of attorney for health care** (also called a health care proxy or medical power of attorney) is a proxy directive: it names an agent who makes health care decisions for the principal when the principal cannot. Many states combine both in one "advance health care directive." In mental health, a **psychiatric advance directive (PAD)** records treatment preferences specific to mental health care — preferred hospitals or providers, medications the person has found helpful or harmful, people to contact, and who should be notified.

### Durable versus springing powers of attorney

A **durable POA** remains in effect — or takes effect — even if the principal later becomes incapacitated; durability is what makes it useful for future planning. A **springing POA** only "springs" into effect when a specified condition occurs, usually the principal's incapacity, often confirmed by one or more physicians. A non-durable POA ends automatically if the principal becomes incapacitated, which makes it useless for advance planning. POAs can also be limited to specific domains (finances, real estate, health care) rather than general. Crucially, a financial POA does **not** automatically give authority over health care decisions — the document must say so.

### What the agent may and may not do

An agent is a **fiduciary**: legally obligated to act in the principal's best interest and to follow the principal's expressed wishes. The agent cannot simply do whatever they want, and the authority is revocable by the principal while the principal has capacity. Agents cannot make decisions the document does not authorize, and in many jurisdictions an agent cannot override a PAD's clear preferences without legal process.

### The nurse's role

The nurse's role is **education, assessment, documentation, and reporting** — never legal advice. On admission, ask whether advance directives exist, offer the facility's information, ensure copies are scanned into the chart, and verify the document is current and witnessed per policy. If a client wants to create one, refer to the provider, social worker, or patient advocate. If a treatment plan conflicts with a documented directive, the nurse **reports** the conflict up the chain (provider, ethics committee, legal/risk management per policy) rather than ignoring or unilaterally resolving it. Scope and jurisdictional variation apply: what a nurse may witness, who must sign, and how PADs are honored differ by state and country.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

An advance directive is like writing a note to your future self: "If I can't say what I want someday, here is what I want, and here is the friend I trust to help decide." A power of attorney is like giving that friend a key to your decisions — but only for the doors you choose. The note and the key both make sure your wishes are followed even when you can't speak up.

## High-Yield Points

- **Capacity** is a clinical, decision-specific assessment; **competency** is a legal status decided by a court.
- A **living will** states wishes; a **health care POA** names a decision-maker; a **psychiatric advance directive (PAD)** applies these ideas to mental health treatment.
- **Durable** POAs survive incapacity; **non-durable** POAs end at incapacity; **springing** POAs activate upon incapacity.
- A financial POA does **not** authorize health care decisions unless the document explicitly says so.
- The agent is a fiduciary — obligated to follow the principal's wishes, not their own.
- Advance directives can prevent or delay guardianship — they are the least-restrictive planning tool.
- Nurse role: ask, educate, document, report conflicts; never give legal advice or make competency determinations.
- Rules vary by state/country and facility policy — know your jurisdiction, and flag PAD enforceability questions for the care team.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-language definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Principal | The person who creates a power of attorney or advance directive | The document exists to protect *their* wishes |
| Agent / attorney-in-fact | The person authorized to act for the principal | Must act as a fiduciary following the principal's wishes |
| Capacity | Clinical judgment about ability to understand, appreciate, reason, and choose for a specific decision | Fluctuates over time; assessed, not legally declared |
| Competency | Legal status of decision-making rights, decided by a judge | Only a court can change it |
| Living will | Instruction directive stating future treatment wishes | Speaks for the person when they cannot |
| Durable power of attorney for health care | Proxy directive naming a health care decision-maker | Survives incapacity; the most common planning document |
| Psychiatric advance directive (PAD) | Advance directive specific to mental health treatment preferences | Preserves autonomy during psychiatric crises |
| Springing POA | POA that activates only when a defined condition (usually incapacity) occurs | Timing of activation must be verified |
| Fiduciary | Legal duty to act in another's best interest | Limits what an agent may do |

## Example / Clinical Scenario

Ms. Alvarez, a 38-year-old with a long history of bipolar I disorder, completes a psychiatric advance directive during a period of stability. She documents that she prefers to be treated at her local community hospital, that lithium has worked well for her and quetiapine causes unacceptable side effects, and that her sister Rosa should be contacted first. She also signs a durable power of attorney for health care naming Rosa as her agent.

Eight months later Ms. Alvarez is brought to the emergency department during a manic episode with poor judgment and inability to cooperate with care. The admitting nurse asks about advance directives, finds the documents in the electronic record, and confirms they are current and properly witnessed. The treatment team reviews her PAD: the team can honor her preferred hospital placement and medication preferences, and Rosa is notified and consulted as agent. The nurse documents that the directives were reviewed and reported to the provider; she does not attempt to interpret legal validity herself. If a proposed treatment had conflicted with the PAD, the nurse's next step would be to report the conflict to the provider and follow facility policy — including, if needed, the ethics committee or legal/risk management. The scenario shows the nurse's real power: knowing the documents exist, making sure they are followed, and escalating conflicts rather than deciding them alone.

## Common Confusions

| Do not confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Capacity | Competency | Capacity is a clinical assessment for one decision and can fluctuate; competency is a legal status decided by a judge |
| Living will | Last will and testament | A living will addresses future *health care*; a last will disposes of property after death |
| Durable POA | Non-durable POA | Durable survives incapacity; non-durable ends at incapacity |
| Financial POA | Health care POA | Authority is document-specific; financial authority does not include health care decisions unless stated |
| POA | Advance directive | A POA appoints a decision-maker; an advance directive records wishes — a durable health care POA is both |
| "The agent can do anything" | Agent's fiduciary limits | Agents must follow the principal's wishes and only act within the document's scope |
| PADs are honored everywhere | State/country variation | PAD enforceability and procedures differ by jurisdiction — verify local law and facility policy |
