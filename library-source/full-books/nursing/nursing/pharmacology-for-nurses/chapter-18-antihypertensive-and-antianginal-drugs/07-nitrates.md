# Nitrates

> **Book:** [Pharmacology for Nurses](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 18: Antihypertensive and Antianginal Drugs](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 7  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Nitrates are a drug class used mainly to relieve and prevent **angina** — the chest pain that happens when the heart muscle does not get enough oxygen. The class includes **nitroglycerin** (sublingual tablets/spray for acute attacks; transdermal patch or ointment for prevention) and the longer-acting **isosorbide** preparations (isosorbide dinitrate, isosorbide mononitrate).

Nitrates work through a remarkable mechanism: the body converts them into **nitric oxide (NO)**, a gas that relaxes smooth muscle. The most important effect is **venodilation** — widening the veins, which reduces the amount of blood returning to the heart (**preload**). Less blood coming back means the heart's chambers fill less, stretch less, and need less oxygen. Nitrates also dilate coronary arteries and, at higher concentrations, arterioles, reducing **afterload**. The net effect: the heart's oxygen demand falls, and in many cases coronary blood flow improves — and the chest pain resolves.

## Why This Matters

- **Acute chest pain is a nursing emergency:** Knowing how nitrates work — and how to respond when a person uses them — is basic competency for any nurse who works with people who have coronary artery disease.
- **Route determines role:** The same drug comes in different forms for different jobs: sublingual for fast relief of an attack, patch/ointment for round-the-clock prevention. Confusing the roles is a classic error.
- **Tolerance is real:** Continuous nitrate exposure makes blood vessels stop responding — the "nitrate-free interval" (a daily gap in patch/ointment use) exists to prevent this. Nurses who apply patches need to understand why the patch comes off at night.
- **A potentially fatal interaction:** Combining nitrates with **phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE-5) inhibitors** (drugs for erectile dysfunction such as sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil) can cause a profound, dangerous drop in blood pressure. Asking about these drugs is a life-saving nursing habit.
- **Headache is the price of admission:** Nitrate headaches are common and can scare people into stopping the drug; anticipating and normalizing them improves adherence.

## Core Concepts

### Why angina happens

Angina occurs when **myocardial oxygen demand exceeds supply**. Demand rises with heart rate, blood pressure (afterload), and the stretch of the heart wall (preload). Supply falls when coronary arteries are narrowed by plaque (atherosclerosis) or spasm. The imbalance causes the muscle to become ischemic — and ischemia is felt as chest pressure, squeezing, or discomfort that may radiate to the arm, jaw, or back.

### How nitrates fix the imbalance

Nitrates are **prodrugs**: the body converts them to **nitric oxide**, which activates an enzyme (guanylate cyclase) inside smooth-muscle cells, raising a signaling molecule called cGMP that relaxes the muscle. The dominant clinical effects:

- **Venodilation → reduced preload.** Veins hold most of the blood volume; relaxing them pools blood away from the heart. The heart fills less, wall stretch falls, and **oxygen demand drops** — this is the primary antianginal mechanism.
- **Coronary vasodilation → improved supply.** Nitrates relax coronary arteries and relieve spasm, improving flow to ischemic zones (especially useful in vasospastic angina).
- **Arteriolar dilation → reduced afterload** (at higher doses). Less resistance means the heart pushes against less pressure — a secondary demand reduction, and the basis of the blood-pressure-lowering effect seen in some uses.

### The forms and their jobs

- **Sublingual nitroglycerin (tablet or spray):** absorbed quickly through the mouth's lining; used to **relieve an acute attack** within minutes. Classic teaching: sit down, use the dose as prescribed, and call for emergency help if the pain does not improve or worsens — the specific instructions (number of doses, timing, when to activate EMS) come from the prescriber and institutional protocol.
- **Transdermal patch or ointment:** delivers nitrate continuously for **prevention** of attacks — used on a schedule that includes a **nitrate-free interval** (commonly overnight) to prevent tolerance. Applied to hairless skin, rotated between sites, and removed per the schedule.
- **Oral isosorbide preparations:** longer-acting nitrates for prevention; subject to the same tolerance concern, so schedules build in gaps.

### Tolerance: why the drug "stops working"

With continuous exposure, vascular smooth muscle becomes **tolerant** — the same dose produces less effect because the tissue can no longer generate as much nitric oxide activity. The remedy is a **nitrate-free interval** of roughly 10–12 hours per day, which restores responsiveness. This is why patches are worn for a prescribed period and then removed, and why the nurse must follow (and teach) the exact schedule.

### Adverse effects and nursing vigilance

- **Headache:** very common, especially early in therapy; often fades with continued use. Teach the person it is expected and report if severe or persistent.
- **Hypotension and dizziness:** venodilation drops blood pressure; orthostatic hypotension and **syncope** are risks. Sit or lie down when using sublingual nitroglycerin, rise slowly, and report lightheadedness.
- **Reflex tachycardia and flushing:** the pressure drop can trigger a compensatory heart-rate increase (raising oxygen demand); facial flushing reflects vasodilation.
- **The PDE-5 inhibitor interaction:** sildenafil, tadalafil, and vardenafil amplify nitrate-induced vasodilation and can cause life-threatening hypotension. Nitrates and PDE-5 inhibitors are generally not combined; always ask about these drugs and confirm the timeline with the prescriber/pharmacist if there is any question.
- **Storage of sublingual tablets:** heat, light, and moisture degrade nitroglycerin; store in the original container and replace per the expiration guidance — check the product label and pharmacy instructions.

### Person-first, scope-aware care

Talk about "a person with angina who uses nitroglycerin," not "a nitrate patient." The chest-pain action plan (dose, repeat timing, when to call for emergency help), the nitrate-free schedule, and who may initiate therapy vary by guideline, state nurse practice act, and institutional policy. Educational content describes the class; **actual doses, intervals, and decisions must always be verified against current references, the facility formulary, and the prescriber's orders.**

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Your heart is a muscle that needs oxygen when it works hard, just like your leg muscles when you run. Angina is the pain your heart feels when it can't get enough oxygen. Nitrate medicine turns into a special gas that tells your veins to get wider — like opening a wider straw. Wider veins mean less blood piles up in the heart, so the heart doesn't stretch as much, needs less oxygen, and the pain goes away.

## High-Yield Points

- **Mechanism:** nitrates → nitric oxide → smooth-muscle relaxation. The dominant effect is **venodilation → reduced preload → lower myocardial oxygen demand**; coronary dilation improves supply (key for vasospastic angina).
- **Forms:** sublingual (acute relief, fast onset), transdermal patch/ointment and oral isosorbide (prevention, slower onset).
- **Tolerance:** continuous exposure makes nitrates stop working; a **nitrate-free interval** (commonly ~10–12 hours, often overnight with patches) restores effect — follow the prescribed schedule.
- **PDE-5 inhibitor interaction:** sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil + nitrates can cause severe, life-threatening hypotension — ask directly about these drugs.
- **Headache is common** and expected, especially early; it usually fades — teach rather than let the person stop the drug.
- **Hypotension/orthostasis:** sit or lie down when using sublingual nitroglycerin; rise slowly; report dizziness or fainting.
- **Angina is a warning sign:** teach the person's prescribed action plan — including when to seek emergency care if pain persists or worsens.
- **Storage matters:** sublingual nitroglycerin degrades with heat, light, and moisture; keep in the original container and follow expiration guidance.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-language definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Angina | Chest pain from an imbalance between myocardial oxygen demand and supply | The condition nitrates treat; recognizing it (and when it is not improving) is a safety skill |
| Nitric oxide (NO) | A gas produced from nitrates that relaxes smooth muscle | The active molecule behind all nitrate effects |
| Preload | The volume of blood returning to the heart before contraction | Reduced by venodilation — the primary nitrate mechanism that lowers oxygen demand |
| Afterload | The resistance the heart pushes against to eject blood | Reduced at higher nitrate doses — a secondary demand-lowering effect |
| Prodrug | An inactive drug converted into its active form in the body | Nitrates only work after conversion to nitric oxide |
| Tolerance | Loss of drug effect with continuous exposure | Why nitrates need a nitrate-free interval to keep working |
| Nitrate-free interval | A daily gap in nitrate exposure that restores responsiveness | The reason patches come off and oral schedules build in breaks |
| PDE-5 inhibitor | Drug class for erectile dysfunction (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil) | Combining with nitrates causes dangerous hypotension — a critical screening question |
| Ischemia | Inadequate oxygen supply to tissue | The underlying problem in angina; relieved when nitrate therapy restores the demand/supply balance |

## Example: The Chest Pain Action Plan

Mr. Brooks, age 66, has stable angina and carries sublingual nitroglycerin spray. During a home-care visit, the nurse reviews his chest-pain plan with him and his wife:

1. **The plan (as prescribed):** when chest pain starts, Mr. Brooks sits down, uses the spray as ordered, and waits the prescribed interval. If the pain does not improve — or worsens — he calls emergency services. The nurse confirms his instructions match the prescriber's plan and the discharge teaching.
2. **The teaching check:** the nurse asks Mr. Brooks to demonstrate how he uses the spray. She notices it is stored in his car's glove box — hot in summer, cold in winter, exactly the heat/moisture exposure that degrades nitroglycerin. She teaches him to keep it in the original container, at room temperature, within reach, and to check the expiration date.
3. **The screening question:** the nurse asks about all medications, including anything "for erectile dysfunction." Mr. Brooks hesitates and admits he borrowed a friend's sildenafil last month. The nurse explains the interaction risk in plain language: nitrates plus that drug can drop blood pressure dangerously. She documents the report and flags it for the prescriber and pharmacist.
4. **Headache reassurance:** Mr. Brooks mentions the spray gives him a headache. The nurse confirms this is a common, expected effect that often lessens with continued use — and tells him to report headaches that are severe or persistent.

The lesson: the drug works, but its safe use lives in the details — correct storage, correct action plan, and the questions nurses ask that patients don't volunteer.

> **Safety note:** This scenario describes a drug class, teaching themes, and safety screening only. Actual doses, spray use instructions, and emergency thresholds vary by product, guideline, and institution — always verify against current references, the formulary, and the prescriber's orders.

## Common Confusions

| Do not confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Sublingual nitroglycerin | Transdermal patch | Sublingual = fast relief of an acute attack; patch = slow, continuous prevention (with a nitrate-free interval). They are not interchangeable |
| Nitrate headache | A sign the drug is wrong | Headache is an expected vasodilation effect, especially early in therapy; it usually fades — report if severe or persistent |
| Tolerance | Addiction | Tolerance is loss of effect with continuous exposure (fixed by a nitrate-free interval); nitrates are not addictive |
| Nitrates | Other "heart pain" drugs (e.g., beta-blockers, CCBs) | All reduce myocardial oxygen demand, but nitrates do it mainly by cutting preload via venodilation; beta-blockers by slowing the heart; CCBs by relaxing vessels and slowing the heart — different mechanisms, sometimes combined |
| "The patch works better if I never take it off" | Following the prescribed removal schedule | Continuous exposure causes tolerance — the patch must come off per the schedule so the drug keeps working |
| Asking about PDE-5 inhibitors | Optional or embarrassing | It is a life-saving screening question; hypotension from the combination can be fatal |
