# Antiplatelets

> **Book:** [Pharmacology for Nurses](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 20: Anticoagulant, Antiplatelet, and Thrombolytic Drugs](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 3  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

**Antiplatelet drugs** target **primary hemostasis** — the platelet plug. Platelets are the first responders of clotting: at a site of vessel injury (or plaque rupture inside an artery), they stick to the damaged surface, release recruiting signals, and lock together via fibrinogen to form a plug (see [Introduction to Clotting and Coagulation](01-introduction-to-clotting-and-coagulation.md)). Antiplatelets interfere with those steps, making plugs less likely. Because arterial thrombi — behind heart attacks, ischemic strokes, and stent blockages — are **platelet-rich**, antiplatelets are the cornerstone of *arterial* thromboprophylaxis and treatment, unlike anticoagulants (fibrin cascade) and thrombolytics (existing clots). The class includes **aspirin**, the **P2Y12 receptor blockers** (clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor), the intravenous **GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors** (abciximab, eptifibatide, tirofiban), and the phosphodiesterase inhibitors **dipyridamole** and **cilostazol**.

## Why This Matters

Cardiovascular disease — driven largely by arterial thrombosis — is the leading cause of death worldwide, and antiplatelets are among the most widely used medicines. Aspirin is taken daily by millions for secondary prevention after a heart attack or stroke, and antiplatelets keep coronary stents open: stopping them prematurely can cause acute stent thrombosis. The mechanism that prevents arterial clots also raises bleeding risk — bruising, GI bleeding, and bleeding around surgery. Nurses need to know *which step each drug blocks*, *how long the effect lasts* (aspirin's is irreversible, for the life of the platelet), and *what to teach* (adherence, bleeding precautions). Doses, schedules, and administration must be verified against current references, the institutional formulary, and prescriber orders.

## Core Concepts

### Aspirin: the irreversible COX-1 inhibitor

Aspirin inhibits **cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1)**, the enzyme platelets use to make **thromboxane A₂**, a powerful recruiting signal. The inhibition is **irreversible**: because platelets cannot make new COX-1 (they have no nucleus), the effect lasts for the platelet's lifetime — about a week to ten days. That duration is why aspirin works at low, antiplatelet doses and must be stopped well ahead of elective surgery (timing is protocol- and prescriber-driven). Because aspirin also blocks COX-1 in the stomach lining, GI irritation and bleeding are classic concerns; current evidence supports aspirin far more for *secondary* prevention than for primary prevention in low-risk people — verify current recommendations. There is no specific antidote.

### P2Y12 receptor blockers: cutting the ADP signal

Platelets respond to **ADP**, released from activated platelets, through the **P2Y12 receptor**. Blocking it suppresses activation and amplifies aspirin's effect — why the two are combined as **dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT)** after acute coronary syndromes and stents. The drugs differ in an exam-critical way:
- **Clopidogrel** is a **prodrug** — it must be converted to its active form by liver enzymes (CYP450), so its effect depends on that activation step.
- **Prasugrel** is also a prodrug but is activated more reliably.
- **Ticagrelor** is **direct-acting** (not a prodrug) and **reversible**, giving faster onset and offset.

These differences matter for drug interactions (drugs that inhibit the activating enzymes can blunt clopidogrel's effect) and for onset speed in acute settings. As with aspirin, there is no simple reversal agent.

### GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors: blocking the final handshake

The last step of aggregation is the "handshake": platelets bind to each other through **fibrinogen**, which attaches to their **GP IIb/IIIa** receptors. The IV agents **abciximab, eptifibatide, and tirofiban** block that receptor, stopping aggregation at the final common step regardless of which signal triggered it. Because they are potent IV drugs, they are reserved for high-risk, monitored settings — typically around percutaneous coronary intervention. Bleeding risk is significant; they are used only where close monitoring and protocol-driven care are available.

### Phosphodiesterase inhibitors: dipyridamole and cilostazol

**Dipyridamole** and **cilostazol** raise **cAMP** inside platelets by inhibiting phosphodiesterase, making them less responsive to activation. Dipyridamole is used (often with aspirin) for stroke prevention, and cilostazol for peripheral artery disease symptoms (it also dilates vessels).

### Nursing care across the class

- **Bleeding precautions**: teach people to watch for bruising, bleeding gums, dark stools, blood in urine, and prolonged cut bleeding.
- **Adherence is life-or-death after stents**: premature discontinuation can trigger acute stent thrombosis — teach people never to stop without talking to the prescriber, and coordinate surgery or dental work through the care team.
- **GI protection**: many people on aspirin need gastroprotective strategies — follow the prescriber's plan.
- **Interactions**: over-the-counter NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen) can interfere with aspirin's antiplatelet effect and add bleeding risk; teach people to check before adding any product.
- **Scope**: who may administer, teach, or monitor these drugs varies by institution and jurisdiction — practice within your scope and follow policy.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Platelets are like tiny soldiers that pile up to patch a cut — but inside a narrowed artery, they can pile up where they shouldn't and block the pipe. Antiplatelet medicines make the soldiers less eager to grab each other, so they are less likely to form a dangerous pile. The trade-off: when you get cut, the patch takes longer to build, so you may bleed longer.

## High-Yield Points

- Antiplatelets target the **platelet plug** — the strategy for **arterial** disease (MI, stroke, stents); anticoagulants target the fibrin cascade; thrombolytics dissolve existing clots.
- **Aspirin** irreversibly inhibits **COX-1** → less **thromboxane A₂**; effect lasts the platelet's lifetime; GI bleeding is a key risk; no specific antidote.
- **P2Y12 blockers** cut the ADP signal: **clopidogrel** (prodrug), **prasugrel** (prodrug, reliable activation), **ticagrelor** (direct, reversible).
- **Aspirin + P2Y12 blocker = DAPT**, standard after acute coronary syndromes and stents; stopping early is high-risk.
- **GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors** (abciximab, eptifibatide, tirofiban) block the final fibrinogen cross-link; IV, high potency, monitored settings.
- **Dipyridamole / cilostazol** raise platelet cAMP to dampen aggregation.
- Nursing: bleeding precautions, strict adherence education (especially post-stent), GI protection, interaction awareness; verify dosing and administration against current references and orders.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Platelet plug | The clump of activated platelets that seals small vessel injuries | Primary hemostasis — the exact step antiplatelets interrupt |
| COX-1 | The enzyme platelets use to make thromboxane A₂ | Aspirin's target; blocking it cuts platelet activation |
| Thromboxane A₂ | A platelet signal that recruits more platelets | Explains why COX-1 inhibition weakens the plug-building response |
| P2Y12 receptor | The platelet receptor for ADP, a key activation signal | The target of clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor |
| DAPT | Dual antiplatelet therapy: aspirin + a P2Y12 blocker | Standard after ACS/stents; stopping early is high-risk |
| GP IIb/IIIa receptor | The platelet "handle" that fibrinogen grabs to link platelets | Final common step of aggregation; IV inhibitor target |
| Prodrug | An inactive drug converted by the body into its active form | Explains clopidogrel's dependence on liver enzymes and its onset differences |

## Example

Mr. Patel, 58, is admitted with a heart attack (acute coronary syndrome) and undergoes coronary stenting. His antiplatelet plan is **DAPT**: aspirin (irreversible COX-1 inhibition, cutting thromboxane A₂) plus clopidogrel (a prodrug that, once activated, blocks the P2Y12 receptor). The nurse's discharge teaching hits three points: (1) *Do not stop either drug without talking to the prescriber* — the stent is a foreign surface where platelets love to form plugs, and stopping early risks acute stent thrombosis; (2) *Watch for bleeding* — bruising, dark stools, bleeding gums; and (3) *Ask before adding any over-the-counter drug*, since some NSAIDs can blunt aspirin's antiplatelet effect. Months later, when Mr. Patel needs elective dental work, the care team coordinates his antiplatelet management per current guidelines — never on the nurse's own judgment.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Antiplatelet | Anticoagulant | Antiplatelets block the platelet plug (arterial disease); anticoagulants slow the clotting-factor cascade (venous disease, AF) |
| Antiplatelet | Thrombolytic | Antiplatelets *prevent* platelet plugs; thrombolytics *dissolve* existing fibrin clots |
| Aspirin (irreversible) | Other NSAIDs (reversible) | Aspirin permanently disables platelet COX-1 for the platelet's lifetime; other NSAIDs inhibit reversibly while present |
| Clopidogrel | Ticagrelor | Clopidogrel is a prodrug needing enzyme activation (slower, interaction-prone); ticagrelor is direct-acting and reversible (faster onset/offset) |
| Aspirin alone | DAPT | Aspirin alone is single-agent prevention; DAPT (aspirin + P2Y12 blocker) is standard after ACS/stents |
| Platelet plug | Fibrin clot | The plug is the fast, fragile platelet patch; the clot is the fibrin-reinforced stable structure |

## Quick Review

1. Why is aspirin's antiplatelet effect described as "irreversible," and roughly how long does it last?
2. What is the target of clopidogrel, and why does clopidogrel — but not ticagrelor — depend on liver enzyme activation?
3. What does DAPT stand for, and in what situations is it standard?
4. Which drug class blocks the final step of platelet aggregation, and why are those drugs given IV in monitored settings?
5. A person with a new coronary stent asks whether it is safe to skip their aspirin "just for a few days." What should the nurse teach, and why?

1. Aspirin permanently inactivates platelet COX-1, and platelets cannot synthesize new enzyme; the effect lasts for the platelet's lifetime (roughly a week to ten days).
2. Clopidogrel blocks the P2Y12 receptor (the ADP signal). It is a prodrug requiring CYP450 activation, whereas ticagrelor is direct-acting — hence differences in onset, offset, and interactions.
3. Dual antiplatelet therapy: aspirin plus a P2Y12 blocker; standard after acute coronary syndromes and coronary stenting.
4. GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors (abciximab, eptifibatide, tirofiban) block the fibrinogen cross-link between platelets — the final common step of aggregation. They are potent IV agents with significant bleeding risk, reserved for monitored settings such as PCI.
