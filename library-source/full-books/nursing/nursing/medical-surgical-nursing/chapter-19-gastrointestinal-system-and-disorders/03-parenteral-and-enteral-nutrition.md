# Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition

> **Book:** [Medical-Surgical Nursing](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 19: Gastrointestinal System and Disorders](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 3  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Not every patient can eat enough by mouth. A person recovering from major surgery, a stroke that caused trouble swallowing, a serious burn, or a disease that prevents the bowel from absorbing food may need nutrition delivered another way. This topic covers the two main routes of nutrition support: **enteral nutrition**, which delivers liquid formulas through the digestive tract (by mouth or through a tube into the stomach or intestine), and **parenteral nutrition**, which delivers nutrients directly into the bloodstream through an intravenous (IV) line.

The central rule of thumb: **if the gut works, use the gut.** Enteral nutrition is preferred whenever the digestive tract can safely handle food, because it keeps the intestines active, preserves the gut's protective barrier, uses the body's own digestive machinery, and carries lower infection risk than IV feeding. Parenteral nutrition is reserved for when the GI tract cannot be used — for example, during a bowel obstruction, after extensive bowel removal, or when the bowel must be kept at rest. Choosing the route, formula, and monitoring plan is an interprofessional decision (provider orders, registered dietitian calculation); the nurse's role is to administer safely, monitor tolerance, and communicate findings.

## Why This Matters

- **Malnutrition worsens every outcome.** Patients who cannot eat lose muscle, heal more slowly, and fight infection poorly; nutrition support is a cornerstone of recovery.
- **Enteral and parenteral feeding are common, high-stakes nursing skills.** Wrong tube placement, infection, or metabolic imbalance can be life-threatening.
- **Tube feeding is everywhere in med-surg.** Stroke, head injury, surgery, critical illness, and dysphagia all frequently lead to enteral feeding.
- **Parenteral nutrition is a high-alert therapy** requiring sterile technique, careful line care, and close monitoring.
- **Exam favorite:** enteral vs parenteral routes, indications, complications, and the nursing checks that keep patients safe.

## Core Concepts

### Enteral nutrition: feeding through the gut

Enteral nutrition means nutrients go through the GI tract — from **oral nutrition supplements** (drinks adding calories and protein) to **tube feeding**:

- **Nasogastric (NG) tube:** nose to stomach; used for short-term feeding (days to weeks).
- **Gastrostomy tube (e.g., PEG):** through the abdominal wall into the stomach; used for longer-term feeding.
- **Jejunostomy tube:** into the jejunum (upper small intestine); used when the stomach must be bypassed or aspiration risk is high.

Formulas may be given as **bolus** (a larger amount over a short time, like a meal), **intermittent** (several scheduled feeds), or **continuous** (a steady drip, often with a pump). Formula type (standard, high-protein, fiber-containing, disease-specific) is chosen by the provider and dietitian.

### Parenteral nutrition: feeding into the bloodstream

Parenteral nutrition delivers a sterile liquid containing carbohydrates, amino acids (protein), lipids (fat), electrolytes, vitamins, and trace elements directly into a vein — bypassing the gut entirely. It is used when the GI tract cannot be used (bowel obstruction, short bowel syndrome, severe malabsorption, or bowel rest). Two forms:

- **Peripheral parenteral nutrition (PPN):** through a regular peripheral IV, usually short-term.
- **Total parenteral nutrition (TPN):** through a central venous catheter (PICC or central line), because the concentrated solution can irritate smaller veins; supports longer-term or total nutrition needs.

Because parenteral nutrition puts a concentrated solution straight into the blood, it demands strict sterile technique, careful line care, and close monitoring for infection and metabolic problems.

### Nursing care during nutrition support

Nursing responsibilities cluster around **safety, tolerance, and monitoring:**

- **Verify tube placement before every use** of an enteral tube — giving formula into the lungs (aspiration) is a serious, preventable harm. Confirmation methods (pH testing, X-ray verification) follow institutional policy.
- **Position and protect:** keep the head of the bed elevated during and after feeding per policy to reduce aspiration risk; check tube position per protocol.
- **Monitor tolerance:** watch for nausea, vomiting, distention, diarrhea, constipation, and discomfort; document and report.
- **Track the big picture:** daily weights, intake and output, hydration status, and ordered labs. Refeeding a severely malnourished person can cause dangerous fluid and electrolyte shifts (**refeeding syndrome**), so feedings are started and advanced cautiously per orders.
- **Protect the IV line (parenteral):** sterile technique, intact dressings, clear labeling; never use a dedicated PN line for other purposes unless policy allows.
- **Involve the team:** provider orders, dietitian calculates, pharmacy compounds, nurse administers and monitors. Scope of practice for placement confirmation and PN administration varies by state and institution — follow facility policy.

### Transitioning

Nutrition support is rarely permanent. As a patient recovers, the team transitions care — from continuous tube feeding to bolus feeds, then to thickened oral foods with speech therapy, and finally to a normal diet. Many patients go home with feeding tubes, so teaching the patient and family is essential. Throughout, comfort, dignity, and the patient's goals shape how aggressively feeding is pursued.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Imagine your body is a car and food is fuel. If you can't chew and swallow, we can pour fuel down a straw straight into the gas tank — that's tube feeding (enteral nutrition), and it's the first choice when the engine can still use it. If the fuel tank itself is broken, we drip fuel directly into the engine through a hose — that's IV nutrition (parenteral nutrition). Either way, nurses make sure the fuel actually goes where it belongs and nothing gets clogged, infected, or spilled.

## High-Yield Points

- **"If the gut works, use it":** enteral nutrition is preferred over parenteral when the GI tract can safely be used.
- Enteral routes: NG (short term), gastrostomy (long term), jejunostomy (bypassing the stomach / high aspiration risk).
- Parenteral routes: PPN via peripheral IV (short term); TPN via central line (longer term / concentrated).
- **Verify enteral tube placement before every use** and elevate the head of the bed per policy — aspiration is a serious preventable harm.
- Monitor tolerance: nausea, vomiting, distention, diarrhea, constipation, and fluid balance; document and report.
- Refeeding syndrome is a real danger when severely malnourished people are fed aggressively — advance feedings cautiously per orders.
- Parenteral nutrition is high-alert: strict sterile technique, careful line care, monitoring for infection and metabolic complications.
- Nutrition support is interprofessional (provider, dietitian, pharmacy, nursing); scope and protocols vary by state and institution.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Enteral nutrition | Feeding through the digestive tract (oral supplements or tube feeding) | Preferred route when the gut works; keeps the gut active and preserves its barrier |
| Parenteral nutrition | Feeding directly into the bloodstream through an IV line | Used when the GI tract cannot be used; high-alert with infection and metabolic risks |
| Nasogastric (NG) tube | Tube from nose to stomach | Common short-term route; placement must be verified before each use |
| Gastrostomy tube | Tube through the abdominal wall into the stomach | Longer-term feeding; may lower aspiration risk versus NG in some patients |
| Jejunostomy tube | Tube into the jejunum | Bypasses the stomach; used when the stomach must be avoided |
| Total parenteral nutrition (TPN) | Complete IV nutrition through a central line | Supports total nutrition when the gut cannot be used |
| Bolus / continuous feeding | Feeding as discrete "meals" vs a steady drip | Delivery method affects tolerance, aspiration risk, and nursing workflow |
| Refeeding syndrome | Dangerous fluid/electrolyte shifts when malnourished people are refed quickly | Prevented by slow, monitored refeeding — a key safety concept |

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process

1. **Verify the order:** confirm formula, route, rate, and duration with the provider's order and the dietitian's plan.
2. **Check the tube (enteral):** confirm placement per institutional policy (pH testing or recent X-ray) before anything goes in.
3. **Position the patient:** elevate the head of the bed per policy and keep it elevated during and after feeding.
4. **Administer:** hang formula or flush the tube as ordered; use a pump for continuous feeds; strict sterile technique for parenteral lines.
5. **Monitor:** watch for intolerance signs, check weights and intake/output, and review ordered labs.
6. **Document and communicate:** record what was given and tolerated; report concerns to the provider promptly.
7. **Plan the transition:** work toward oral intake as the patient recovers; educate the patient/family for home care when applicable.

## Example

Ms. Carter, a person with a severe stroke, cannot swallow safely — a swallowing study confirms a high risk of food entering her lungs. The provider orders enteral nutrition through an NG tube. Before the first feeding, the nurse verifies tube placement per hospital policy, elevates the head of the bed, and starts the ordered continuous feed at the prescribed rate. Over the next days the nurse checks for nausea and distention, tracks weight and intake, and documents tolerance. When Ms. Carter develops diarrhea, the nurse reports it; the team adjusts the formula and rate, and the diarrhea resolves. Weeks later, speech therapy determines she can swallow again, and the team gradually transitions her from tube feeds to thickened liquids. The nurse coordinates each step and teaches Ms. Carter's family what to watch for at home. This is the full arc of nutrition support: start safely, monitor closely, adjust as needed, and always work toward recovery.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Enteral nutrition | Parenteral nutrition | Enteral goes through the GI tract (mouth/tube); parenteral goes into the bloodstream (IV) |
| NG tube | Gastrostomy tube | NG is temporary, nose-to-stomach; gastrostomy is a longer-term tube through the abdominal wall |
| TPN | PPN | TPN is concentrated, central line, longer-term/total support; PPN is peripheral, short-term |
| Bolus feeding | Continuous feeding | Bolus = discrete "meal" amounts; continuous = steady drip, often with a pump |
| Not eating by mouth | Not needing nutrition | A patient who cannot eat still needs nutrition — that is exactly when support is ordered |
