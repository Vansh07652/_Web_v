# Peripheral Vascular Disease

> **Book:** [Medical-Surgical Nursing](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 12: Cardiovascular System](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 7  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Peripheral vascular disease (PVD) is damage or dysfunction of the blood vessels *outside* the heart and brain — most importantly the arteries and veins of the legs. In everyday practice the term usually means **peripheral arterial disease (PAD)**: atherosclerotic narrowing of the arteries that supply the limbs, so that working muscles outstrip their oxygen supply. But PVD as a concept has two faces that must never be blurred: **arterial disease** (blocked pipes, starved tissue) and **venous disease** (leaky valves, pooled blood), which was introduced with chronic venous insufficiency in the previous topic. The two produce opposite symptoms, opposite physical findings, and opposite care plans, and comparing them is one of the highest-yield exercises in cardiovascular nursing.

PAD deserves special attention because it is common, underdiagnosed, and a warning sign for the whole body: a person with clogged leg arteries very likely has clogged coronary and carotid arteries too, so PAD predicts heart attack and stroke. It is also treatable — walking programs, risk-factor control, and foot protection can keep people walking and keep limbs intact. This topic covers PAD, the acute arterial occlusion, the classic comparison with chronic venous insufficiency, and the vasospastic and inflammatory conditions (Raynaud's phenomenon and Buerger's disease) that cause cold, painful fingers and toes.

## Why This Matters

Peripheral arterial disease is called the "forgotten cardiovascular disease" precisely because it is missed: many people dismiss their leg pain as aging or arthritis and never mention it, and the first sign can be a non-healing foot ulcer that ends in amputation. For the nurse, this topic matters at three levels. **Detection:** asking "do your legs hurt when you walk?" and checking foot pulses catches disease before tissue is lost. **Protection:** once PAD is known, meticulous foot care, proper footwear, and injury avoidance prevent the ulcers that lead to amputations. **Systemic risk:** identifying PAD flags a person at high risk for heart attack and stroke, which changes the whole prevention conversation. And because arterial and venous disease are treated oppositely, getting the comparison right is a safety issue: elevating the legs is helpful for venous disease but harmful for arterial disease.

## Core Concepts

### PAD: arteries narrowed by atherosclerosis

Peripheral arterial disease is the same process as coronary artery disease, moved to the limbs: fatty plaques narrow the arteries, most often in the aorto-iliac, femoral, and popliteal segments, reducing blood flow. The classic symptom is **intermittent claudication** — a cramping, aching, or burning pain in the calf (or thigh/buttock, depending on the level of the blockage) that comes on predictably with walking a certain distance and is relieved within a few minutes of rest. The pain is a demand problem: muscles need more oxygen during exercise, and the narrowed artery cannot deliver it. As disease worsens, pain comes on with less and less activity until it appears at rest, classically at night in the foot, relieved by hanging the leg over the bed (gravity helps). **Rest pain** signals critical ischemia — tissue is in danger.

### The physical examination story

The nurse's findings tell the story. In arterial disease the affected leg is cool, pale (especially when elevated — "elevation pallor"), and the pulses are weak or absent; the foot may be shiny, hairless, with thickened nails; with chronic severe disease the leg may look dusky red when dependent ("dependent rubor") because maximally dilated capillaries fill with sluggish blood. In venous disease, by contrast, the leg is warm, swollen, with brown hemosiderin staining and varicosities, and pulses are usually normal. This is the classic comparison table of vascular nursing, and it is worth memorizing cold.

### The six Ps of acute arterial occlusion

When an artery to a limb is suddenly blocked — by an embolus from the heart or a proximal aneurysm, or by in-situ thrombosis — the limb is in acute danger and the clock runs in hours, not days. The mnemonic is the **six Ps**: **pain** (sudden, severe), **pallor** (pale, then mottled), **pulselessness** (absent pulse beyond the block), **paresthesia** (numbness/tingling), **paralysis** (loss of movement — a late and ominous sign), and **poikilothermia** (the limb takes on room temperature). This is a surgical emergency: the nurse's role is immediate recognition, marking the level where pulses are lost, keeping the limb in a neutral/dependent position per protocol, and urgent escalation. Paralysis and sensory loss mean muscle is dying; delay costs the limb.

### Arterial versus venous ulcers

Ulcers put the whole picture together. **Arterial ulcers** sit on pressure points — toes, heels, lateral foot — are deep, punched-out, dry, and exquisitely painful (worse at night, worse with elevation), with a pale or necrotic base and little exudate. **Venous ulcers** sit above the medial ankle, are shallow, irregular, moist, and weepy, surrounded by brown staining and edema, and are less painful. The treatment directions are opposite: arterial ulcers need improved blood flow (revascularization decisions, walking programs, protecting the limb from injury), while venous ulcers need elevation, compression, and mobility. *Scope note:* ulcer care, dressings, and wound products are chosen by the care team per institutional protocol — the nurse's contribution is accurate assessment, documentation, infection vigilance, and teaching.

### Nursing care: protect the feet, keep the blood moving

For the person with PAD, nursing care is largely prevention teaching. Feet must be inspected daily (a mirror or family member helps), washed gently, dried carefully, and moisturized — but never between the toes. Shoes must fit; walking barefoot is discouraged; toenails should be cut by someone who can see well (straight across, not into the corners); heating pads and hot water bottles are avoided because the insensitive, ischemic foot can burn without the person noticing. A supervised walking program is a mainstay — walking to near-pain, resting, and repeating builds collateral circulation. Smoking cessation is the single most powerful intervention and deserves nonjudgmental, repeated support. Positioning is disease-specific: arterial legs are rested in a *dependent* position (gravity assists flow; elevation worsens pain), while venous legs are *elevated*. Knowing which disease is which is a patient-safety issue.

### Raynaud's phenomenon and Buerger's disease

Two less common but classic disorders round out the topic. **Raynaud's phenomenon** is episodic spasm of small arteries in the fingers (and toes), triggered by cold or stress, causing color changes — white, then blue, then red as flow returns — with numbness and tingling. It is usually primary (benign, common in young women) but can be secondary to conditions like scleroderma, in which case it is more severe. Teaching: keep the whole body warm, avoid triggers, protect hands. **Buerger's disease (thromboangiitis obliterans)** is a rare inflammatory disease of small and medium vessels, almost exclusively in people who smoke, causing pain, ischemia, and ulcers of fingers and toes that can progress to amputation; the essential intervention is complete smoking cessation.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Your leg arteries are like the fuel lines in a car. If the fuel line gets narrow and clogged, the car runs fine on flat roads but sputters going up a hill — that's leg pain when you walk. If the line gets completely blocked, the car stops and the engine can be ruined — that's an emergency. Keeping the fuel lines clean (not smoking, walking, healthy eating) keeps the car running.

## High-Yield Points

- PAD = atherosclerotic narrowing of limb arteries; a marker of whole-body atherosclerosis — predicts heart attack and stroke.
- Intermittent claudication: predictable calf pain with walking, relieved by rest; rest pain (night foot pain, relieved by dangling) signals critical ischemia.
- Six Ps of acute arterial occlusion: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia — an emergency with hours to act.
- Arterial leg: cool, pale, hairless, weak pulses, painful ulcers on toes/heels; venous leg: warm, swollen, stained, painless ulcers above the ankle.
- Positioning is opposite and safety-critical: arterial legs dependent, venous legs elevated.
- Foot care for PAD: daily inspection, proper footwear, no barefoot walking, no heating pads; smoking cessation is the most powerful intervention.
- Never assume leg pain in an older person is "just aging" — ask about walking pain and check pulses.
- Raynaud's: cold/stress-triggered finger color changes; Buerger's: smoking-linked inflammatory vessel disease.
- *Scope note:* revascularization, wound products, and anticoagulation are provider decisions; nursing delivers assessment, protection, and teaching.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-language definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) | Atherosclerotic narrowing of arteries supplying the limbs | Causes claudication and rest pain; flags heart attack/stroke risk |
| Intermittent claudication | Cramping leg pain with walking, relieved by rest | The hallmark early symptom of PAD — easy to dismiss, crucial to ask about |
| Rest pain | Ischemic pain at rest, classically at night in the foot | Signals critical limb ischemia — tissue is in danger |
| Six Ps | Pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia | The recognition checklist for acute arterial occlusion |
| Dependent rubor | Dusky red color when the ischemic leg hangs down | Classic sign of severe arterial disease |
| Hemosiderin staining | Brownish skin discoloration from pooled blood breakdown | Hallmark of chronic venous insufficiency |
| Raynaud's phenomenon | Episodic spasm of finger/toe arteries with cold or stress | Causes white-blue-red color changes; usually benign, sometimes secondary |
| Buerger's disease | Inflammatory vessel disease linked to smoking | Can destroy fingers/toes; requires complete smoking cessation |
| Critical limb ischemia | Severe PAD with rest pain, ulcers, or gangrene | The stage where limb loss is imminent without intervention |

## Example / Clinical Scenario

Mr. Santos, 67, a long-time smoker with type 2 diabetes, tells the nurse at his annual visit that his legs "get tired" walking to the mailbox and he blames "old age." The nurse asks the key question — *does the pain stop when you rest?* — and the answer is yes, after a few minutes. On exam, the nurse finds the right foot cool and pale, no palpable dorsalis pedis pulse, and the left foot warm with a palpable pulse; she documents the asymmetry. She suspects PAD and reports it so the provider can arrange an ankle-brachial index (a simple pressure comparison that is the standard screening test). Meanwhile she teaches foot care: daily inspection of both feet, well-fitting shoes, no walking barefoot, no heating pads — and she raises smoking cessation support, nonjudgmentally, as the single most important step. She also explains that the leg pain is a warning sign about the heart and brain too, so the coming workup matters beyond his legs. Her one question — "does it stop with rest?" — separates claudication from arthritis and starts the patient on a path that prevents an amputation.

## Common Confusions

| Do not confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Claudication | Arthritis / "old age" | Claudication is reproducible with exercise and *relieved by rest* — ask the question |
| Rest pain | Night cramps (charley horse) | Rest pain is in the *foot*, worse when flat, relieved by hanging the leg down; cramps are sudden muscle spasms |
| Arterial ulcer | Venous ulcer | Arterial: toes/heels, deep, dry, very painful, pale base; venous: above ankle, shallow, moist, less painful, stained skin |
| Arterial disease | Venous disease | Cool/pale/weak pulse vs warm/swollen/stained; positioning and care are opposites |
| Raynaud's | Buerger's disease | Raynaud's is vasospasm triggered by cold/stress (color changes); Buerger's is inflammatory, smoking-linked, destructive |
| PAD | DVT (previous topic) | PAD is chronic arterial narrowing (pain with exercise); DVT is acute venous clot (swelling, warmth, risk of PE) |
| "Pulse present" | "No PAD" | Pulses can be present with significant disease; symptoms and ankle-brachial index are the real tests |
