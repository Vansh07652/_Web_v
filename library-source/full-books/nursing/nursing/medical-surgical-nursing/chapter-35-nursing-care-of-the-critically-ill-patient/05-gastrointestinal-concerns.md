# Gastrointestinal Concerns

> **Book:** [Medical-Surgical Nursing](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 35: Nursing Care of the Critically Ill Patient](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 5  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

The gastrointestinal (GI) tract does far more than digest food: it absorbs nutrients, keeps fluid moving, houses much of the body's immune tissue, and acts as a barrier between gut contents and the rest of the body. In critical illness the gut becomes a vulnerable organ. When the body is stressed — by shock, sepsis, major surgery, or trauma — blood flow is redirected from the abdominal organs toward the heart and brain, bowel motility slows, and the lining of the stomach and intestines can be injured. Problems that start in the abdomen ripple outward: hidden bleeding, rising pressure inside the belly that squeezes the lungs and kidneys, and nutritional deficits that slow healing everywhere.

This topic maps the major GI concerns in critical illness — stress-related mucosal injury, ileus, abdominal compartment syndrome, GI bleeding, and nutrition delivery — and the assessment habits that catch them early. The guiding idea is *trend, not snapshot*: in a sedated or unstable patient, one exam tells you little; a series tells you a great deal.

## Why This Matters

- **The abdomen is a "silent" system in the ICU.** Critically ill patients are often sedated, ventilated, or too weak to report pain, so classic symptoms may be absent or masked. The nurse must rely on serial objective data: girth, bowel sounds, outputs, and lab trends.
- **GI complications are common and costly.** Stress-related mucosal injury, ileus, and feeding intolerance prolong ventilator days, ICU stays, and recovery.
- **Nutrition is part of the treatment plan.** Critically ill patients have high metabolic demands, and the team must feed them safely even when the gut struggles.

## Core Concepts

### The gut in the stress response

During hemodynamic instability, the body prioritizes blood flow to the brain and heart at the expense of the splanchnic (abdominal organ) circulation, leaving the gut lining hypoperfused and vulnerable to injury. **Bacterial translocation** — intestinal bacteria crossing a damaged gut lining into the bloodstream — is an area of active study, not a settled fact.

### Stress-related mucosal disease

Superficial erosions or ulcers of the stomach lining can develop in critically ill people, historically linked to physiologic stress. Because bleeding can be significant, many ICU patients receive prophylaxis per institutional protocol; drug choice, dosing, and starting thresholds vary — **a protocol-driven decision, not a universal one**. The nurse administers ordered therapy and watches for bleeding (coffee-ground or bloody nasogastric aspirate, black or tarry stools, falling hemoglobin trends, vital-sign changes), reporting promptly.

### Ileus and bowel function

Motility slows under anesthesia, opioids, sedatives, bed rest, electrolyte disturbances, and inflammation, producing **ileus**: the bowel's contractions weaken or stop without any physical blockage. The abdomen distends, bowel sounds fade, and feeding may not be tolerated. Ileus differs from a **mechanical bowel obstruction**, where something physically blocks the lumen (adhesions, tumor, hernia); management differs, and imaging plus serial exams help the team tell them apart.

### Abdominal compartment syndrome

Fluid, blood, edema, or severely distended bowel can raise pressure inside the abdominal cavity until it compresses organs and vessels — **abdominal compartment syndrome**. The squeezed kidneys produce less urine, the diaphragm is pushed up making breathing harder, and blood flow to abdominal organs falls. Risk factors include massive fluid resuscitation, abdominal surgery or trauma, and tense distension. Pressure is measured indirectly, commonly via a bladder catheter; technique and thresholds follow institutional protocol. Rising pressure with falling urine output or rising ventilator pressures warrants rapid escalation.

### GI bleeding in the ICU

Bleeding can come from stress-related mucosal injury, ulcers, esophageal varices, or procedure/device sites. It may be obvious — bloody emesis, melena (black, tarry stools) — or occult, detected only through trends: dropping hemoglobin, rising heart rate, falling blood pressure. The nurse tracks aspirates, stool, vital signs, and lab trends so the team can respond per protocol.

### Nutrition delivery

Critically ill patients need calories, protein, and micronutrients to heal. **Enteral nutrition** (feeding through the digestive tract — oral or via tube) is generally favored over **parenteral nutrition** (IV feeding) when the GI tract is usable, because using the gut preserves its function and is associated with fewer infectious complications. Protocols vary, and some patients cannot safely receive enteral feeding (e.g., bowel ischemia or obstruction). Nursing responsibilities: verify tube placement per policy, monitor tolerance (residuals, distension, nausea, diarrhea, constipation), position to reduce aspiration risk where feasible, and track ordered parameters.

### Assessment priorities: trend, not snapshot

Compare today's abdomen with yesterday's: girth at a consistent landmark, bowel sounds, stool and tube outputs, feeding tolerance, and lab trends such as lactate (a tissue-perfusion marker). Because the person may be sedated and unable to describe pain, objective trends and clear provider communication replace the typical ward exam's pain report.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

The belly works hard to digest food, but when someone is very sick, the body sends blood to the brain and heart first, so the stomach and intestines get less. That can cause bleeding inside the belly, bloating, and trouble digesting food. Nurses watch the belly closely — how big it looks, what sounds it makes, what comes out of tubes — to catch problems early and keep the sick person fed.

## High-Yield Points

- The abdomen is an unreliable reporter in critically ill patients: pain may be masked by sedation. Rely on serial objective data.
- Stress-related mucosal injury is a common ICU complication; prophylaxis and treatment follow institutional protocol — never assume a universal drug or dose.
- Distinguish **ileus** (motility failure, no blockage) from **mechanical obstruction** (physical blockage).
- Rising intra-abdominal pressure can compress the kidneys and lungs; report distension with falling urine output or rising ventilator pressures.
- GI bleeding may be occult; monitor aspirates, stool, vital signs, and hemoglobin trends.
- Enteral feeding is generally preferred when the GI tract is usable; verify tube placement and monitor tolerance per policy.
- Scope note: tube insertion, residual checks, and protocol decisions vary by state practice acts and facility policy.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-language definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Stress-related mucosal disease | Erosions or ulcers of the stomach lining during critical illness | A common, potentially bleeding ICU complication |
| Ileus | Bowel contractions slow or stop without a physical blockage | Causes distension and feeding intolerance; differs from obstruction |
| Mechanical bowel obstruction | Something physically blocks the intestine's lumen | May need imaging and sometimes surgical intervention |
| Abdominal compartment syndrome | Abnormally high belly pressure compressing organs and vessels | Can harm the kidneys and lungs; needs rapid team response |
| Enteral nutrition | Feeding through the digestive tract (oral or via tube) | Preferred when the gut works; preserves gut function |
| Parenteral nutrition | Nutrition given directly into the bloodstream (IV) | Used when the GI tract cannot be used; higher infection risk |
| Melena | Black, tarry stool from digested blood | A classic sign of upper GI bleeding |

## Example: The distended abdomen on day 3

A person is sedated and ventilated after major abdominal surgery. On day 3, the nurse measures the abdomen at the same landmark as yesterday: girth is up 4 cm, bowel sounds are absent, there has been no stool, and enteral feeding was stopped twice for large residuals. The nurse documents the trends and notifies the provider; the team discusses bowel rest, imaging to rule out mechanical obstruction, and alternative nutrition plans per protocol. The nurse's contribution is *pattern recognition*: a steadily growing girth with absent bowel sounds and feeding intolerance is a developing story. The nurse does **not** prescribe treatment, independently stop feeding, or diagnose obstruction — those are team decisions guided by institutional protocol.

## Common Confusions

| Do not confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Ileus | Mechanical bowel obstruction | Ileus = contractions stop without blockage; obstruction = physical blockage. Imaging and serial exams distinguish them |
| Enteral nutrition | Parenteral nutrition | Enteral uses the GI tract; parenteral bypasses it via IV |
| Stress-related mucosal injury | Chronic peptic ulcer disease | SRMD arises during critical illness; PUD is chronic with different causes (e.g., *H. pylori*, NSAIDs) |
| Any feeding intolerance | True GI failure | Some residuals are expected and managed per protocol; not every intolerance means feeding stops permanently |
| Visible bleeding | All GI bleeding | Bleeding can be occult, detected only through lab and vital-sign trends |
