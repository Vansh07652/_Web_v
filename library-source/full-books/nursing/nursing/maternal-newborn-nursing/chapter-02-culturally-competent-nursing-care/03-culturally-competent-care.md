# Culturally Competent Care

> **Book:** [Maternal-Newborn Nursing](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 2: Culturally Competent Nursing Care](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 3  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Culturally competent care is the practice of delivering care that genuinely fits the pregnant person's and family's cultural context — their beliefs, language, values, and expectations — across pregnancy, labor, birth, postpartum, newborn care, and even loss. It is not a personality trait or a checklist; it is a **process** of continually developing awareness, knowledge, and skill.

Birth is one of the most culturally loaded events in human life. Every society has rules about who should be present, what may be eaten, how pain is expressed, who touches whom, and what happens to the placenta and the newborn. When the nurse's approach collides with those rules, the pregnant person experiences fear, shame, or distrust — and may delay care, hide important information, or leave against advice. Culturally competent care prevents that collision and turns difference into dialogue. Related ideas sharpen the concept: **cultural humility** (a lifelong stance of learning and self-reflection) and **cultural safety** (care that the patient themselves experiences as respectful and non-diminishing).

## Why This Matters

- **Birth is cultural.** Respecting rituals and preferences — from birth position to placenta disposition to postpartum confinement — is core nursing care, not a luxury.
- **It is a safety issue.** Patients who feel unheard or judged are less likely to report symptoms, return for follow-up, or trust discharge teaching.
- **It is a legal and ethical issue.** Language access, informed consent, and non-discriminatory care are accreditation standards; policy and law define the specifics.
- **It reduces disparities.** Respectful care is one lever against documented inequities in maternal health outcomes.
- **Exam relevance.** Models of cultural competence (e.g., Campinha-Bacote, Giger–Davidhizar) and communication frameworks (e.g., LEARN) are classic test material.

## Core Concepts

### The cultural competence continuum

Competence is a developmental range. At one end is **cultural destructiveness** (attitudes that harm other cultures) and **incapacity** (unintentional discrimination); in the middle, **cultural blindness** (treating everyone "the same," which ignores real differences) and **pre-competence** (recognizing gaps and beginning to act); at the far end, **competence** (consistently responsive care) and **proficiency** (deeply internalized skill). Most nurses are working somewhere along this continuum — the goal is forward movement, not perfection.

### Campinha-Bacote: the process of cultural competence

This widely taught model holds that "cultural competence is a process, not an event." Its five interlocking constructs:

- **Cultural desire** — genuinely wanting to become competent.
- **Cultural awareness** — examining your own biases, assumptions, and heritage.
- **Cultural knowledge** — learning about other cultures' health beliefs and practices.
- **Cultural skill** — conducting a culturally based assessment (e.g., asking how the person explains their condition and what they expect from care).
- **Cultural encounters** — real, face-to-face cross-cultural interactions that refine all the others.

### Frameworks for organizing cultural assessment

- The **Giger–Davidhizar model** organizes culture into six phenomena: **communication, space, social organization, time, environmental control, and biological variations**. "Environmental control" covers beliefs about controlling illness; "time" covers clock time vs. event time (which affects appointment adherence).
- The **Purnell model** maps culture across a dozen domains — heritage, communication, family roles, nutrition, pregnancy and childbearing, death rituals, spirituality, health care practices — a comprehensive assessment menu.

### Cultural humility and cultural safety

- **Cultural humility** (Tervalon and Murray-García): a lifelong commitment to self-reflection and correcting power imbalances. Its key insight: you can never fully master another person's culture — you remain a learner, and the patient is the expert on their own life.
- **Cultural safety** (originating in New Zealand nursing): the *patient* decides whether care felt safe, respectful, and empowering. It shifts the measure from the provider's good intentions to the recipient's actual experience — an important test-trap distinction.

### Communication across cultures

- **Use trained medical interpreters** for language barriers — in person, by phone, or video, per facility policy. Family members, especially children, are poor substitutes: they may mistranslate, filter, breach privacy, or reverse family roles.
- The **LEARN framework** structures cross-cultural negotiation: **L**isten to the person's explanation, **E**xplain your understanding and perspective, **A**cknowledge differences and similarities, **R**ecommend a plan, **N**egotiate an agreement that respects both views.
- Nonverbal meanings vary — eye contact, touch, space, and silence. Observe and mirror the person's comfort level.
- Use the person's preferred name and pronouns; never assume relationship status, gender identity, or who makes decisions.

### Culturally competent care across the childbearing cycle

- **Prenatal:** explore beliefs about pregnancy (e.g., hot–cold food rules), modesty and provider gender preferences, and who should be in the room.
- **Labor and birth:** negotiate family presence, movement and position preferences, and rituals such as placenta retention — disposition follows facility policy and jurisdiction.
- **Postpartum:** anticipate confinement practices, dietary customs, breastfeeding norms, and newborn care traditions (e.g., herbal applications, coin rubbing). Assess each practice on its own merits; many are harmless and should be honored.
- **Loss and bereavement:** religious and cultural death rituals (baptism, burial customs, naming) matter profoundly; provide privacy, time, and nonjudgmental support.
- **LGBTQ+ families:** use inclusive language (partner, parent, chestfeeding where appropriate) and recognize both parents.
- **Respectful maternity care** is the umbrella: dignity, autonomy, informed consent, communication, and freedom from mistreatment during childbirth.

## ELI-10

> Being culturally competent is like being a good guest in someone else's home. You don't assume their rules — you ask, you watch, and you follow their lead. You also notice your own habits, because what feels normal to you may feel strange to them. The goal is that they feel safe and respected, not that you prove you know everything about their culture.

## High-Yield Points

- Cultural competence is a **process** (Campinha-Bacote: desire → awareness → knowledge → skill → encounters), not a finish line.
- Cultural humility means staying a learner; the patient is the expert on their culture.
- Cultural safety is judged by the **patient's experience**, not the provider's intention.
- Giger–Davidhizar's six phenomena: communication, space, social organization, time, environmental control, biological variations.
- Use trained medical interpreters; family members and children are not adequate substitutes.
- LEARN = Listen, Explain, Acknowledge, Recommend, Negotiate.
- Assess each person individually; stereotyping is the opposite of competence.
- Interpreter services, placental disposition, and disclosure rules follow facility policy and law — nurses work within their scope.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-language definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Cultural competence | Ongoing process of providing effective, respectful care across cultures | The organizing goal of this chapter |
| Cultural humility | Lifelong self-reflection that keeps the nurse a learner | Prevents the arrogance of assuming you "know" a culture |
| Cultural safety | Care the patient experiences as respectful and non-diminishing | Shifts the measure from provider intent to patient experience |
| Medical interpreter | Trained professional who translates health information accurately | Reduces errors and protects privacy; preferred over family translators |
| LEARN framework | Listen, Explain, Acknowledge, Recommend, Negotiate | A step-by-step way to negotiate care across differing beliefs |
| Cultural encounter | Real interaction with a person of another culture | The practice that refines all other cultural abilities |
| Cultural desire | Genuine motivation to become culturally competent | The engine that drives the whole process |
| Respectful maternity care | Care honoring dignity, autonomy, consent, and communication during childbirth | A safety and human-rights standard in maternal-newborn care |

## Example

A Spanish-speaking laboring person is admitted with their partner. The nurse brings up the facility's video interpreter service before taking the detailed history — the partner starts to translate, and the nurse gently explains that a professional interpreter keeps the conversation private and accurate, per facility policy. Later the family asks to keep the placenta for a traditional burial; the nurse acknowledges its meaning, explains that disposition follows facility and jurisdictional policy, and contacts the care team about accommodating the request. When discussing the newborn's first feeding, the nurse uses LEARN — listening to the grandmother's beliefs about colostrum before recommending early breastfeeding. The family feels heard, and the nurse delivered safe, respectful care without abandoning professional standards.

## Common Confusions

| Do not confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Cultural competence | Cultural humility | Competence is a developed skill set; humility is the ongoing stance that you never stop learning |
| Cultural safety | Provider's good intentions | Safety is defined by the patient's experience of care |
| Family member as interpreter | Medical interpreter | Family may filter, mistranslate, or breach privacy; trained interpreters are the standard |
| Cultural assessment | Stereotyping | Assessment asks the individual; stereotyping applies group assumptions to everyone |
| Awareness alone | Competence | Knowing about cultures is necessary but insufficient — skill and encounters are required |
| Respecting a practice | Endorsing a practice | Nurses honor harmless traditions without recommending them; harmful ones go through the care team and policy |
