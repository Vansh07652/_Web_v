# Lamaze International Childbirth Education

> **Book:** [Maternal-Newborn Nursing](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 14: Childbirth Education Options](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 1  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Lamaze International is a nonprofit organization and the name behind one of the world's most recognized childbirth education programs. Its roots lie in *psychoprophylaxis*, a method developed in the Soviet Union and popularized in France in the 1950s by obstetrician Fernand Lamaze. The core idea: labor pain is not purely physical but partly a conditioned response, so it can be reshaped by learning. With weeks of practice — breathing, relaxation, focused attention — a calm coping response can replace the fear–tension–pain reaction when labor arrives, the way an athlete's training replaces hesitation with a practiced move. The program came to the United States as ASPO (American Society for Psychoprophylaxis in Obstetrics), founded in 1960, renamed Lamaze International in 1994. Today's Lamaze emphasizes evidence-based "Healthy Birth Practices" and informed decision-making, taught by certified childbirth educators.

## Why This Matters

Childbirth education is often where a pregnant person first learns what labor will be like, and Lamaze is the program many encounter first. Nurses meet Lamaze-prepared families constantly, so recognizing the techniques — slow-paced breathing, position changes, focal points — lets the nurse support what the person is already doing. Lamaze's emphasis on continuous support and freedom of movement aligns with strong evidence about comfort and progress in labor, and it connects directly to nonpharmacological pain management covered elsewhere in this book. Finally, Lamaze's commitment to informed decision-making is a reminder that the nurse supports the person's choices — whatever method they chose.

## Core Concepts

### Psychoprophylaxis: the theory behind the program

"Psychoprophylaxis" means "mind" + "prevention." The theory holds that fear and tension amplify the pain experience of labor, and that a learned coping response can dampen that amplification. Class time is therefore spent *practicing* — breathing, relaxing, focusing — until the behaviors become automatic. By labor, the prepared person has a trained response to draw on rather than improvising mid-contraction. This is why Lamaze classes emphasize rehearsal and partner practice over lectures.

### The six Lamaze Healthy Birth Practices

Lamaze has distilled its philosophy into six evidence-based practices, a handy study list:

1. **Let labor begin on its own** — avoid scheduling birth without a medical reason.
2. **Walk, move around, and change positions throughout labor.**
3. **Bring a loved one, friend, or doula for continuous support.**
4. **Avoid interventions that are not medically necessary.**
5. **Avoid giving birth on your back, and follow your body's urges to push.**
6. **Keep mother and baby together after birth.**

These are guidelines for normal, low-risk birth, not guarantees — medical need can change the plan at any time.

### Techniques taught in class

Lamaze teaches a toolkit, not one magic technique. Breathing is the famous piece: *slow-paced breathing* (a slow, calming rhythm used through most of labor) and *modified-paced breathing* (a lighter, faster pattern for stronger contractions). Around the breathing sits relaxation, focal points (fixing the eyes on an object), touch and massage (such as effleurage, light stroking), vocalization, and — crucially — movement and position changes, from walking to rocking to squatting. The message: use whatever combination works in the moment.

### The birth partner's role

Lamaze was built around a companion — partner, friend, or doula — trained alongside the pregnant person. The companion remembers the techniques when the laboring person cannot, cues breathing, provides touch and massage, and is a steady presence. This is why classes are attended as a team. For nurses, a well-coached partner reduces anxiety and frees the nurse for clinical tasks, though the nurse remains responsible for assessment and safety.

### Classes and educators

Lamaze classes are typically taught over several weeks in the third trimester by certified educators — Lamaze Certified Childbirth Educators (LCCE) — who complete accredited training. Formats vary (group, private, weekend, online), but content typically covers labor physiology, the six Healthy Birth Practices, comfort techniques, recognizing labor, decision-making about interventions, and postpartum basics. Certification signals standardized, evidence-based education.

### Evidence, informed choice, and the nurse's role

Modern Lamaze explicitly supports informed decision-making — including choosing analgesia or anesthesia when wanted; it is not "no epidural, ever." Research reviews consistently find that continuous labor support, a Lamaze hallmark, benefits laboring people. The nurse's role is to meet the family where they are: recognize the techniques, support them, and respect that plans change with medical need.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Lamaze is a class that teaches people to stay calm during birth. They practice slow breathing and ways to relax their bodies, like an athlete training for a big game. A partner comes to class too, so they know how to help. Practicing ahead of time makes birth less scary and easier to handle.

## High-Yield Points

- Lamaze descends from **psychoprophylaxis**: conditioning a calm coping response to replace the fear–tension–pain reaction.
- Timeline: Lamaze popularized the method in 1950s France; ASPO founded 1960; renamed **Lamaze International** 1994.
- Classes are taught by certified educators (**LCCE**), typically over several weeks in the third trimester, with the partner attending.
- The **six Healthy Birth Practices**: labor begins on its own; move and change positions; continuous support; avoid unnecessary interventions; avoid giving birth on the back; keep mother and baby together.
- Core techniques: slow-paced and modified-paced breathing, relaxation, focal points, effleurage, vocalization, movement.
- Lamaze supports **informed decision-making**, including the choice of analgesia — it does not promise pain-free birth.
- Continuous labor support is among the best-evidenced comfort measures; nurses should support whatever technique the laboring person uses.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Psychoprophylaxis | Preventing excessive labor pain through conditioned, practiced coping responses | The theoretical foundation of Lamaze |
| Lamaze International | The nonprofit (formerly ASPO, renamed 1994) that certifies educators | The credentialing body behind LCCE |
| LCCE | Lamaze Certified Childbirth Educator | Certification signals standardized, evidence-based classes |
| Healthy Birth Practices | Lamaze's six evidence-based guidelines for normal, low-risk birth | The philosophy nurses will see families practicing |
| Slow-paced breathing | A slow, calming rhythm used through most of labor | The signature Lamaze coping technique, cued by the partner |
| Modified-paced breathing | A lighter, faster pattern for stronger contractions | A second tool when slow breathing is not enough |
| Focal point | An object or image the laboring person fixes their eyes on | A focus technique that supports relaxation |
| Effleurage | Light, rhythmic stroking (often of the abdomen or thighs) | A soothing touch technique partners can use |
| Continuous support | A companion present throughout labor | One of the six practices and strongly supported by research |

## Example: Supporting a Lamaze-prepared laboring person

A laboring person at 6 cm is using slow-paced breathing through contractions while their partner counts softly and strokes their back. The nurse recognizes the pattern, lowers the lights, and waits for the contraction to end before speaking. Between contractions, the nurse offers a position change — "Would you like to try the peanut ball, or stand at the bedside for a few?" — consistent with the practice of moving and changing positions. Later, when the person asks for an epidural, the nurse supports that choice without judgment and without framing it as "giving up" on the class techniques. The nurse documents the comfort measures used and the person's preferences. Notice what the nurse did not do: impose a technique, rush the partner's coaching, or treat the method as a promise that the person would never want medication.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Lamaze | "Just breathing exercises" | Breathing is one tool; the program also teaches movement, relaxation, support, and decision-making |
| Lamaze | A guarantee of pain-free birth | It teaches coping; pain varies, and analgesia may still be chosen |
| Lamaze | "No epidural, ever" | Lamaze supports informed choice, including pharmacological pain relief |
| Psychoprophylaxis | Hypnosis | Psychoprophylaxis works by conditioned learning; hypnobirthing uses self-hypnosis — different mechanisms |
| Partner coaching | Partner providing medical care | The partner gives comfort; clinical assessment stays with the nurse |
