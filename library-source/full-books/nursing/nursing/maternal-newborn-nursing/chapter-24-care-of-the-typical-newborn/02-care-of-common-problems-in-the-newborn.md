# Care of Common Problems in the Newborn

> **Book:** [Maternal-Newborn Nursing](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 24: Care of the Typical Newborn](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 2  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

A "typical" newborn still develops a surprising number of alarming-looking conditions — rashes that appear overnight, yellow skin, white patches on the tongue, spitting up, a weeping belly button. Most of these are either normal variants (they would happen to any newborn) or common, self-limiting problems that resolve with supportive care. The nurse's core skill is **triage by pattern**: recognizing what is expected for the baby's age, distinguishing benign from dangerous, and knowing which findings require reassurance and teaching versus which require prompt escalation.

This topic walks through the common problems seen in the typical newborn nursery — jaundice, skin findings, oral findings, feeding and gastrointestinal concerns, and umbilical issues — and gives each one a consistent framework: *what it is, why it happens, what the nurse watches for, and when to escalate.* Because evaluation criteria (bilirubin thresholds, fever definitions, follow-up timing) vary by institution and change with new evidence, this study guide describes principles rather than prescribing protocols. Always follow facility policy and provider orders, and flag any unfamiliar finding for review.

## Why This Matters

Newborns cannot tell us what is wrong; the nurse's observation is the baby's voice. Most parents will encounter at least one of these problems in the first weeks, and how the nurse responds shapes both the baby's outcome and the family's confidence. Missing the difference between physiologic jaundice and a rapid bilirubin rise, or between milk curds and thrush, can delay needed care. Conversely, treating a benign finding like erythema toxicum as an emergency causes needless stress and testing. For exams, the classic questions turn on exactly these distinctions — timing of jaundice, the "wipe test" for thrush, and the red flags that separate normal spitting up from vomiting.

## Core Concepts

### Physiologic jaundice: expected, but only on its own timeline

Jaundice is the yellow discoloration from bilirubin, a byproduct of red blood cell breakdown. Newborns produce more bilirubin than older children (more red cells, shorter lifespan) and their immature livers clear it more slowly, so a mild rise is normal. **Physiologic jaundice** typically appears after the first 24 hours of life, peaks around days 3–5 in term newborns, and resolves as feeding increases and the liver matures. The nursing priorities are feeding well (stooling removes bilirubin), watching the *pattern* of jaundice (onset, spread, and progression), and following the facility's bilirubin-monitoring protocol. Jaundice that appears in the first 24 hours, rises rapidly, reaches the palms and soles, or is accompanied by lethargy or poor feeding is **not** physiologic — it requires prompt provider evaluation because very high bilirubin can damage the brain (kernicterus). Sunlight exposure is not a safe home treatment; monitoring and any treatment follow provider orders.

### Common skin findings: alarming to look at, benign underneath

- **Erythema toxicum** — blotchy red patches with tiny white or yellow papules, often appearing in the first days. It looks dramatic but is harmless, comes and goes, and requires no treatment.
- **Milia** — tiny white cysts on the nose, cheeks, and chin, from blocked sebaceous glands; they resolve on their own.
- **Neonatal acne** — small red bumps or pustules on the face, driven by maternal hormones; it clears without treatment.
- **Mongolian spots** — flat, blue-gray patches, often over the lower back and buttocks, more common in newborns with darker skin; they are pigmentation, not bruising, and fade over years.
- **Peeling skin** — normal shedding, especially in post-term newborns.

### Oral findings: thrush versus milk curds

**Epstein pearls** are tiny white cysts on the gums or palate — normal and self-resolving. **Thrush** (oral candidiasis) appears as white patches on the tongue, gums, or inner cheeks that **do not wipe away** — scraping them leaves a red, sometimes bleeding base. Milk curds, by contrast, wipe off easily. Thrush can make feeding uncomfortable and may require provider-prescribed treatment; it can also coexist with diaper-area candidiasis.

### Feeding and gastrointestinal concerns: spitting up versus vomiting

Most newborns spit up small amounts after feeds — effortless, small-volume, and painless — because the lower esophageal sphincter is immature. Parents should know this is normal as long as the baby is gaining weight and not distressed. **Vomiting** is forceful, large-volume, and often projectile; projectile vomiting in particular, or vomiting with blood, bile (green), poor weight gain, or signs of dehydration, requires evaluation. Dehydration warnings include fewer wet diapers than expected, a dry mouth, a sunken fontanelle, and lethargy. Infrequent stooling is not automatically constipation — many breastfed babies stool rarely after the early weeks — but hard, difficult-to-pass stools or no stool by the expected early window need assessment.

### Umbilical and diaper-area concerns

An **umbilical granuloma** is a small, moist pink nodule on the stump after it falls off; it is usually harmless and treated per provider instructions. The stump itself can become infected (omphalitis) — warning signs are redness spreading onto the belly, warmth, swelling, drainage, or odor, and the baby may be febrile or lethargic. Diaper dermatitis (diaper rash) is common, usually from moisture and friction; prevention is frequent changes, gentle cleaning, drying, and barrier cream as taught. A rash with satellite red dots or one that does not improve may be fungal and needs provider assessment.

### Red flags: when "common" stops being common

Any of the following warrants prompt provider notification: temperature outside the expected range for the facility's policy, poor feeding or lethargy, respiratory distress (fast or labored breathing, grunting), worsening jaundice, projectile vomiting or bile-stained vomit, blood in stool, seizure-like movements, vesicles or blisters on the skin, and umbilical redness with drainage or odor. The nurse's documentation of these findings — onset, timing, and progression — is the information the provider needs to act.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

New babies get lots of funny spots and changes — rashes, yellow skin, white dots — and almost all of them go away on their own. The nurse's job is to tell the difference between a harmless spot and a sign that something needs a doctor. Parents learn what to watch for and who to call, so they never have to guess.

## High-Yield Points

- Physiologic jaundice appears after 24 hours, peaks around days 3–5 in term newborns, and resolves; onset within the first 24 hours or rapid progression is a red flag.
- Feeding promotes stooling, which is the body's main route for clearing bilirubin; monitor the pattern, not just the color.
- Erythema toxicum, milia, neonatal acne, Mongolian spots, and peeling skin are benign — document and reassure.
- Thrush does not wipe off; milk curds do. Scraping thrush leaves a red base.
- Spitting up is effortless and small; vomiting is forceful. Projectile or bile-stained vomiting requires evaluation.
- Watch output: fewer wet diapers than expected, dry mouth, sunken fontanelle, and lethargy suggest dehydration.
- Umbilical stump warning signs: spreading redness, warmth, drainage, or odor.
- Any blister/vesicle rash in a newborn is urgent until proven otherwise — it is not "just a rash."
- All thresholds and treatments follow institutional protocols and provider orders — this guide teaches principles only.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Physiologic jaundice | Expected bilirubin rise appearing after 24 hours and resolving as the liver matures | Normal, but must be distinguished from pathologic jaundice by timing and pattern |

| Erythema toxicum | A harmless blotchy rash with small papules in the first days of life | Commonly mistaken for infection; recognizing it prevents needless workup |
| Milia | Tiny white cysts from blocked sebaceous glands | Benign and self-resolving; no treatment |

| Projectile vomiting | Forceful vomiting that travels a distance | A red flag requiring evaluation, especially with bile or poor weight gain |

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process

1. Identify the finding and anchor it in the baby's age: is this expected at this day of life?
2. Gather the pattern: onset, timing, progression, and any associated signs (feeding, output, temperature, behavior).
3. Apply the facility's assessment tools (e.g., jaundice risk assessment, bilirubin protocol, vital-sign criteria).
4. For benign findings: document, reassure the family with accurate information, and teach what to watch.
5. For red flags: notify the provider promptly with a clear, factual description; follow orders.
6. Document teaching, family understanding, and any follow-up plan; escalate scope issues per policy.

## Example

A newborn is now 3 days old. During the morning assessment, the nurse notices yellow coloring that has spread from the face to the chest. The record shows the jaundice first appeared around 30 hours of age. The nurse reviews the risk picture: the baby is breastfeeding well, has adequate wet diapers, and is alert — but there was bruising from the birth, and the baby's stools are still transitioning. The nurse checks the facility's jaundice-monitoring protocol, obtains a transcutaneous bilirubin measurement per policy, and reports the value and pattern to the provider, who orders a follow-up assessment. The nurse then teaches the parent: feed frequently to encourage stooling, watch for jaundice reaching the arms, legs, palms, or soles, and call immediately if the baby becomes sleepy or hard to wake or feeds poorly. The parent also asks about putting the baby in sunlight — the nurse explains this is not a safe treatment and that the plan is monitoring and provider-directed follow-up. By evening, the bilirubin trend is stable and the family goes home with a clear call-back plan.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Physiologic jaundice | Pathologic jaundice | Physiologic appears after 24 hours and follows an expected course; onset in the first 24 hours, rapid rise, or systemic signs are pathologic |
| Erythema toxicum | An infectious rash | Erythema toxicum is blotchy with papules, appears in the first days, and the baby is otherwise well; vesicles or systemic illness are urgent |
| Milk curds | Thrush | Curds wipe off; thrush does not and leaves a red base when scraped |
| Spitting up | Vomiting | Spitting up is effortless and small; vomiting is forceful, and projectile or bile-stained vomiting is a red flag |
| Mongolian spots | Bruising | Mongolian spots are flat blue-gray pigmentation (present at birth, fade over years); bruising changes color and resolves in days |
| Milia | Neonatal acne | Milia are tiny white cysts from blocked glands; neonatal acne is red bumps/pustules from maternal hormones — both benign |
| "Sunlight cures jaundice" | Provider-directed monitoring | Sun exposure is not a safe treatment; bilirubin follows the facility protocol |
