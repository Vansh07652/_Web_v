# Informed Decision Making and Self-Care

> **Book:** [Fundamentals of Nursing](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 17: Patient and Family Education](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 1  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Informed decision making is the process by which a patient makes a voluntary, reasoned choice about their health care after receiving and understanding the relevant information. It is the everyday reality behind the formal legal act of informed consent. Self-care is the set of everyday activities people perform to maintain their own health and well-being — taking medications, preparing meals, monitoring symptoms, and deciding when to call for help. The two ideas connect: informed decisions shape what the self-care plan will be, and self-care demands that the patient actually understand the decision they made.

Nurses sit at the center of both processes. They are usually the ones who notice whether a patient truly understood the provider's explanation, who clarify and re-teach in plain language, and who help patients turn a decision into a realistic daily routine.

## Why This Matters

Informed consent is a legal doctrine, not just a form. A consent is valid only if the patient received adequate information, understood it, and chose freely. When any element is missing — a patient who signed without understanding, or signed under family pressure — the consent may be legally questionable and clinically unsafe. Beyond the law, autonomy (respecting the patient's right to self-determination) is a core ethical principle in nursing. Self-care matters because most health care happens at home: patients manage chronic conditions, medications, and symptoms between visits. Patients who understand their plan are safer, and health literacy — the ability to obtain, understand, and use health information — is one of the strongest predictors of health outcomes. Both topics are heavily tested on the NCLEX.

## Core Concepts

### The four elements of informed decision making

A meaningful decision rests on four elements:

- **Adequate information:** the nature of the option, its purpose, benefits, risks, and alternatives — explained in language the patient can understand.
- **Comprehension:** the patient actually grasps the key points.
- **Voluntariness:** no coercion from providers, family, or anyone else.
- **Capacity:** the ability to understand the information, weigh it, and communicate a choice.

If any element fails, the decision is not truly informed.

### Capacity and competence

Capacity is a clinical judgment: it is decision-specific (a patient may have capacity to choose a meal but not to refuse a complex surgery) and can fluctuate with illness, medication, or fatigue. Competence is a legal term, determined by a court, about a person's general ability to make decisions. Nurses contribute observations about a patient's orientation, reasoning, and communication, but the provider — and ultimately the legal system — makes the formal determination.

### Informed consent and the nurse's role

The healthcare provider who will perform the procedure obtains informed consent; that is not the nurse's job. The nurse's role includes witnessing the signature, verifying that the patient understands what was explained, clarifying and teaching as needed, answering questions, and reporting concerns (for example, "the patient does not seem to understand" or "the family is pressuring the patient") to the provider. Scope and institutional policies vary, so nurses should know their local expectations.

### Shared decision making

Shared decision making is a collaborative conversation: the clinician presents the reasonable options with honest information about benefits and risks, and the patient brings their values, preferences, and life situation. Together they reach a plan. It is especially useful for preference-sensitive decisions, where several medically reasonable options exist. Shared decision making is broader and more ongoing than informed consent, which authorizes a specific procedure.

### Self-care and self-care deficit

The nursing theorist Dorothea Orem described self-care as the activities people initiate and perform to maintain life, health, and well-being. Self-care requisites include universal needs (air, water, food, activity, rest, solitude, safety), developmental needs, and health-deviation needs (what a person must do because of illness or treatment). A self-care deficit exists when the demands of self-care exceed the person's abilities — because of illness, disability, knowledge gaps, or lack of support. The nurse's response is education and support that builds the person's capacity, not doing everything for them.

### Factors that shape self-care

Self-care succeeds or fails based on health literacy, language, culture and beliefs, cognitive and functional abilities, motivation and readiness, social and family support, financial resources, and the environment. Assessment of these factors is the starting point for any self-care plan.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Choosing your own care is like choosing your own lunch: you need to know what's on the menu, understand what each option is, and pick freely without someone forcing you. Self-care is what you do every day to stay healthy, like taking your medicine or eating well. The nurse's job is to make sure you understand the menu and know how to take care of yourself.

## High-Yield Points

- A valid informed decision requires all four elements: information, comprehension, voluntariness, and capacity.
- Capacity is clinical and decision-specific; competence is a legal determination.
- The provider obtains informed consent; the nurse verifies understanding, clarifies, witnesses, and reports concerns — specifics vary by state and facility.
- Teach-back (asking the patient to explain in their own words) is a practical way to verify comprehension.
- Shared decision making is an ongoing, collaborative conversation; informed consent authorizes a specific procedure.
- Orem's self-care deficit: when self-care demands exceed the person's abilities, nursing provides education and support.
- Health literacy, culture, and social support strongly shape both decision making and self-care.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Informed consent | Permission for a treatment given after understanding the information | Legally and ethically valid only when the patient truly understood and chose freely |
| Capacity | The clinical ability to understand, weigh, and communicate a decision | Decision-specific and changeable; determines whether a patient can decide |
| Competence | A legal status, determined by a court, about decision-making ability | Different from capacity; rarely a bedside judgment |
| Autonomy | The ethical principle of respecting a person's right to self-determination | The foundation of informed decision making |
| Shared decision making | A collaboration in which clinician and patient discuss options against the patient's values | Produces plans patients actually accept and follow |
| Health literacy | The ability to obtain, understand, and use health information | Low health literacy is a major barrier to comprehension and self-care |
| Self-care | Daily activities people perform to maintain health and well-being | The core of living with chronic conditions between visits |
| Teach-back | Asking the patient to repeat key information in their own words | Verifies comprehension instead of assuming it |

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process

1. Assess what the patient already knows, believes, wants, and can do.
2. Provide information in plain language, using an interpreter when needed.
3. Verify comprehension with teach-back.
4. Support the patient's informed choice, even if you would choose differently — unless safety requires further discussion.
5. Co-create a realistic self-care plan with small, concrete steps.
6. Evaluate and document; arrange follow-up.

## Example

Ms. Ito has just been diagnosed with a chronic condition that requires a daily medication and daily symptom monitoring. The provider explains the plan quickly; Ms. Ito nods but asks no questions. Later, the nurse uses a simple diagram, explains in plain language, and asks Ms. Ito to say back what she will do each morning. It becomes clear she misunderstood the timing of the medication. The nurse re-teaches, and with Ms. Ito's permission includes her daughter, who helps her organize reminders. Together they write a one-page daily plan. The nurse documents the teaching and Ms. Ito's correct teach-back, and arranges a follow-up phone call. The nurse did not make the plan for Ms. Ito — she helped Ms. Ito make it, and verified she understood it.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Informed consent | Shared decision making | Consent authorizes a specific procedure after disclosure; shared decision making is a broader, ongoing conversation about options |
| Capacity | Competence | Capacity is a clinical, decision-specific judgment; competence is a legal determination |
| Explaining a procedure | Obtaining consent | The nurse may clarify and verify understanding; the provider is responsible for obtaining informed consent |
| Patient education | Giving information | Education is interactive and verified; giving information is one-way |
| Doing things for the patient | Supporting self-care | Support builds the patient's own ability; doing for the patient creates dependence |
