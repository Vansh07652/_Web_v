# Pain Assessment

> **Book:** [Fundamentals of Nursing](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 30: Pain Assessment](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 4  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Pain assessment is the systematic process of gathering and interpreting information about a person's pain — how much it hurts, where, what it feels like, what makes it worse or better, and how it affects their life. It is the foundation of everything that follows: you cannot treat pain you have not measured, and you cannot evaluate treatment without reassessing.

The single most important idea in this topic: **the person's self-report is the most reliable indicator of pain.** Pain has no monitor, no blood test, no scan. The classic nursing principle holds that pain is whatever the person experiencing it says it is, existing whenever they say it does. Assessment tools — pain scales, behavioral checklists, and interview guides — exist to make that report specific, consistent, and communicable between caregivers.

A good pain assessment combines a thorough pain interview (the story), an intensity scale chosen to fit the person (the number or faces), and reassessment after interventions (did it work?). It is a conversation, not a form.

> ⚠️ **Educational content only.** Scale selection, reassessment timeframes, and documentation standards vary by facility and patient population; verify with current evidence and institutional policy.

## Why This Matters

- **Untreated pain is dangerous.** Unrelieved pain can cause shallow breathing and poor cough (raising pneumonia risk), immobility (raising clot risk), delayed healing, and sleep deprivation.
- **Pain drives treatment decisions.** The right drug, dose, route, and non-drug strategy all begin with accurate assessment. "Patient uncomfortable" cannot guide therapy; "sharp 8/10 incision pain worse with coughing, eased to 3/10 by repositioning and splinting" can.
- **It is a communication tool.** A number means nothing unless the scale and context are recorded; consistent use of one scale lets every caregiver compare apples to apples.

## Core Concepts

### Self-report is the gold standard

If a person can tell you about their pain, their report outranks every other piece of evidence. Ask, listen, and record their words. Clarifying questions are part of good assessment — but the report is never dismissed because "it shouldn't hurt that much" or "they don't look like they're in pain." Pain is subjective by definition.

### The comprehensive pain interview: PQRSTU

A complete pain history goes far beyond a number. The **PQRSTU** mnemonic organizes it:

- **P — Provoking/palliating factors:** What brings it on? What makes it worse or better?
- **Q — Quality:** What does it feel like — sharp, burning, aching, cramping? Quality hints at cause.
- **R — Region/radiation:** Where exactly is it? Does it travel anywhere?
- **S — Severity:** Use the chosen intensity scale (0–10, faces, or words).
- **T — Timing:** When did it start? Constant or intermittent? Worse at night, with movement, or after eating?
- **U — Understanding/impact:** What does the person think causes it? How does it affect sleep, appetite, mood, mobility, and work?

A related mnemonic, **OLDCARTS** (Onset, Location, Duration, Characteristics, Aggravating factors, Relieving factors, Treatments, Severity), covers the same ground.

### Pain intensity scales

Scales turn a feeling into a number or symbol that can be compared over time:

- **Numeric Rating Scale (NRS):** 0 = no pain, 10 = worst possible pain; quick and widely used with adults and older children.
- **Wong-Baker FACES:** A row of faces from smiling to crying; the person points to the face matching their pain. Useful for children and adults with limited language, literacy, or cognition.
- **Verbal descriptor scale:** Words instead of numbers ("none, mild, moderate, severe").
- **Visual analog scale:** A line from "no pain" to "worst pain imaginable"; the person marks a point along it.

Match the scale to the person's age, cognition, and literacy; use **the same scale consistently** so scores are comparable; document which scale was used. A "6" on FACES is not a "6" on the NRS.

### Assessing pain when self-report is not possible

Infants, people with advanced dementia, and people who are sedated cannot give a numeric score — but their pain is not ignored; assessment is adapted:

- **FLACC** (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability) scores observable behaviors in children and other nonverbal people.
- **PAINAD** (Pain Assessment in Advanced Dementia) rates breathing, vocalization, facial expression, body language, and consolability in older adults with dementia.
- Behavioral tools measure **distress**, which may come from pain — or from fear, hunger, or discomfort. Use them as a structured substitute for self-report, involve caregivers who know the person's baseline, and treat the result as a signal to investigate.
- **Vital signs are not reliable pain indicators:** heart rate and blood pressure may rise briefly with sudden acute pain but habituate quickly — normal vital signs do not rule out severe pain.

### Reassessment and documentation

Assessment is a loop: assess → intervene → reassess → document. Reassess after any intervention (medication, repositioning, heat/cold) using the same scale, within a timeframe appropriate to the route and per facility policy — timeframes vary, so verify the policy rather than assuming.

Document the score, scale, the person's own words, quality/location/timing, aggravating and relieving factors, functional impact, the intervention, and the response — so the next nurse can continue the plan without re-interviewing from scratch.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I’m 10

Asking someone how much something hurts is like asking which ice-cream flavor they like — only they know the real answer. So we ask them to pick a number from 0 to 10, or point to a face, and we use that same scale every time to see if the pain is getting better or worse. If someone can't talk, we watch their face, body, and sounds carefully — but watching is not as good as asking.

## High-Yield Points

- **Self-report is the gold standard** — pain is whatever the person experiencing it says it is; no test or observation overrides it.
- **A complete assessment covers more than intensity:** quality, location/radiation, timing, aggravating and relieving factors, and impact on function and sleep (PQRSTU or OLDCARTS).
- **Match the scale to the person** and use the **same scale consistently**; document which scale was used.
- **Vital signs are not reliable pain indicators** — they habituate quickly and can be normal with severe pain.
- **Behavioral tools (FLACC, PAINAD) are substitutes, not equals** — they measure distress, so results are a signal to investigate, not a diagnosis.
- **Reassess after every intervention** with the same scale and document the response.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Self-report | The person's own description of their pain | The gold standard — no objective test can measure pain |
| PQRSTU | Mnemonic: Provoking/palliating, Quality, Region/radiation, Severity, Timing, Understanding/impact | Ensures the full pain story is gathered, not just a number |
| Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) | 0–10 scale where 0 = no pain and 10 = worst possible pain | Widely used intensity measure for adults and older children |
| Wong-Baker FACES | Picture scale of faces from smiling to crying | Lets children and people with limited language or literacy point to their pain |
| FLACC | Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability | Structured behavioral assessment for children and other nonverbal people |
| PAINAD | Pain Assessment in Advanced Dementia | Structured behavioral assessment for older adults with dementia |

## Example: The Post-Op Interview

Mr. Rivera is one day post-abdominal surgery. The nurse asks, "Tell me about your pain." He says: "It's a sharp, tearing feeling right at my incision, worse every time I cough. Resting helps a little, and holding a pillow over it helps a lot. It started this morning and it kept me from sleeping last night." On the 0–10 scale he chooses 7 at rest and 9 when coughing. He adds that he is worried the incision "came open."

That conversation produces a full PQRSTU picture: provoking (coughing), quality (sharp/tearing), region (incision), severity (7–9/10), timing (constant, worse with movement), and understanding (fear the wound is opening). The nurse checks the incision — it is intact — teaches him to splint with a pillow when coughing, gives the ordered medication, and later reassesses: "Same scale — where is the pain now?" He reports 3 at rest. Documented: scale, score, characteristics, intervention, response, and teaching.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| A pain scale number | A complete pain assessment | The number only measures intensity; quality, location, timing, and impact come from the interview |
| Elevated vital signs | Proof of pain | Heart rate and blood pressure rise briefly with acute pain and habituate; normal vital signs do not rule out pain |
| Behavioral tools (FLACC, PAINAD) | Self-report | Behavioral tools measure observable distress in people who cannot report; they are a substitute, not an equal |
| "Patient is sleeping" | "Patient has no pain" | People in pain sleep, especially when exhausted; sleep is not evidence of comfort |
| "That shouldn't hurt that much" | A valid reason to discount the report | Pain is subjective; the person's report is the gold standard |
