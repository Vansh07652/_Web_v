# Theories Related to Growth and Development

> **Book:** [Fundamentals of Nursing](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 39: Conception Through Adolescence](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 2  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

A theory is an organized way of explaining observations. Topic 1 described *what* happens as children grow and develop; theories ask *why* — and, more practically for nurses, *what to look for next*. No single theory explains all of human development. Each major framework focuses on one domain: Freud and Erikson on personality and social relationships, Piaget on thinking, Kohlberg on moral reasoning, Maslow on human needs, and Havighurst on the tasks each age presents. The frameworks overlap, complement one another, and sometimes disagree — which is expected, because human development is too rich for one lens.

Nurses are not expected to prove or disprove these theories. Instead, theories function as assessment tools and communication shortcuts. When a nurse says a toddler is working on "autonomy," that one word — drawn from Erikson — summarizes a whole pattern of behavior: wanting to choose, insisting on "me do it," and needing limits that do not crush emerging confidence. Knowing the theories lets you predict typical behavior, interpret what you observe, and shape teaching that fits how the child actually thinks.

## Why This Matters

- **Theories turn observations into meaning.** A preschooler's imaginary friend is not a problem to fix — it is expected behavior for the preoperational mind. Theory tells you which behaviors are typical and which deserve concern.
- **They guide communication.** You explain a procedure differently to a toddler (concrete, brief, immediate) than to an adolescent (abstract, detailed, with choices). Piaget's stages explain why.
- **They structure psychosocial assessment.** Erikson's stages give you a checklist of what each age is "working on" — trust, autonomy, identity — so you can ask better questions.
- **They support anticipatory guidance.** If parents know a 2-year-old's negativism ("no!" to everything) is a normal push for autonomy, they respond with patience instead of alarm.
- **They reveal the limits of labels.** Theories are models, not verdicts; no child fits a stage perfectly, and cultural context changes how development looks.

## Core Concepts

### Why Development Needs Theories

Without a framework, a nurse collects observations without knowing which matter. Theories organize facts into patterns, generate predictions ("what should this child be able to do next?"), and highlight what to assess. They also carry a caution: every theory was built in a particular time and culture, and each has limitations. Use them as lenses, not as rules that define a child.

### Freud's Psychosexual Theory

Freud proposed that personality develops through stages centered on pleasure-seeking energy focused in different body areas: **oral** (birth–1 year: sucking, mouthing), **anal** (1–3 years: toilet training and control), **phallic** (3–6 years: awareness of gender differences), **latency** (6–12 years: relatively quiet period), and **genital** (adolescence onward: mature intimacy). Freud's theory is historically important and widely criticized — much of it lacks empirical support, and its claims about early childhood sexuality are contested. Nurses today rarely apply it directly, but it introduced the idea that early experiences shape later personality.

### Erikson's Psychosocial Theory

Erikson described eight lifelong stages, each built around a psychosocial crisis that must be resolved. The first five span the ages in this chapter:

| Stage | Crisis | Virtue gained |
|---|---|---|
| Infancy | Trust vs. mistrust | Hope |
| Toddler | Autonomy vs. shame and doubt | Will |
| Preschool | Initiative vs. guilt | Purpose |
| School-age | Industry vs. inferiority | Competence |
| Adolescence | Identity vs. role confusion | Fidelity |

The remaining three (intimacy, generativity, ego integrity) belong to adulthood, covered in Chapter 40. Unlike Freud, Erikson emphasized social relationships and culture, and his framework remains a staple of nursing assessment: "Is this child developing trust through consistent, responsive care?"

### Piaget's Cognitive Theory

Piaget described how *thinking* develops in four stages:

- **Sensorimotor (birth–2 years):** infants learn through senses and movement; the hallmark achievement is **object permanence** — knowing a hidden toy still exists.
- **Preoperational (2–7 years):** symbolic thinking and language explode, but thinking is **egocentric** (seeing the world from one's own viewpoint), and children use magical thinking and centration (focusing on one feature at a time).
- **Concrete operational (7–11 years):** logical thinking about real, tangible things; children master **conservation** — recognizing that amount stays the same despite changes in shape or container.
- **Formal operational (12 years and up):** abstract, hypothetical, and systematic reasoning becomes possible.

Piaget's stages explain everyday nursing observations: why a preschooler fears a "shot" more than the illness it prevents, and why an adolescent can weigh options the way a younger child cannot.

### Kohlberg's Moral Development

Kohlberg described moral reasoning in three levels: **preconventional** (young children: rules obeyed to avoid punishment or gain reward), **conventional** (older children and many adults: rules followed to maintain approval, relationships, and social order), and **postconventional** (principled reasoning based on universal ethical principles). Critiques matter here: Kohlberg's original research used mostly male participants, and the stages describe *reasoning about* right and wrong, not *behavior*. Moral reasoning and moral action are not the same thing.

### Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

Maslow ranked human needs from most to least basic: **physiological** (food, water, sleep, oxygen), **safety**, **love and belonging**, **esteem**, and **self-actualization**. The hierarchy is a useful nursing triage tool — a hungry, frightened child is unlikely to learn, and safety needs come before teaching. Maslow himself did not intend it as a rigid ladder; people move between levels, and the ordering varies across cultures and situations.

### Havighurst's Developmental Tasks

Havighurst defined **developmental tasks** as skills that arise at certain periods of life — tasks whose mastery leads to happiness and success with later tasks, and whose failure leads to difficulty ahead. Examples in childhood include learning to walk and talk (infancy), learning to get along with age-mates (early childhood), and achieving personal independence (adolescence). Havighurst's tasks pair naturally with anticipatory guidance: teach families what task is *next*, and they know what to support.

### Using Theories in Practice

In practice, nurses take an **eclectic** approach: match the theory to the question. Use Erikson for psychosocial assessment, Piaget for how to explain things, Maslow to prioritize, and Havighurst to plan anticipatory guidance. Remember the limits: stages describe *typical* patterns, not every child; cultural and individual variation is normal; and a theory-based "label" must never replace a full assessment of the actual child and family.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Think of development theories as different maps of the same city. One map shows the roads, another shows the parks, and a third shows the schools — all of the same city, drawn for different purposes. No single map shows everything, but each one helps you find what you're looking for. Growth theories are like that: each one explains a different part of how children change.

## High-Yield Points

- **Match the theorist to the domain:** Erikson = psychosocial, Piaget = cognitive, Kohlberg = moral, Maslow = needs, Havighurst = tasks, Freud = psychosexual.
- **Erikson's childhood stages (high-yield pairings):** trust vs. mistrust (infancy), autonomy vs. shame/doubt (toddler), initiative vs. guilt (preschool), industry vs. inferiority (school-age), identity vs. role confusion (adolescence).
- **Piaget's hallmark concepts:** object permanence (sensorimotor), egocentrism and magical thinking (preoperational), conservation (concrete operational), abstract/hypothetical reasoning (formal operational).
- **Kohlberg's levels:** preconventional (punishment/reward), conventional (approval/rules), postconventional (principled). Describes reasoning, not behavior.
- **Maslow:** basic needs come before higher needs — use the hierarchy to prioritize care and teaching.
- **Theories are tools, not verdicts:** cultural context and individual variation always matter; flag rather than force a fit.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Psychosocial theory | Framework explaining development through social relationships and personality (Erikson) | Backbone of nursing psychosocial assessment |
| Cognitive theory | Framework explaining how thinking develops (Piaget) | Tells you how to explain things to each age |
| Object permanence | Knowing an object exists even when out of sight | Hallmark of sensorimotor stage; absence after infancy warrants follow-up |
| Egocentrism | Seeing the world only from one's own viewpoint | Explains preschooler communication needs |
| Conservation | Recognizing amount stays the same despite changed appearance | Landmark of concrete operational thinking |
| Developmental task | Skill that arises at a particular period; mastery supports later tasks (Havighurst) | Directs anticipatory guidance |
| Hierarchy of needs | Maslow's ordering of human needs from basic to self-actualization | Helps prioritize care and teaching |
| Eclectic approach | Using different theories for different questions | Realistic way nurses apply theory in practice |

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process: Applying a Theory to a Clinical Question

1. **Name the question.** Example: "Why is 4-year-old Jayden refusing to hold still for the dressing change, and how do I explain it to him?"
2. **Pick the fitting lens.** The question is about thinking and communication → Piaget (preoperational); about feelings and control → Erikson (initiative vs. guilt).
3. **Predict behavior from the theory.** Preoperational thinking means Jayden interprets the procedure through imagination and his own viewpoint — he may fear it as punishment or magic.
4. **Design the approach.** Use concrete, simple language; let him "help" (respecting initiative); use play or a doll; keep explanations immediate rather than abstract.
5. **Evaluate and document.** Note what worked and the child's response; share the strategy with the family and team. Flag any behavior outside expected patterns for provider review.

## Example

**Scenario:** During a well-child visit, a 6-year-old's mother asks the nurse, "She still believes in the tooth fairy — is that normal?" The nurse applies Piaget: at 6, children are in the preoperational-to-concrete transition, and magical thinking is typical. The nurse reassures the mother that this is developmentally expected and explains that logical thinking about such ideas usually strengthens as the child moves through the concrete operational stage. The nurse also uses Erikson's industry vs. inferiority lens to encourage the child's own reasoning: "What do you think happens?" — supporting her sense of competence rather than dismissing her belief. The theory didn't change the answer; it gave the nurse confidence and words for it.

## Common Confusions

| Do not confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Erikson's stages | Freud's stages | Erikson = psychosocial (social relationships, lifelong); Freud = psychosexual (body-focused pleasure zones, historically criticized). |
| Piaget's stages | Kohlberg's stages | Piaget = how thinking develops; Kohlberg = how moral reasoning develops. |
| Object permanence | Conservation | Object permanence: things exist when hidden (sensorimotor). Conservation: amount is unchanged by appearance (concrete operational). |
| Egocentrism | Selfishness | Egocentrism is a cognitive limitation of young children (can't take another's viewpoint), not a moral failing. |
| Kohlberg's moral *reasoning* | Moral *behavior* | A child can reason about right and wrong yet act differently; the stages describe reasoning, not action. |
| A stage theory | A fixed schedule | Stages describe typical sequences; timing and expression vary widely by child and culture. |
