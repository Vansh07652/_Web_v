# Cultural Practice in Nursing

> **Book:** [Fundamentals of Nursing](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 5: Cultural Competence](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 3  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Cultural practice in nursing is the everyday, hands-on application of cultural understanding to patient care: assessment, planning, communication, interventions, and evaluation. Topic 1 explained *what* cultural differences are; this topic is about *what to do* with that knowledge at the bedside. The organizing idea is **cultural competence** — often described in nursing education not as a destination but as a continuous process made up of components such as cultural awareness (knowing your own lens), cultural knowledge (learning about health beliefs and practices), cultural skill (conducting culturally sensitive assessments), cultural encounters (direct experience with diverse patients), and cultural desire (genuine motivation to be culturally competent). Alongside competence, **cultural humility** adds a lifelong commitment to self-reflection and to balancing power in the nurse–patient relationship.

Nursing curricula also teach structured assessment frameworks that organize what to look for — dimensions such as communication, space and touch, social organization, time orientation, environmental control, and biological variations, or models that map the influences (technology, religion, kinship, politics, economics, and education) on a patient's care. You do not need to memorize every framework; you need to be able to ask a respectful, thorough set of questions and adapt care accordingly.

## Why This Matters

Culturally congruent care — care that fits the patient's values, beliefs, and practices — improves trust, satisfaction, adherence, and safety. A dietary restriction ignored, a prayer schedule interrupted, a traditional remedy undisclosed, or an end-of-life ritual mishandled can each cause real harm, even when the clinical treatment is flawless. Assessment without assumption is the core skill: the same diagnosis can look completely different to two patients with different beliefs about illness, healing, and who should decide.

This material is also heavily tested: culturally competent communication questions ask which response best respects a patient's background, and "best response" questions reward the option that asks rather than assumes. Finally, language access and culturally competent care are legal and accreditation expectations in many settings — knowing your facility's interpreter and policy resources is part of professional practice.

## Core Concepts

### Cultural competence is a process, not a finish line

No one "completes" cultural competence. Each new patient, illness, and setting requires fresh learning. The process view keeps the nurse humble: knowledge about a group is a hypothesis to test against the individual, and every cultural encounter builds skill. Cultural humility extends this by asking the nurse to reflect on their own biases and on the power imbalance inherent in being the caregiver.

### Cultural assessment: the questions that matter

A culturally sensitive assessment gathers, respectfully, the information that shapes care. Standard areas include:

- **Health beliefs and practices:** What do you believe caused this illness? What treatments have you tried (including traditional or herbal remedies, and visits to traditional healers)? What do you expect will help?
- **Communication:** What language do you prefer for health information? Who should receive information?
- **Nutrition and dietary practices:** Are there foods you can't or won't eat, or that are important to you? Are there fasting practices?
- **Religion and spirituality:** Are there practices (prayer, rituals, sacred objects) we should support? Are there restrictions related to your faith?
- **Family and decision making:** Who should be involved in decisions about your care?
- **Modesty and touch:** Are there preferences about who provides care or how privacy is protected?
- **Death and dying:** Are there beliefs or rituals we should know about if your condition changes?

These questions are woven into the admission assessment and documented so the whole team can honor them.

### Communication and the use of interpreters

When a patient and nurse do not share a language, a **professional medical interpreter** (in person, by phone, or by video) is the standard for important communication: assessments, teaching, consent discussions, and discharge instructions. Using family members as interpreters carries serious risks — confidentiality breaches, inaccurate or incomplete translation, role reversal, and the family editing bad news — and using a minor as an interpreter is generally unacceptable. **Interpreter and translator are different:** an interpreter works with spoken language in real time; a translator works with written documents. Note that interpreter availability and the rules about who may interpret vary by facility and by law in some jurisdictions.

### Religion, diet, and daily practices at the bedside

Many cultural needs are simple to honor with planning: arranging meals that fit religious dietary rules, protecting prayer times and sacred objects, respecting modesty during procedures, and adjusting visiting so the family can be present. The nurse's job is to ask, plan, and communicate the plan to the team — while flagging genuine safety concerns (such as an allergy or an infection-control rule) for discussion rather than silently overriding the patient's practice.

### Death, dying, and end-of-life beliefs

Beliefs about death are among the most culturally meaningful — and most easily mishandled — aspects of care. Cultures differ on who should be present, what rituals should occur at and after death, how the body should be handled, and views on autopsy, organ donation, and life-sustaining treatment. The nurse asks about these preferences when the patient is able to express them, honors them within facility and legal limits, and communicates them to the care team so the end of life is handled with dignity.

### Self-awareness and bias

Cultural practice includes examining the nurse's own assumptions. Everyone carries biases; unexamined bias shows up in practice as shorter teaching sessions, different pain assessments, or dismissive labeling ("difficult patient") for people who are simply different. Self-reflection, supervision, and feedback from colleagues are how bias gets corrected.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Being a culturally smart nurse is like being a good host: you don't serve pork to a guest who can't eat it, and you don't make a guest pray in the hallway. You ask what they need, you write it down so the whole team remembers, and you call a translator when you don't speak the same language. Asking is the secret — guessing is how you mess up.

## High-Yield Points

- **Cultural competence is a process:** awareness → knowledge → skill → encounters → desire; add humility for self-reflection.
- **Assess, don't assume:** health beliefs, remedies tried, language preference, diet, religion, family decision makers, modesty, death/dying beliefs.
- **Ask about traditional remedies and healers** — many patients use them alongside biomedical care, and the team needs to know for safety.
- **Use professional interpreters for significant communication** — not family members, and never children, except in true emergencies.
- **Interpreter ≠ translator:** spoken language in real time versus written documents.
- **Document cultural preferences** so every shift provides consistent care.
- **Flag safety conflicts** (allergies, infection control, unsafe practices) and resolve them through discussion and policy — don't silently override.
- **Exam trap:** the best response to a cultural scenario almost always involves asking the patient or using a professional interpreter — not ignoring the issue or applying a stereotype.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Cultural competence | The ongoing ability to provide care that fits a patient's culture | The practical goal of this chapter |
| Cultural humility | A lifelong commitment to self-reflection and balanced power in care | Keeps competence from becoming arrogance |
| Cultural assessment | Systematically asking about beliefs, practices, and preferences | Turns assumptions into individualized care |
| Culturally congruent care | Care that matches the patient's values and practices | Improves trust, adherence, and safety |
| Interpreter | A person who converts spoken language in real time | Essential for accurate, confidential communication |
| Translator | A person who converts written documents between languages | Needed for written materials like consent forms |
| Transcultural nursing | The nursing specialty focused on culture and care | The scholarly home of these frameworks |
| Bias | A preference or prejudice that distorts judgment | Unexamined bias silently changes the care people receive |

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process

1. **Prepare yourself:** notice your own assumptions about the patient's background.
2. **Assess:** ask the cultural assessment questions respectfully (with a professional interpreter if needed).
3. **Plan:** incorporate the answers — diet, prayer, family involvement, modesty, language — into the care plan, and flag any safety concerns.
4. **Communicate:** document preferences and update the team so care is consistent across shifts.
5. **Evaluate:** check whether the plan honored the patient's practices, and adjust as preferences change.

## Example

A nurse admits a patient who recently moved to the country and speaks limited English. Using a video interpreter, the nurse completes the admission and asks about health beliefs. The patient reports taking a traditional herbal remedy for a stomach complaint and explains that prayer at specific times is important, that meals must follow religious dietary rules, and that a cousin will be the main contact for decisions. The nurse documents the remedy (so a pharmacist can review it for interactions), arranges for the dietary office to provide appropriate meals, protects the prayer times in the schedule, and notes the cousin as the preferred contact — after confirming that is what the patient wants. *(Illustrative scenario; interpreter options and documentation follow facility policy.)*

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Interpreter | Translator | Interpreter = spoken language, real time; translator = written documents |
| Cultural competence | Cultural humility | Competence is knowledge and skill; humility is the ongoing stance of reflection and power-sharing |
| Assessing culture | Stereotyping | Assessment asks the individual; stereotyping assumes the group's label fits |
| Asking questions | Prying | Respectful, purpose-driven questions about care are expected; judgmental curiosity is not |
| One assessment for all | Individualized assessment | Every patient's cultural needs differ — reassess, don't copy |
| Accommodating a practice | Accepting any practice | Accommodate within safety and law; escalate genuine conflicts |
