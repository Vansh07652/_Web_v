# Ethical Practice in Culture and Diversity

> **Book:** [Fundamentals of Nursing](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 5: Cultural Competence](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 2  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Ethics is the systematic study of right and wrong conduct, and nursing ethics applies that study to decisions made in patient care. The core ethical principles that guide nurses are **autonomy** (respecting a person's right to make their own decisions), **beneficence** (acting in the patient's best interest), **nonmaleficence** (avoiding harm), **justice** (fairness in distributing benefits and burdens), **fidelity** (keeping promises and commitments), and **veracity** (truthfulness).

Culture complicates each of these principles in practice. Whose autonomy counts when a family expects to make health decisions together? Is direct truth-telling always the right way to be honest? What happens when a cultural practice conflicts with a prescribed treatment or a safety rule? Ethical practice in culture and diversity means applying ethical principles *while* respecting a patient's cultural values — and recognizing that respect for culture does not require accepting practices that are unsafe or illegal. When values genuinely conflict, nurses do not simply pick a side: they gather facts, clarify what can be honored, explain what cannot, seek alternatives, and escalate through the proper channels (charge nurse, ethics committee, or institutional policy).

## Why This Matters

Every culturally diverse encounter contains potential ethical friction: informed consent, disclosure of a diagnosis, refusal of treatment, religious objections, and questions about who has authority to decide. Nurses are often the first to notice the conflict and the ones who must respond in the moment — before an ethics consultant can arrive. Getting this right protects patients from harm and nurses from ethical and legal liability; getting it wrong can mean coercing consent, ignoring documented wishes, or alienating the family the patient relies on.

Exam questions in this area test whether you can name the principle at stake and apply it to a scenario — for example, recognizing that overriding a patient's refusal violates autonomy even when the nurse means well. In practice, the guiding documents are the nursing code of ethics (such as the ANA Code of Ethics in the United States), state law, and institutional policy; because these differ by setting, nurses must know their own facility's resources and reporting pathways.

## Core Concepts

### The core ethical principles

- **Autonomy:** respecting the patient's right to self-determination, including informed consent and refusal of care. Requires that the patient be given accurate information and have decision-making capacity.
- **Beneficence:** acting for the patient's good — the reason nurses intervene at all.
- **Nonmaleficence:** above all, do no harm; weigh risks of every action and inaction.
- **Justice:** fairness — treating similar cases similarly and ensuring that care and resources are not unfairly withheld from any group.
- **Fidelity and veracity:** keeping promises and telling the truth, which build the trust the therapeutic relationship depends on.

### Autonomy in a cultural context

Autonomy is usually pictured as one individual deciding for themselves, but many cultures practice **family-centered decision making**: the family, or a designated elder, expects to be consulted — and sometimes to decide. Respecting autonomy here does not mean ignoring the family; it means determining the patient's preference about *who should be involved* and honoring it. For informed consent, the nurse's role is to ensure the patient receives complete, understandable information (using an interpreter when needed), has capacity, and is free from coercion — then to support the decision-making process the patient prefers, within legal requirements. When the patient cannot decide, a legally recognized surrogate decision maker acts on their behalf; laws about who qualifies vary by jurisdiction.

### Veracity and truth-telling across cultures

In some cultural traditions, the family prefers that serious diagnoses be disclosed to them first, to "protect" the patient from distress, and direct disclosure to the patient is considered harmful. Nurses must balance honesty (veracity), respect for the patient's right to know (autonomy), and cultural sensitivity. The ethical path is not to assume the family's preference is the patient's preference, nor to assume the patient wants full disclosure regardless. The nurse gathers information about how the patient wants information handled — asking the patient directly when possible — and works with the care team to find a truthful approach the family can accept. Institutional policy and the ethics committee can help when a family insists on withholding information the patient has a legal right to receive.

### When cultural practices conflict with safety or the law

Most cultural practices — dietary rules, prayer, traditional remedies, healing rituals — can be accommodated safely. Some requests, however, conflict with treatment, safety rules, or law: a refusal of a life-sustaining treatment, a traditional remedy that may interact with prescribed medications, or a practice that violates a facility's infection-control standards. The nurse's duties are to respect what can be respected, provide complete information about risks, explore compromises and alternatives, document the discussion, and escalate — never to secretly substitute treatment, coerce consent, or silently allow an unsafe situation to continue. Some refusals are legally protected (for example, religiously based refusals of certain treatments within legal limits), so nurses must know the law in their jurisdiction and the facility's policies rather than relying on personal opinion.

### An ethical decision-making process

When principles collide, a structured approach keeps the reasoning transparent: (1) identify the ethical problem; (2) gather the facts — the patient's values, family input, medical facts, policy, and law; (3) name the principles in conflict; (4) consider options and their consequences; (5) choose a course and act; (6) evaluate the outcome. This mirrors the nursing process and gives the team a shared language for hard cases.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Ethics is like the rules for being a good helper: don't hurt people, tell the truth, keep your promises, and let people decide what happens to their own body. Sometimes the rules bump into each other — like when a family wants to keep bad news a secret but you think the patient should know. A good helper doesn't guess; they ask what the patient wants, explain things honestly, and get help from the "rule book" people (like an ethics committee) when it's really hard.

## High-Yield Points

- **Six principles to memorize:** autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice, fidelity, veracity — and be able to *apply* them to scenarios.
- **Autonomy ≠ always individual:** ask who the patient wants involved; family-centered decision making can be fully ethical when it reflects the patient's preference.
- **Informed consent needs:** complete information, capacity, freedom from coercion, and — when there is a language barrier — a professional interpreter, not a guessing game.
- **Truth-telling conflicts are not solved by choosing "culture" or "rules":** gather facts, involve the team, and escalate; never deceive the patient.
- **Respecting culture has limits:** accommodation, not acceptance of unsafe or illegal practices; document and escalate conflicts.
- **Know your resources:** ethics committee, charge nurse, institutional policy, and state law — they vary by setting and jurisdiction.
- **Exam trap:** an answer that respects the patient's stated preference is usually right; an answer that imposes the nurse's values (or the family's) without inquiry is usually wrong.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Autonomy | Respecting a person's right to make their own decisions | The basis of informed consent and refusal of care |
| Beneficence | Acting in the patient's best interest | The purpose behind nursing intervention |
| Nonmaleficence | Avoiding harm | The "do no harm" duty that limits interventions |
| Justice | Fairness in distributing benefits and burdens | Protects against discrimination in who gets care |
| Veracity | Truthfulness | Foundation of trust; often tested in disclosure conflicts |
| Fidelity | Keeping promises and commitments | Sustains the therapeutic relationship over time |
| Informed consent | Agreement to care given with full information, capacity, and no coercion | Legally and ethically required before procedures |
| Surrogate decision maker | A legally recognized person who decides for a patient who cannot | Who to involve when the patient lacks capacity |
| Ethical dilemma | A situation where valid principles conflict and no option is fully right | Calls for a structured decision process, not impulse |
| Moral distress | Knowing the right action but being unable to carry it out | Real and common in nursing; needs support and advocacy |

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process

1. **Identify the ethical problem** — name the conflict precisely (e.g., family requests nondisclosure).
2. **Gather facts:** the patient's values and stated preferences, family input, medical facts, facility policy, and applicable law.
3. **Name the principles in conflict** (e.g., veracity and autonomy versus nonmaleficence as the family sees it).
4. **Generate options and weigh consequences** — including compromises that honor culture and safety together.
5. **Act** — within scope, with documentation, and with escalation (charge nurse, ethics committee) when needed.
6. **Evaluate** the outcome and reflect on what could improve next time.

## Example

A patient with a serious new diagnosis is alert and able to decide. The adult children ask the nurse to keep the diagnosis from the patient, explaining that in their family, protecting the patient from bad news is the tradition. The nurse does not simply agree, and does not simply override the family. Instead, the nurse privately asks the patient, through an interpreter, how they want information handled: "Some people want to know everything; some prefer a family member to hear details first. What would help you?" The patient chooses to have the eldest child present during disclosure. The team then plans a family meeting where the diagnosis is shared honestly, with the patient's chosen support present. *(Illustrative scenario — how disclosure is handled varies by facility policy and jurisdiction.)*

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Ethics | Law | Ethics guides right conduct; law sets minimum enforceable rules. Something can be legal but ethically questionable (and vice versa) |
| Autonomy | Paternalism | Autonomy lets the patient decide with full information; paternalism decides for the patient "for their own good" |
| Beneficence | Nonmaleficence | Beneficence means doing good; nonmaleficence means avoiding harm — both must be weighed in every action |
| Respecting a cultural practice | Accepting any practice | Respect = accommodate, inform, negotiate, escalate; it does not require accepting unsafe or illegal acts |
| Ethical dilemma | Moral distress | A dilemma is a genuine conflict of principles; moral distress is knowing the right action but being blocked from doing it |
| Family preference | Patient preference | The family's view is important but is not automatically the patient's; the patient's stated preference governs |
