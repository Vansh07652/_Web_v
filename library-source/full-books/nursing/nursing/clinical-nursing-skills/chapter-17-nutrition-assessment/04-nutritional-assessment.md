# Nutritional Assessment

> **Book:** [Clinical Nursing Skills](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 17: Nutrition Assessment](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 4  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Nutritional assessment is the systematic process of gathering and interpreting information about a person's nutritional status. It answers three practical questions: Is this person getting enough fuel and building blocks? Is the body able to use what is taken in? And is there evidence that nutrition is already failing — or at risk of failing — in a way that affects health and recovery?

It is not a single test or number but a picture assembled from several data streams: what the person eats, body measurements, laboratory information, physical findings, and the person's own story about appetite, access to food, and daily life. Nurses gather much of this data because they see patients eat, weigh them, observe their skin and energy, and talk with them across multiple shifts. In this chapter, nutritional assessment is the step that turns the earlier topics — what nutrients do, what influences intake, what therapeutic diets look like — into a documented picture of one person.

## Why This Matters

Malnutrition is common in hospitalized people and often goes unrecognized. Poor nutrition is linked to slower wound healing, weaker immune defenses, muscle loss, more complications, and longer stays; a person who enters the hospital undernourished can worsen quickly. Because nurses see patients daily, they are often first to notice the early clues: a tray returned untouched, clothes fitting more loosely, fatigue out of proportion to activity. Recognizing those clues, collecting the right data, and communicating it to the dietitian and provider can change the course of care. On exams this topic tests the ability to distinguish screening from assessment, read a weight trend, and sort data into the right category.

## Core Concepts

### Screening comes first

Screening is a quick, standardized way to identify people *at risk* so they can get a deeper look. It is usually done on admission and repeated during a stay, using a few simple questions and measures — recent weight change, recent intake, illness or stress — to flag risk. A positive screen does not diagnose malnutrition; it triggers referral for a comprehensive assessment. Institutions select validated tools (for example, the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool or the Mini Nutritional Assessment, depending on setting and population). *Exact tools and referral thresholds vary by institution and guideline — verify against your facility's policy and current references.*

### The five data streams

A comprehensive assessment combines five types of information:

1. **Dietary history.** What the person actually eats: a 24-hour recall, food frequency questionnaire, food diary, or usual-pattern diet history. Recall depends on memory — people forget snacks and under-report portions — so treat it as a starting point, not a precise record.
2. **Anthropometric measures.** Height, weight, body mass index (BMI, a weight-for-height index), and sometimes arm circumference or skinfold thickness as estimates of muscle and fat stores. Weight measured consistently over time is the most useful single measure.
3. **Biochemical data.** Laboratory values that can support the picture, such as markers of protein status. Labs are influenced by inflammation, fluid shifts, and illness, so they are supporting evidence, never proof — always pair them with physical and dietary findings and current reference ranges. *Specific markers and interpretation should be checked against current references before drawing conclusions.*
4. **Physical examination.** Observation of skin, hair, nails, mucous membranes, and mouth for findings that can accompany deficiencies (for example, dry brittle hair, changes in the tongue or mouth, poor skin integrity). Most such signs are nonspecific — they raise questions rather than answer them.
5. **Client history.** Medical diagnoses, surgeries, gastrointestinal symptoms, medications that affect appetite or absorption, chewing and swallowing problems, dental health, and the ability to shop, cook, and feed oneself. Social and cultural context matters too: finances, food access, living situation, mental health, and religious or cultural food practices all shape what a person can and will eat.

### Weight: the story told in numbers

Weight change is the most informative single trend in nutritional assessment. Weigh consistently — the same scale, similar clothing and time of day — and compare readings over time, because a single reading means little. Unintentional weight loss is a red flag: it can signal inadequate intake, impaired absorption, or the body burning its own stores. Two cautions: sudden weight changes are often fluid shifts (edema, diuresis, IV fluids), not tissue loss; and the percent-weight-loss thresholds used to grade severity vary by guideline — *check current references rather than memorizing one cutoff.*

### From data to a picture — and who does what

Assessment is synthesis: compare intake with needs, current weight with baseline, and physical signs with history. Separate *observations* ("weight 4 kg lower than 3 months ago," "ate less than half of lunch") from *interpretations* ("at risk for malnutrition related to decreased intake"), and document objectively. Nurses screen, gather data, observe intake, weigh, document, and flag concerns — but comprehensive assessment, diagnosis of malnutrition, and prescription of therapeutic diets are typically the role of the registered dietitian nutritionist (RDN). Nurses implement diet orders, monitor tolerance, and educate the person and family. Exact role boundaries vary by state and institution; follow facility policies and scope-of-practice guidance.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

> Checking someone's nutrition is like checking a car before a long trip: how much fuel went in (what the person eats), how full the tank is (weight and measurements), what the gauges say (lab work), and whether the engine runs smoothly (how the person looks, feels, and functions). One clue alone isn't enough — you put all the clues together to decide if the car is ready for the trip.

## High-Yield Points

- **Screening ≠ assessment.** Screening flags risk; assessment builds the full picture. A positive screen triggers referral, not a diagnosis.
- **Weight trends beat single readings** — consistent technique makes the trend trustworthy; sudden shifts are often fluid, not tissue.
- **Five data streams:** dietary history, anthropometrics, biochemical data, physical findings, client history.
- **Lab values are supporting data**, distorted by inflammation and fluid status — never proof alone.
- **Unintentional weight loss is a red flag** warranting prompt communication and investigation.
- **Recall data is imperfect** — people forget, under-report, and misestimate portions.
- **Scope note:** nurses screen, gather, document, and flag; the RDN performs the comprehensive assessment and prescribes therapeutic diets (varies by state/institution).
- **Document observations separately from interpretations**, in person-first, objective language.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Nutritional screening | A quick, standardized check to identify people at risk of malnutrition | Decides who needs a full assessment; fast and easy to repeat |
| Comprehensive nutritional assessment | An in-depth evaluation using multiple data streams | Produces the detailed picture that guides nutrition care |
| Anthropometry | Body measurements: height, weight, BMI, arm circumference | Quantifies body size and muscle/fat stores; weight trend is key |
| BMI | A weight-for-height calculation indicating general body size | Useful for populations, limited in individuals with high muscle mass or fluid shifts |
| 24-hour recall | Asking the person to describe everything eaten and drunk in the past day | Quick intake snapshot; limited by memory and under-reporting |
| Unintentional weight loss | Weight loss that happens without trying | One of the strongest warning signs in nutritional assessment |
| Registered dietitian nutritionist (RDN) | The professional who performs comprehensive assessment and prescribes therapeutic diets | Defines the scope boundary: nurses gather and flag, RDN diagnoses and prescribes |

## Example

**The tray that keeps coming back.** Mr. Okafor, 72, is admitted with pneumonia. Over three days he eats less than a quarter of each meal. His admission screen flagged risk, and his chart shows he weighed 4 kg less three months ago at a clinic visit; today he is down another 0.5 kg. During morning care he says his food "doesn't taste right," his dentures no longer fit, he lives alone, and he usually eats "tea and toast" because cooking for one feels like too much effort.

**Your nursing actions:** weigh him consistently and document the trend; observe and record intake at each meal; note the denture, taste, and social history; and communicate the weight trend, intake pattern, and risk factors to the provider and RDN. You do *not* diagnose malnutrition — that is the RDN's comprehensive assessment, which your screening data and observations now justify.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Screening | Comprehensive assessment | Screening is a quick risk flag; assessment is in-depth. A positive screen triggers referral, not a diagnosis |
| BMI as individual verdict | BMI as a general indicator | BMI ignores muscle mass, fluid, age, and body composition; one clue among many |
| Sudden weight change | True tissue loss | Overnight swings are usually fluid shifts; tissue loss shows up in trends over weeks |
| "Eating enough" | "Getting enough" | Chewing, swallowing, malabsorption, and medications can prevent the body from using what is eaten |
| A single lab value | Proof of malnutrition | Labs are distorted by inflammation and hydration; they support, never replace, the full picture |
| One weight reading | A weight trend | The direction and rate of change carry the information, not any single number |
