# Principles of Nursing Practice

> **Book:** [Clinical Nursing Skills](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 1: The Role of the Nurse in Comprehensive Care](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 1  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Principles of nursing practice are the foundational commitments and standards that shape how nurses think, decide, and act in every encounter with a patient, family, or community. They are not a list of tasks to check off; they are a professional value system that sits underneath every skill in this book — from taking a blood pressure to teaching a person about a new medication. The principles include respect for human dignity, individualized care, patient safety, evidence-informed decision making, advocacy, accountability, and effective communication.

These principles come from several sources: state nurse practice acts (the laws that define what a nurse may legally do), professional codes such as the American Nurses Association (ANA) Code of Ethics, standards of practice published by professional organizations, and institutional policies. Because laws and policies vary by state, country, and facility, the specific way a principle is carried out changes with the setting — but the underlying commitments are shared across nursing.

## Why This Matters

- Principles turn "good intentions" into consistent, defensible practice. A nurse who understands why privacy matters is more likely to knock before entering, pull the curtain, and protect health information even when rushed.
- Nearly every clinical skill in this book is an application of one or more principles. If you understand the principle, the skill makes sense; if you only memorize the steps, one unexpected situation leaves you without a plan.
- Patient safety depends on principle-driven behavior: reporting errors and near misses, double-checking, honest documentation, and speaking up when something looks wrong.
- Legal accountability: practicing within scope and documenting accurately are both professional principles and legal duties.
- Exam relevance: licensure-style questions rarely ask "define dignity" — they present a situation and ask which action best respects the patient. That is principle application, and it is how this material is most often tested.

## Core Concepts

### Respect for Human Dignity and Worth

Every person has worth regardless of diagnosis, background, ability, or lifestyle. In practice this means using person-first language ("a person with diabetes," not "a diabetic"), protecting privacy and confidentiality, addressing the person by their preferred name, and providing care without judgment. Respect also includes honoring the person's cultural and spiritual practices when compatible with safe care, and asking rather than assuming what matters to them.

### Individualized, Person-Centered Care

Standardized protocols and clinical pathways are starting points, not finished plans. Each person brings unique preferences, values, family structures, resources, and goals. Individualized care means tailoring the approach — timing, teaching style, who is included in discussions — to the person while staying within safe, evidence-informed practice. Patient-centered care is examined in depth in Chapter 2 of this book.

### Safety as a Non-Negotiable Principle

The principle of doing no harm (nonmaleficence) makes safety an organizing value of nursing. It shows up as identifying patients correctly, performing safety checks, using technologies such as barcode scanning where available, reporting errors and near misses without blame, questioning unclear orders, and recognizing when a situation exceeds your own ability. A safety culture treats errors as system problems to fix, not only individual failures to punish.

### Autonomy, Advocacy, and Informed Participation

Patients have the right to make informed decisions about their own care, including the right to accept or refuse treatments. The nurse's role is to make sure the person has understandable information, to support their choices, and to advocate when the patient's wishes are not being heard. Advocacy means speaking for or alongside the patient — not deciding for them. Nurses often assist with the informed consent process, but who may obtain consent is defined by law and facility policy, so nurses must know their local rules.

### Accountability and Scope of Practice

Accountability means owning your actions, your omissions, and their consequences — including your documentation, because the health record is the evidence of care. Scope of practice is the legal boundary of what a nurse may do, set by the state nurse practice act and refined by facility policy. Delegation — assigning a task to a licensed practical nurse or unlicensed assistive personnel — is part of the registered nurse role, but the RN remains accountable for the outcome and must confirm that the person is competent for the task. What can be delegated varies by state and setting; when in doubt, the nurse checks policy and asks.

### Communication and the Therapeutic Relationship

Nursing care is delivered through relationships. Effective communication includes active listening, plain language, verifying understanding (for example, with teach-back), and structured handoffs such as SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) to prevent information loss between shifts and between teams. Communication is also how principles become visible: a calm, respectful tone is dignity in action.

### Ethical Foundations

Four classic ethical principles anchor nursing decisions: beneficence (acting for the person's good), nonmaleficence (avoiding harm), autonomy (respecting the person's right to self-determination), and justice (fair distribution of care and resources), along with veracity (truthfulness) and fidelity (keeping promises). When values conflict — for example, a person refuses a treatment the team believes is beneficial — nurses use ethical reasoning, consult colleagues, and may involve the facility's ethics committee. There is often no perfect answer; the goal is a defensible, transparent decision.

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Nursing principles are the rules of the game for how nurses treat people. They say: be kind and respectful, keep people safe, tell the truth, help people make their own choices, and own up to what you do. When a nurse is busy or stressed, these rules are the guide that keeps care fair and safe for everyone.

## High-Yield Points

- Principles are the "why" behind every skill: dignity, safety, advocacy, accountability, communication, and ethical action.
- Person-first language and privacy/confidentiality are concrete, testable expressions of respect.
- Safety is a principle, not a task — report errors and near misses; question unclear orders.
- Advocacy supports the patient's informed choices; it is not deciding for the patient.
- Scope of practice comes from state law and facility policy; delegation does not transfer accountability.
- The ANA Code of Ethics and the state nurse practice act are key professional and legal anchors.
- Documentation is accountability — if it was not recorded, from a legal standpoint it was not done.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Nurse practice act | State law that defines legal nursing practice and scope in that jurisdiction | Determines what a nurse may legally do; varies by state |
| Scope of practice | The boundary of activities a nurse is licensed and trained to perform | Practicing beyond scope is unsafe and illegal |
| Advocacy | Speaking for or alongside the patient to protect their rights and wishes | Ensures the patient's voice is heard, especially when vulnerable |
| Autonomy | The patient's right to make informed decisions about their own care | Shapes consent, refusal, and shared decision-making |
| Beneficence | Acting for the patient's good | Explains why we intervene at all |
| Nonmaleficence | Avoiding or minimizing harm ("do no harm") | The foundation of patient safety |
| Delegation | Assigning a task to another care team member within legal limits | The RN remains accountable for the outcome |
| SBAR | Structured handoff: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation | Reduces communication errors between caregivers |

## Example: Applying Principles During an Admission

Nurse Chen is admitting Mr. Okonkwo, an older adult who uses a walker and asks to be called "Sam." First, Nurse Chen introduces herself and explains what will happen during the admission (communication and autonomy). She closes the curtain and asks whether his family may stay during the interview (privacy and preferences). When the provider later discusses a procedure, Sam says he wants to think it over. Instead of pressuring him, Nurse Chen gives a simple explanation of the procedure's purpose, checks his understanding with teach-back, and documents that he is considering his options (informed participation and documentation). Later she notices a spill near the doorway and cleans it immediately (safety). At shift change she gives the oncoming nurse a structured SBAR report (communication and continuity). In one ordinary admission, every principle shows up in action — which is the point: principles are practiced, not just memorized.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Advocacy | Making decisions for the patient | Advocacy supports and amplifies the patient's own informed choice |
| Scope of practice | Institutional policy | Law sets the outer boundary; facility policy may be narrower |
| Delegation | Abandonment of responsibility | The RN stays accountable and must verify competency |
| Respecting refusal | Disobeying the provider | A competent patient may refuse care; the nurse educates, documents, and reports |
| Principles | Procedures or steps | Procedures answer "how"; principles answer "why" and apply when the steps don't |
