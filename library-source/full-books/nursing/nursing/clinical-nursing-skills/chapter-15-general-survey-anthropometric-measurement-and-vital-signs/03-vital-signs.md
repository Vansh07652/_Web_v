# Vital Signs

> **Book:** [Clinical Nursing Skills](../../README.md)  
> **Chapter:** [Chapter 15: General Survey, Anthropometric Measurement, and Vital Signs](../README.md)  
> **Topic:** 3  
> **Source structure:** OpenStax public textbook outline

## Overview

Vital signs — sometimes called **cardinal signs** — are the measurements that reflect the body's most basic life functions: **temperature, pulse, respirations, and blood pressure**. Pain is widely taught as the "fifth vital sign" (a practice that has been debated and refined in recent years), and oxygen saturation (SpO₂) is routinely measured alongside the classic four in most clinical settings.

They are called "vital" for a reason: they are the fastest, cheapest window into whether the body's core systems are working, and changes in them are often the earliest detectable sign that a patient is getting worse. This topic provides the big picture — what the vital signs are, when to take them, and how to interpret them; later topics cover temperature, heart rate, respiration, and blood pressure in depth.

## Why This Matters

- **Early warning.** Abnormal vital signs often precede serious deterioration, which is why facilities build structured early-warning systems around them.
- **Baseline and trend.** A single reading is nearly meaningless alone; the value comes from comparing it with the patient's baseline and following the trend over time.
- **Evaluating treatment.** Whether an intervention worked — fever reduced, pain improved — is judged by reassessing the vital signs.
- **Medication and procedure safety.** Orders and policies commonly require vital signs before, during, or after certain medications and procedures.
- **Communication.** Vital signs anchor shift report and handoffs; abnormal values trigger escalation through structured tools (for example, SBAR-style communication).
- **Scope note.** Measuring vital signs is often delegated to unlicensed assistive personnel; interpreting them and deciding the response is the nurse's responsibility. Scope and institutional policy vary.

## Core Concepts

### The Vital Signs and What Each Reflects

- **Temperature:** the balance between heat produced and heat lost; reflects metabolism and the inflammatory response (covered in the Temperature topic).
- **Pulse / heart rate:** the number of heartbeats per minute; reflects cardiac function, perfusion, and autonomic state (covered in the Heart Rate topic).
- **Respirations:** breaths per minute; reflects ventilation and gas exchange (covered in the Respiration topic).
- **Blood pressure:** the force of blood against the arterial walls; reflects cardiac output and vascular resistance (covered in the Blood Pressure topic).
- **Pain** (often called the fifth vital sign): subjective, assessed with patient self-report scales rather than measured directly.
- **Oxygen saturation (SpO₂):** an estimate of blood oxygen level from pulse oximetry; commonly measured, but not one of the classic four.

### When to Measure Vital Signs

- On admission or first contact, to establish a baseline.
- Per provider orders or facility policy: routine frequencies, before and after certain procedures, and before/after medications that affect vital signs.
- When the patient's condition changes or the patient reports new symptoms.
- After interventions, to evaluate the response.

Frequency is driven by orders and policy, not nurse discretion alone — institutional variation applies, and the nurse follows the facility's parameters and escalation criteria.

### Obtaining Accurate Measurements

Accuracy depends on three things: technique, equipment, and patient preparation.

- **Technique:** correct cuff size and placement for blood pressure, correct thermometer route for temperature, correct counting time for pulse and respirations.
- **Equipment:** calibrated, facility-approved devices; the right size cuff and the right thermometer probe.
- **Patient preparation:** allow rest after activity; note recent exercise, caffeine, smoking, or anxiety; avoid hot or cold drinks before an oral temperature; position the patient comfortably.

A single abnormal reading deserves a repeat and context before any action — but a pattern of readings (a trend) is more reliable than any one number.

### Baseline, Ranges, and Individual Variation

Textbook reference ranges — for example, an adult resting heart rate of roughly 60–100 beats per minute, respirations of roughly 12–20 per minute, and an average oral temperature around 37 °C (98.6 °F) — are teaching approximations. They vary with age, fitness, and population: infants and children have faster heart and respiratory rates than adults, and athletes often have slower resting heart rates. Every facility has its own parameters and escalation criteria. Follow your program's and institution's standards, and flag any conflicting figures for source review.

Most importantly, "normal for this patient" can differ from the textbook. A resting heart rate of 88 might be unremarkable for one person and a significant change for another whose baseline is 60.

### Documentation and Communication

Document vital signs promptly, including the route or method where it matters (for example, "38.2 °C oral"), the time, and any relevant patient state (activity, position, pain). Communicate abnormal findings through handoff tools such as SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) and the facility's escalation process. Know who to notify and when — that is defined by policy, not improvised.

### Factors That Affect Vital Signs

Age, sex, circadian rhythm, exercise, pain, anxiety and stress, medications, caffeine and nicotine, fever, blood loss, and dehydration all shift vital signs. Recognizing these factors keeps you from misreading a normal response (a fast pulse in an anxious patient) as disease — and from dismissing a genuine change as "just anxiety."

## ELI-10: Explain Like I'm 10

Vital signs are the body's "check engine" gauges: temperature, heartbeat, breathing, and blood pressure. Nurses read these gauges at visits and write the numbers down, then compare them with earlier readings — like checking whether a car's temperature gauge is higher than it was yesterday. If a gauge changes suddenly, the nurse investigates and tells the rest of the team.

## High-Yield Points

- The four classic vital signs are temperature, pulse, respirations, and blood pressure; pain is often called the fifth, and SpO₂ is commonly added.
- Vital signs are interpreted as trends against a baseline, not as isolated numbers.
- Accuracy depends on technique, equipment (including cuff size), and patient preparation.
- Measurement frequency is driven by orders and facility policy; interpretation and response are nursing responsibilities.
- Document the route or method with the value (for example, "T 38.2 °C oral").
- Textbook ranges vary by age and population — follow institutional parameters.
- Report abnormal findings promptly with structured communication and the facility's escalation criteria.

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain-Language Definition | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Vital signs (cardinal signs) | Measurements of basic life functions: temperature, pulse, respirations, blood pressure. | They are the fastest window into whether core body systems are working. |
| Temperature | The balance between heat produced and heat lost by the body. | A key indicator of infection, inflammation, and thermoregulation problems. |
| Pulse | The palpable wave of blood felt in an artery with each heartbeat. | It provides heart rate, rhythm, and perfusion information at the bedside. |
| Respirations | The number of breaths per minute. | Reflects ventilation; often an early indicator of deterioration in commonly taught frameworks. |
| Blood pressure | The force of blood against arterial walls. | Reflects cardiac output and vascular resistance; a core perfusion measure. |
| SpO₂ | Oxygen saturation estimated by pulse oximetry. | A routine, noninvasive oxygen-level estimate with known limitations. |
| Baseline / trend | The patient's usual values, and the pattern of values over time. | All later readings are interpreted against the baseline and the trend. |
| SBAR | Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation — a structured handoff format. | It keeps vital-sign communication organized and complete. |

## How It Works / Step-by-Step Process

1. **Verify the plan:** check orders and facility policy for frequency and any special instructions (for example, before a medication).
2. **Prepare:** explain to the patient; position comfortably; allow rest if the patient was active; gather calibrated equipment.
3. **Measure efficiently:** a common order is temperature, then pulse, then respirations — with respirations counted while fingers are still on the pulse site so the patient does not consciously alter breathing.
4. **Record:** document each value with route/method, time, and relevant patient state.
5. **Interpret and act:** compare with baseline and trend; repeat questionable readings; escalate abnormal findings per facility policy.

## Example

**Postoperative scenario.** A patient returns from surgery; the nurse takes vital signs two hours later: temperature 38.0 °C oral, heart rate 96, respirations 18, blood pressure stable, pain rated 6/10. The heart rate alone (96) looks unremarkable — until the nurse checks the chart: the patient's pre-op resting heart rate was 72, and the temperature was 36.8 °C. The **trend** tells a different story than either single reading.

The nurse considers the context: recent surgery, pain rated 6/10, a rising temperature, and a heart rate climbing above baseline. The response is not a diagnosis — it is assessment, comfort measures, documentation of the trend, and notification of the provider per facility criteria. The vital signs did their job: they turned a subtle change into a question the team could act on — "the numbers" are never the whole story.

## Common Confusions

| Do Not Confuse | With | The Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Vital signs as numbers | Vital signs as information | The numbers only mean something in context: baseline, trend, symptoms, and time since intervention. |
| "Fifth vital sign" as a universal practice | Facility-specific pain assessment | The term is debated and some settings have moved away from it — follow your facility's practice. |
| SpO₂ | A direct blood oxygen measurement | SpO₂ is an oximetry estimate with limitations (for example, poor perfusion can affect readings). |
| One abnormal reading | An abnormal trend | Repeat the reading and look at the pattern before concluding anything. |
| Adult reference ranges | Pediatric ranges | Infants and children normally have faster heart and respiratory rates than adults. |
